## Abstract

How a phone is a client of this stack. It is a SIP-22 device of an account
whose key lives somewhere else, paired by showing its key once; it is
admitted to an exchange by that registration rather than by a second entry
on a whitelist; it registers a SIP-45 endpoint each time it connects; and
when woken it does a bounded amount of work in a fixed order -- subscribe,
fetch, persist, then and only then compose a notification from plaintext it
alone opened -- and goes back to sleep. Nothing the phone shows on its lock
screen has passed through anybody's push service, because the push service
was handed four bytes. Two rules on the exchange make this work: a
registered device inherits its account's transport admission, and a device
that held a stream and let it go is not held to a wake it has already
answered.

## Motivation

SIP-45 gave an exchange a way to wake a device it cannot hold a stream to,
and stopped there: "no phone client exists yet; this is the half of the
problem the exchange can solve ahead of one." This is the other half. A
desktop client is a process that runs; a phone client is a process that is
*allowed to run*, for seconds at a time, when the platform decides -- and
what it may do in those seconds, in what order, is the whole design. Get
the order wrong and the phone shows a notification for a message it then
loses, or wakes a hundred times for a channel it is in and shows nothing,
or spends a one-time prekey it has not written down.

Three things stood in the way that SIP-45 did not cover. **Getting the phone
in the door**: SIP-8's whitelist admits keys, a phone has a new one, and
the one route that could enrol it is behind the door. **Getting it its
account**: SIP-22 lets a device be registered by a sibling and SIP-42 lets a
sibling hand it history, but neither says how a phone with no exchange and
no account finds either. **Staying woken for the right things**: SIP-45's
coalescing measures from the last wake, and a device that answered a wake,
held a stream for ten seconds and left is, to the exchange, a device woken
ten seconds ago -- silent for the next twenty whatever arrives.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. "The phone" below is any client that
cannot hold SIP-30's stream while it is not in the foreground; nothing here
is specific to a handset.

### The phone is a device

The phone generates an Ed25519 **device key** on first run, keeps its secret
in the platform's key store, and never has the account's key. It acts under
a SIP-20 credential for scope `sqex-chat`, registered at each exchange the
account uses (SIP-22). Its transport identity (SIP-3) is the device key.

**A registered device is admitted.** An exchange that whitelists transport
keys (SIP-8) MUST admit the transport key of every device currently
registered (SIP-22) to an account the whitelist admits, for as long as the
registration stands, and MUST stop admitting it when the device is revoked
or its credential expires. The device is admitted *because of* the account,
not listed beside it: `whitelist list` does not show it, and removing the
account removes its devices with it. This is what lets a phone be enrolled
from the desktop without an operator, and what makes SIP-22's revoke the
only step in losing a phone.

### Pairing

Pairing puts two facts where they are needed: the phone's key on a device
that can sign for the account, and the account and its exchanges on the
phone. Each is one string, shown as text and as a QR code, so it can be
scanned or typed.

1. The phone shows its device key: `sqx-device:` followed by the key in
   base58. It knows nothing else yet and shows nothing else.
2. A client holding the account's key -- or a device already registered to
   it -- reads the key, issues a SIP-20 credential naming it for scope
   `sqex-chat` with a `not_after` of the person's choosing, and registers
   it at every exchange the account uses. SIP-22 lets a *registered*
   sibling register the next, and an account that has never registered
   anything is nobody's sibling: a client on the account key registers
   itself first. It then shows the phone where to go: `sqx-pair:` followed by
   the account key in base58, `@`, and the exchange domains separated by
   `,` -- `sqx-pair:6xhq…@squic.org,trunk.exchange`. A person MAY type a
   SIP-38 name (`colin@squic.org`) instead, and the phone resolves it to
   the same account and starts from that one domain; the others follow
   from SIP-46 and from the channels' homes (SIP-43).
3. The phone reaches each domain by SIP-33, connects as its device key, and
   asks `/device/list` for the account. It MUST find itself there, with
   the credential the registering client presented (SIP-32), before it
   treats the account as its own; it holds no other proof. It keeps the
   credential: SIP-42's `Hello` needs it.
4. Both sides open a SIP-42 session toward each other and the phone takes
   the history it wants. The registering client SHOULD keep an `open`
   outstanding toward the new device until one session has completed.

Nothing in pairing goes to the exchange that SIP-22 and SIP-42 did not
already send it, and the two strings carry no secret: a device key is
public, and a `sqx-pair:` string names an account anybody could look up.
The registration is the act of trust, and it is signed by the account.

### Connecting

Every time the phone connects it does four things before anything else,
in this order:

1. Subscribes (SIP-30). While the stream is open the exchange will not wake
   it (SIP-45), which is what stops a phone in the foreground being pushed
   for what it is already watching.
2. Registers its SIP-45 endpoint, with `ttl` at least as long as it expects
   to be away and at most 30 days. Re-registering is cheap and idempotent;
   a phone that forgot to would go quiet at the end of the last `ttl`,
   silently.
3. Tops up its SIP-23 prekeys to `POOL` if below `LOW_WATER`. A phone is the
   device most likely to be sealed to while absent and least able to
   refill on demand: a pool that runs dry falls back to the fallback key,
   which is exactly the forward-secrecy loss SIP-23 exists to avoid.
4. Reconciles, as SIP-30 says a fresh subscription must.

A phone holds the stream for as long as the platform lets it and closes it
cleanly (ending the request) when it is about to be suspended, so the
exchange counts it absent from that moment rather than from the transport's
idle timeout.

### The wake window

Woken (SIP-45), the phone has a budget it does not choose -- tens of
seconds on the platforms that exist -- and MUST do the following in this
order, stopping cleanly wherever the budget runs out:

1. Connect, subscribe, register the endpoint, top up prekeys: the
   *Connecting* sequence.
2. Fetch what is new in every channel the reconcile found had moved, newest
   channel first; open what it can (SIP-17 envelopes, SIP-16 entries);
   **write it to the store**.
3. Only then compose notifications, from the store.
4. Close the stream and stop.

The order of 2 and 3 is the rule this section exists for. Opening a SIP-17
envelope sealed to a one-time prekey spends the prekey; a phone that
composed the notification first and was suspended before writing would
hold a notification for a message nothing can open again. The store is
written before anything is shown, and a window that ends between the two
loses a notification, which the next window shows, rather than a message.

A wake that finds nothing new -- the desktop got there first, or the event
was for a device that was already awake -- composes nothing and says
nothing. A wake is not a message, and a phone MUST NOT show "you have a new
message" on the strength of one.

**The wake, released.** An exchange MUST measure SIP-45's coalescing
interval from the later of the device's last wake and the close of the
device's last stream. A device that was woken, connected and left has
answered that wake, and the next event after it leaves is the first of a
new absence, to be woken for at once. (Without this a phone that answered
in ten seconds is deaf for twenty, and the more promptly it answers the
more it misses.)

### Notifications

A notification is composed on the phone from plaintext the phone opened,
and MUST NOT be sent to the exchange, the distributor, or anything else
off the device; the platform's own notification surface is the only
consumer. What it contains is the person's setting, and the phone MUST
offer at least: sender and text; sender only; the fact of a message. The
default is the phone's to choose and this document does not.

A `Ringing` wake (SIP-36) is the one where composition follows the entry
rather than the setting: the phone fetches the invitation, and if
`now - entry time < ring_secs` presents an incoming call through the
platform's call surface, joining the SIP-13 room on answer exactly as a
desktop would. Past `ring_secs` it is a missed call and is shown as one.
SIP-39's `CrossCall` is the same with the bridge in place of the room.

A phone SHOULD show at most one notification per channel per window,
naming the newest, with a count, rather than one per entry: a busy channel
is one thing that happened.

### What the phone keeps

The phone is a partial holder. It keeps a bounded history -- by age, size,
or both, at the person's setting -- and its SIP-42 `Have` says exactly what
it holds, no more; nothing there requires completeness, and a sibling that
wants what the phone has dropped goes to the exchange or another sibling.
What it keeps it keeps sealed at rest under a key in the platform's key
store, and its device secret never leaves that store.

A phone that is lost is revoked (SIP-22) from any other device of the
account, or by the account itself. A phone registered after the desktop
cannot revoke the desktop (SIP-22's seniority rule), which is the right way
round for the device most likely to be in a stranger's hand.

### Several exchanges

One device key serves every exchange the account uses; the phone registers
at each and holds one endpoint at each. The same endpoint MAY be given to
all of them: the distributor already knows the device, and learns from a
second exchange's wakes only that there is a second exchange. A phone that
would rather not tell one distributor how many exchanges it uses registers
a distinct endpoint at each, which UnifiedPush distributors allow.

### The distributor

On platforms with UnifiedPush the phone uses whatever distributor the
person installed, and the exchange's side is SIP-45 exactly. On a platform
whose only way to run a suspended application is its vendor's push service,
the distributor is a bridge that holds the vendor credentials -- run by
whoever publishes the application, or by the person -- and the phone's
endpoint is an address at that bridge. The bridge learns what SIP-45 says
a distributor learns, *when*, per device token; it MUST forward nothing but
a wake and MUST be sent nothing but one, and its message to the vendor's
service MUST be one that gives the application run time on every delivery,
with no content of its own beyond a fixed placeholder the application
replaces. A bridge that batched, delayed, or dropped wakes to fit a vendor's
budget for silent pushes would turn a message into a maybe; it is the
bridge's job to choose the delivery kind that is not budgeted that way.

### Limits

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| Endpoint `ttl` the phone registers | at most 30 days; at least the platform's suspension horizon |
| Prekey top-up | to `POOL` (64) when below `LOW_WATER` (16), every connect |
| Notifications per channel per window | 1 |
| Pairing strings | `sqx-device:<base58>`, `sqx-pair:<base58>@<domain>[,<domain>]*`; at most 512 bytes |

## Rationale

**Why the phone never holds the account key.** Because it is the device
most likely to be lost, and SIP-22 already draws the line in the right
place: a device is revoked, an account is succeeded (SIP-44). A phone with
the account key would make losing the phone a succession.

**Why admission follows registration.** The alternative is an operator
adding every phone by hand, which on a whitelisted exchange means every
person mailing an admin a key each time they buy a phone. The account was
admitted; the account signed the credential; the credential is on record
at the exchange; there is nothing further to decide. The rule is scoped to
registered devices of admitted accounts and lasts exactly as long as the
registration, so it adds nothing the whitelist did not already imply.

**Why two strings and not one.** One string in one direction cannot do it:
the registering client needs the phone's key before it can sign, and the
phone needs the account and the domains before it can go anywhere. A
single round trip through the exchange -- the phone sends its key somewhere
and waits -- needs the phone admitted first, which is the thing being
arranged. Two strings, each public, each typeable, is the whole of it.

**Why persist before notify.** Stated in the section; the point of putting
it in a table of rules rather than a paragraph of advice is that the
failure is silent and the platform induces it: a suspended process looks,
from inside, exactly like one that finished.

**Why release the wake when the stream closes.** SIP-45's coalescing was
written for a device that stays away; the phone is the device that comes
and goes in seconds. Measuring from the last wake punishes the prompt.
Measuring from whichever came later -- the wake, or the end of the visit
that answered it -- keeps the interval for the device that never came and
lifts it for the one that did.

**Why no wire format for the notification.** Because there is nowhere for
it to go. The moment a notification's contents are specified as bytes on a
wire, some wire will carry them, and the only wire between a sleeping phone
and the world is the one this design keeps empty.

## Compatibility

Additive on the client side. On the exchange: two behaviours, neither on
the wire. An exchange that does not admit registered devices leaves a
phone unable to reach a whitelisted exchange until an operator lists it;
one that does not release the wake on stream close leaves a phone
coalesced for up to 30 seconds after each visit. Both degrade to SIP-45
and SIP-8 as written, and a phone against either keeps working. Pairing
strings are new and are refused by nothing, since they are read by people
and phones and never sent to an exchange.

## Security considerations

**The phone is a full member of every channel the account is in.** It
holds the epoch keys, opens every message, and can be lost. SIP-22's revoke
ends its future; nothing ends its past, and this document adds nothing to
SIP-17's rotation, which is the remedy. A person who loses a phone revokes
it and rotates the channels that matter.

**Admission by registration widens the whitelist by exactly the set of
registered devices.** An account that could register arbitrary devices --
which any account can, being the signer -- could admit arbitrary keys. It
could already admit arbitrary *readers* by inviting them to a channel; what
it gains here is transport admission for keys it vouched for, which is what
a whitelist entry for the account meant. An operator who wants per-device
control has SIP-22's `list` to see and its `revoke` to act, from the
account's side, and can remove the account.

**Pairing strings are not secrets, and a wrong one is not an attack.** A
device key shown to the wrong client gets a credential from the wrong
account, which the phone detects at step 3 -- it is not in *its* account's
list -- and refuses. A `sqx-pair:` string shown to the wrong phone tells it
an account and a domain, both public, and that phone is in no list. What
pairing does not defend against is a person deliberately registering a
device they do not hold, which is SIP-22's grant working as designed.

**The notification surface is the platform's.** What the phone shows on
its lock screen, the platform stores, syncs, and sometimes reads; a
setting of "the fact of a message" is the only one that keeps the
platform as ignorant as the distributor, and the phone MUST offer it.

**The bridge is a party the exchange did not choose and the person may
not have.** On a platform that needs one, the publisher's bridge sees what
SIP-45 allows a distributor to see, per device token, for every user of
the application. That is the floor the platform sets, not this design;
the design keeps the bridge at that floor by handing it nothing to forward.

## Reference implementation

The exchange side is in `sqexd` (sqex 0.67.0): the whitelist handed to the
transport includes every device registered to a listed account, re-derived
on register and revoke and, for a credential that runs out with nobody at
the door, by the minute sweep; and a device's wake clock is cleared when
its last stream ends. Tests: a registered device of a listed account is let
in, revoked or expired it is closed and refused, and a device that answered
a wake and left is woken again for the next post. The client side -- a
phone build of sigil, or a client that shares `sqex-chat` with it -- does
not exist yet; until one does, a desktop test client stands in as SIP-45
says.
