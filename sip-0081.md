## Abstract

SIP-22 keeps an account's devices in the registry of the exchange they
registered at, and SIP-17 seals a channel's key to every device of every
member as `/device/list` reports them. SIP-59 moves an account's home,
and SIP-60 has an exchange that holds **no** devices for an account ask
its home. An exchange that holds *some* -- the account's former home,
above all, where its devices registered before the Move -- answers
those, which are the devices the account had when it left: a device
revoked at the new home is still listed, a device linked there is not,
and after a SIP-62 handover the new key's devices are nowhere in the
list at all. A member reading a copy at the former home seals the
channel key to a device the account has withdrawn and not to the one it
uses. This document has an exchange that records an account as living
elsewhere answer `/device/list` with what its home lists, holding its
own registry only for the case where the home cannot be asked, and
cache the home's answer briefly so a channel of sixty members does not
cost sixty round trips per poll.

## Motivation

The former-home case is the ordinary one. An account lives at X for a
year, registers a phone and a laptop there, and moves to H. Its
conversations with X's members go on being ordered at X (SIP-59: "the
exchange you left goes on serving the conversations that live there");
the members at X go on sealing every epoch key to the account's devices
as X lists them. The account loses the phone, revokes it at H, links a
new one at H. X never hears: revocation is the home's (SIP-22), and
the new phone registered at H. Every key at X from then on is sealed
to a device in a drawer and not to the one in the account's hand -- and
the account's new phone reads nothing at X until the account itself
notices and rotates, which SIP-17 lets it do, once it has worked out
why.

SIP-60 saw this for an exchange that never knew the account, and asked
the home. It did not ask for an exchange that once knew it, and that
is the exchange whose list is most confidently wrong: not empty, just
old.

## Specification

**Home**, **away** and **Move** are SIP-59's; a **learned home** is
SIP-60's; `/device/list`, `Devices` and the **registry** are SIP-22's.

### Whose list

On `/device/list` for an account, an exchange answers:

1. **its own registry**, where the account's home is this exchange or
   nowhere on record -- SIP-22 as it stands;
2. **the home's list**, where the account is recorded as living at
   another exchange, by its signed Move or a learned home -- whether or
   not this exchange holds devices of its own for it. The home is asked
   `/device/list` as SIP-60 asks it, and its answer is served unchanged,
   credentials included: it is the home's word, as SIP-60 already made
   it, and this exchange verifies nothing of it;
3. **its own registry**, where the account lives elsewhere and the home
   could not be asked -- unreachable, refusing, or from before SIP-60 --
   as SIP-60 answers its own (empty) list in that case today. The
   answer is what this exchange held when the account left, and a
   caller cannot tell it from a current one (§Compatibility).

### Caching

An exchange MAY keep the home's answer for an account for up to
`DEVICES_TTL = 60` seconds and serve it again within that time. The
`now` in a served `Devices` is the home's own from the answer kept,
not this exchange's clock; a caller checking a credential's validity
against `now` checks it as of the home's answer, which is at most a
minute old. A failed ask is not kept: the next `/device/list` asks
again.

### What is not changed

`/device/register` and `/device/revoke` are served where they are
served today. An exchange that records an account as away does not
carry a registration or revocation made at it to the home, and this
document does not add to SIP-59's `moved` list; a device registering
at a former home is a client from before SIP-59's Move handling, and
the home's list is what everyone else will see.

## Rationale

**Why the home's list over the union.** A union of the former home's
registry and the home's answer would keep a revoked device in the
list, which is the failure this document exists for. The home is where
the account revokes (SIP-22) and links, and after SIP-62 the only
place its current key's devices are; every other list is a snapshot of
it.

**Why the own registry when the home cannot be asked, and not a
refusal.** A refusal here breaks the two things a client does with the
list at once: sealing a key, which could wait, and binding a signature
to an account for display, which is on the read path of every poll and
would leave a channel unreadable while a home was down. A stale list
lets a key be sealed to a device that may be gone -- the state before
this document -- for the duration of an outage, and keeps reading
working. It is stated as the weaker answer rather than dressed as the
current one.

**Why a cache, and why a minute.** SIP-60's ask was for an account the
exchange had never seen, a rare case. This one fires for every away
member of every channel a client binds on every poll, and a client
already asks for sixty at once. A minute is long enough to collapse a
poll's asks into one round trip per account and short enough that a
revocation at the home is seen at the former home before the account
has finished telling the person who took the phone. The home's `now`
is served rather than this exchange's so that nothing is asserted
twice.

## Compatibility

Additive; no wire change. An exchange from before this document
answers its own registry as today. A client sees a list that is
current where the home answered and old where it did not, in the
same shape; it cannot distinguish the two, which is what it could not
do before either, and which a later document can mark with a field
`Devices` does not yet have -- SIP-22's decoder refuses trailing
bytes, so the field waits for a version of it that does not.

## Security considerations

**What the home learns.** That some exchange asked for the account's
devices, which SIP-60 discloses already for the empty case and SIP-39
for name resolution. The home already admits the asking exchange's key
or does not; nothing new is admitted.

**What the asking exchange serves.** The home's signed credentials,
unchanged; a client verifies each against the account as SIP-32 says,
so a home that listed a device the account never linked would be
caught by every reader, and this exchange adds nothing a reader would
have to trust.

**A learned home that lies.** SIP-60's learned home makes this exchange
proxy and tell, never serve; here it makes it proxy a list. A domain
exchange that named a false home could have this exchange serve that
home's device list for the account -- credentials the reader verifies
against the account key and refuses where they fail, so the false home
can withhold devices and cannot add one. Withholding is denial, which
the domain exchange could do more simply.

**Staleness.** Bounded by the cache's minute where the home answers,
and by the outage where it does not; §Rationale says why the second is
accepted.

## Reference implementation

`sqex` 0.99.0. `sqexd`: `/device/list` asks `Server::devices_at_home`
for any account `Registry::where_is` places elsewhere (a signed Move or
a SIP-60 learned home), before consulting its own registry;
`devices_at_home` keeps the home's answer `DEVICES_TTL` = 60 s per
account and does not keep a failure. Tests (`devices_away_flow`): Alice's
phone and laptop register at X; she moves to H, where the laptop and a
tablet register; Bob at X is told laptop and tablet -- phone and laptop
with the home never asked, which is the negative control -- and a
former home whose home cannot be reached answers what it held.

Run live on 2026-09-20, both exchanges on 0.99.0. A throwaway account
homed at a laptop exchange linked a second device there (two in the
laptop's registry), then moved to trunk.exchange, where only its own
key registered. Asked for the account's devices, the laptop -- its
former home, holding both in its own registry -- answered
trunk.exchange's list: one device, with trunk.exchange's `added` and
`now`, not its own. The other direction showed rule 3 as written:
trunk.exchange, asked for an account whose home is a laptop it has no
route to, answered its own registry, which was empty.

One finding beside the document. The scratchpad tool used to probe
presents "I live here" for whatever identity it runs as, and probing
the laptop as an account homed at trunk.exchange moved that account
there; a Move is one request, and a tool that makes it on start-up is
a tool that moves accounts by accident. Put back the same minute.
