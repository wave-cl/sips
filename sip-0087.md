## Abstract

SIP-17's channel key is thirty-two bytes an admin generates and hands out. This
document replaces *where the key comes from* and changes nothing else: an epoch
key is derived from a **chain** every member already holds and a **commit secret**
a member contributes, so no single party chooses it and nobody outside the chain
can compute it from the contribution alone. The commit is a signed entry in the
channel's own log, so an addition is an act every member verifies before the key
exists. SIP-17's envelope wire is untouched — the same `Envelope`, the same SIP-5
construction, the same SIP-23 prekeys — because what travels in it is now a
commit secret rather than a key. The property this buys is **post-compromise
security**: after a member's key leaks, that member's own next commit removes the
attacker, without asking an admin.

## Motivation

SIP-17 names the problem this document exists for, and names it as the worst one
in the design:

> **An admin is trusted by construction, and this is the weakest point in the
> design.** An admin can mint an epoch and seal it to any identity at all,
> including one the other members would object to and one that is not in the
> member list. The member list in SIP-16's `Info` is what makes the second case
> visible, and clients MUST show it — but an admin who adds an identity and then
> removes it has still handed over a key that stays valid until the next
> rotation. There is no defence within this model. There is a defence in a group
> key agreement, and this SIP does not have one.

It also names where the defence would attach, and promises the carriage survives:

> A proper continuous group key agreement — every member contributing, no single
> party able to choose the key — exists, is well studied, and is far larger than
> this. […] This is the natural place for a later SIP, and the envelope wire
> would not have to change.

### Answering the reason SIP-17 gave for waiting

SIP-17 did not defer this for want of interest. It gave a reason, and the reason
is still true:

> It was rejected for the first version because the rest of the model already
> trusts the admin with membership: an admin who wants to read a channel adds an
> identity of their own, which no key agreement prevents. Paying for a protocol
> that closes one door while the door beside it is open is the wrong first trade.

That is correct and this document does not contradict it. **An admin who is
willing to be seen can still add an identity and read everything said
afterwards.** No key agreement prevents it, this one included, and a document
claiming otherwise would be lying.

What changes is the part SIP-17's own sentence turns on — *"adds an identity and
then removes it has still handed over a key that stays valid until the next
rotation"*. Three things stop being possible:

1. **A silent addition.** Under SIP-17 the key exists as soon as the admin
   generates it, and the envelopes are the only trace; the member list is a
   separate fact that an admin can move back. Here the epoch key **does not
   exist** until a commit naming the member set is in the log, signed, and read
   by the members. There is no key to hand out ahead of the record of who it was
   handed to.
2. **A retroactive addition.** An identity added at epoch *n* cannot compute
   epoch *n−1*, because the chain runs forward and the commit secret alone is not
   the key. Under SIP-17 an admin can seal any past epoch to anybody at any time,
   and `from_epoch`/`to_epoch` exist precisely to make that easy.
3. **An addition outside the key.** An admin can envelope a commit secret to an
   outsider, and the outsider still cannot read the channel: without the chain
   the contribution is thirty-two useless bytes. Membership and readership were
   two facts SIP-17 kept in step by convention. Here they are one fact.

And one requirement SIP-17 could only ask for becomes structural. SIP-17 says an
admin *MUST* rotate after a removal and admits the exchange cannot check it:
*"it holds no key and cannot tell whether one changed"*. Here a removal **is** a
commit, and a commit **is** a new epoch: there is no way to express the removal
without the rotation, so the interface that says "removed" and means "cannot
post" cannot be written.

### The property that is new rather than repaired

**Post-compromise security.** An attacker who holds a member's identity key and
a copy of the traffic reads the channel until an epoch whose commit secret they
do not receive. Any uncompromised member's commit re-randomises the chain, and
the commit secret reaches each device under a SIP-23 prekey that is destroyed on
use — so the attacker cannot recompute it even holding the identity key they
stole. Nothing in the stack offers this today: rotation is an admin's decision,
and a member who suspects their own device has to ask somebody else to act.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. Epochs, sealing, subkey derivation and the
`Envelope` wire are **SIP-17's, unchanged**; this document replaces only the
derivation of the epoch key and adds the entry that carries a commit.

Contexts are the ASCII strings `sqex-chain-v1`, `sqex-epoch-v1` and
`sqex-commit-v1`.

### The chain

A private channel carries, beside its epoch number, a **chain secret**: 32
bytes, held by every member device and by nobody else. It is never transmitted
except in a `Welcome` (below) and is never given to the exchange.

```
chain_0  = 32 random bytes, generated by the channel's creator
```

The creator generates `chain_0` uniformly at random and the channel has no epoch
until the first commit, exactly as SIP-17 has it: a private channel is created
with no epoch, reported as 0, and nothing can be posted until it is keyed.

### A commit

A member changes the epoch by committing. A commit is an ordinary SIP-16 entry,
signed by the committing device under SIP-31 and chained and receipted like any
other, with a body an exchange can read:

```
Commit | epoch: u32 | committer[32] | adds: u8 | removes: u8
       | add[32] × adds | remove[32] × removes
       | contribution[32] | transcript[32] | sig[64]
```

| field | meaning |
|---|---|
| `epoch` | the epoch this commit creates. MUST be the channel's current epoch plus one |
| `committer` | the **account** the committing device acts for |
| `add` / `remove` | accounts entering and leaving at this epoch, ascending by key bytes, no repeats, disjoint |
| `contribution` | the X25519 public key matching the commit secret, published so members can verify the envelopes they were sent agree with the log |
| `transcript` | the transcript hash below |
| `sig` | Ed25519 over every preceding field under `sqex-commit-v1`, by the committing device |

`adds` and `removes` MUST be consistent with SIP-16's membership after the entry:
the exchange already enforces membership and already refuses a post under a
superseded epoch, and this document asks nothing further of it.

*Amended 2026-10-01, from the implementation.* **`transcript` is
`transcript_{n-1}`** — the transcript the committer believes stands *before* this
commit, not the one this commit produces. The other reading is not available: the
derivation below says `commit_n` is the commit body "excluding `sig`", so a
`transcript` field holding `transcript_n` would be hashed into its own
definition. This is also the reading that does the work the field exists for. A
continuing member checks the claim against the value it holds and **MUST refuse a
commit that disagrees**, which is how two members who disagree about history find
out before they disagree about a key; a joining device has no history to check it
against and needs exactly this value to compute `transcript_n` for itself.

A committer **MUST NOT** name its own account in `adds`. It is already inside the
key it is deriving, and a device of it that holds no chain is a sibling's to
supply under SIP-42 rather than a `Welcome`'s — see the amendment to §A Welcome,
where naming yourself would have you read your own chain back as an admission.

### Deriving the epoch key

```
transcript_n = SHA-512(sqex-chain-v1 || transcript_{n-1} || commit_n)[0..32]
chain_n      = SHA-512(sqex-chain-v1 || chain_{n-1} || commit_secret
                       || transcript_n)[0..32]
key_n        = SHA-512(sqex-epoch-v1 || chain_n || transcript_n)[0..32]
```

`transcript_0` is thirty-two zero bytes. `commit_n` is the commit body above
**excluding `sig`**, so a member verifies the transcript from what the log holds.
`key_n` is a SIP-17 channel key in every respect: entries are sealed under it
with SIP-17's per-sender subkeys, and the exchange sees an epoch number and
ciphertext as before.

**Three things this derivation is doing, and each is load-bearing:**

- `chain_{n-1}` is why an outsider handed `commit_secret` gets nothing, and why
  an identity added at epoch *n* cannot reach *n−1*.
- `commit_secret` is why the committer cannot be predicted or replayed, and is
  the whole of post-compromise security.
- `transcript_n` is why the key is bound to the membership. Two members who
  disagree about who was added derive different keys and discover it on the next
  entry, rather than agreeing on a key while disagreeing about the group.

**No single party chooses `key_n`.** The committer chooses `commit_secret` and
nothing else; `chain_{n-1}` is the accumulated contribution of every member who
has ever committed, and `transcript_n` is fixed by the log.

### Carrying the commit secret

`commit_secret` reaches each member **device** in a SIP-17 `Envelope`,
unchanged — same fields, same SIP-5 construction, same SIP-23 prekey, same
`MUST` on a fresh ephemeral and on rejecting a non-contributory term. The
plaintext is thirty-two bytes and `from_epoch` equals `to_epoch` equals the
commit's `epoch`.

The committer MUST envelope to every device of every account that is a member
**after** the commit, itself included, and MUST NOT envelope to a removed
account's devices.

A member device MUST check that `X25519(base, commit_secret)` equals the
commit's `contribution` before using it. Without that check a committer could
send different secrets to different devices, which is equivocation about the key
rather than about the log, and no amount of receipt comparison would find it.

### A Welcome

A joining device holds no chain, so it needs one. A `Welcome` is a SIP-17
`Envelope` whose plaintext is

```
chain[32] || key[32] × (to_epoch - from_epoch + 1)
```

— the chain secret as of the commit that admitted it, followed by whatever past
epoch keys the inviter chose to include. **How many is the inviter's choice, and
SIP-17 §History is the inviter's choice governs it unchanged.**

A recipient distinguishes a `Welcome` from a commit secret **by its own state**:
a device holding no chain secret for the channel reads the envelope as a
`Welcome`, and one holding a chain reads it as a commit secret. This needs no
wire change, which is the point, and it has one failure mode worth naming: a
device that has lost its chain — a restore from a backup older than the current
epoch — would read a commit secret as a `Welcome` and derive nonsense. The
defence is in the log: the commit names its `epoch` and its `adds` in the clear,
so a device MUST read the commit before opening the envelope, and MUST treat an
envelope for an epoch whose commit does not name it as an add as a commit secret
regardless of what it holds.

*Amended 2026-10-01, from the implementation.* Two corrections, and the first is
a wire arithmetic error in the plaintext above.

**The chain takes the first slot of the range, not a slot in front of it.** SIP-17
fixes an envelope's ciphertext at `16 + 32 × range` bytes for the range
`from_epoch..=to_epoch`, and a decoder checks it — so `chain[32] || key[32] ×
(to_epoch - from_epoch + 1)` is one slot longer than the envelope this document
promises not to change. The plaintext is therefore

```
plaintext = chain[32] || key[32] × (to_epoch - from_epoch)
```

where `to_epoch` is the commit's `epoch`, slot 0 is the chain, and slot *i* is the
key for epoch `from_epoch + i`. The smallest `Welcome` is two slots, with
`from_epoch` one below the admitted epoch, carrying the chain and that epoch's
key; §History is the inviter's choice governs how many more, unchanged. A device
**MUST** refuse a `Welcome` whose range does not end at the commit's epoch, and
**MUST** refuse one whose key for that epoch is not the one its chain derives —
the second is the only check a joiner can make on a `Welcome`, since there is no
commit secret to check `contribution` against, and it catches an inviter who hands
over a chain and a key that disagree.

One consequence of the layout is worth stating so that nobody builds on it: a
reader that mistook a `Welcome` for an ordinary SIP-17 envelope would store the
right key for every epoch in the range bar the first and a useless one for
`from_epoch`. That is an accident and not a compatibility guarantee — such a
reader holds no chain and cannot derive a single epoch after the one it was
handed.

**The commit decides which kind an envelope is, in both directions.** The rule
above decides by the recipient's own state and lets the log override it one way.
That has no answer for a device holding a *stale* chain which is then re-admitted:
it would read its welcome as a commit secret, refuse it, and have nothing left to
try but another commit. So: an envelope for an epoch whose commit names the
recipient's account in `adds` is a `Welcome`, and every other envelope is a commit
secret, whatever the device holds. One rule instead of two, and it is the rule the
overriding sentence was already reaching for. The cost is bounded and
attributable: a committer who names a continuing member in `adds` makes that
member overwrite its chain and derive a key nobody shares — a denial of service by
a party who could equally have sent a wrong secret, over a signature, which is the
same trade `contribution` is for.

### A device must not open an envelope it cannot classify

*Added 2026-10-01, from the implementation.* "Read the commit first" is not only
about deriving correctly. SIP-23's prekey is destroyed on use, so an envelope
opened under the wrong reading **cannot be opened again** — a `Welcome`'s chain
stored as an epoch key is thirty-two bytes that decrypt nothing and an envelope
nobody will send a second time. A device that cannot yet say which rule produced
an epoch therefore leaves the envelope where it is; the exchange holds it and
serves it again.

Three facts let a device say it is sure, and it needs one of them:

- a commit for that epoch is in hand;
- it has read the log as far as the newest entry the exchange holds, and found
  none — the *absence* of a commit is a fact about the channel only once the log
  has been read that far;
- it already holds an epoch key for the channel and no chain, which is a channel
  it has established is admin-keyed.

A device with none of them reads the log before it opens anything, which may cost
a round trip and costs it once. For SIP-47 the consequence is sharper and is
normative: **a catch-up MUST take the commits out of the entries it carries
before opening the envelopes it carries**, because both arrive in one answer and
the whole value of asking once is lost if the envelopes have to wait for a second
round.

### Limits

| | |
|---|---|
| `adds` per commit | ≤ 64 |
| `removes` per commit | ≤ 64 |
| Envelopes per commit | bounded by SIP-16's `MAX_MEMBERS` × SIP-22's `MAX_DEVICES` |

At SIP-16's cap of 256 accounts and SIP-22's of 8 devices, a commit is 2 048
envelopes — the same arithmetic SIP-17's rotation already pays, and the same
number `MAX_DEVICES` was chosen against. A commit is not cheaper than a rotation;
it is a rotation with a different provenance.

## Rationale

**Why a chain and not a tree.** MLS's TreeKEM is the well-studied mechanism
SIP-17 was pointing at, and it is genuinely better: a commit costs O(log N)
ciphertexts rather than O(N), which at a large group is the difference between
practical and not. It is deliberately not proposed here, for two reasons and one
of them is a measurement the corpus already has. The first: a tree is a great
deal of machinery — parent nodes, path secrets, resolution, blanking, tree
signing — and every part of it is a way to get the group's key wrong. The second:
SIP-16 caps a channel at 256 members and SIP-22 caps a person at 8 devices, and
SIP-17's rotation already costs 2 048 envelopes at that ceiling without anybody
having called it unaffordable. A tree buys nothing until the cap moves, and if
the cap moves this document's chain is the obvious thing to put a tree under —
`chain_n` is the value a tree would produce, and the key schedule and the
envelope carriage would not change.

**Why the previous chain is mixed in at all.** Without it the scheme is SIP-17
with extra steps: the committer picks a secret, envelopes it, and anybody handed
it can read. Mixing `chain_{n-1}` is what makes the contribution insufficient on
its own, and it is therefore the whole of the "outside the key" property. It also
makes a joiner's admission explicit rather than incidental: to bring somebody in
you must hand them the chain, and the only place it is handed over is a
`Welcome` named by a commit in the log.

**Why the transcript is hashed in rather than merely signed.** A signature over
the membership would let a member *check* the group and still derive the key if
it disagreed. Hashing the transcript into the key means disagreement is not a
warning, it is a decryption failure. This is the same instinct as SIP-17's
per-sender subkeys: make the structural thing structural rather than conventional.

**Why the commit is an entry and not a route of its own.** It needs to be
ordered, durable, attributable and visible to every member — which is what a
SIP-16 entry already is, with SIP-31's signature and chaining and SIP-34's
receipt for free. A dedicated route would have needed its own ordering and its
own equivocation story, and the equivocation story is the reason the entry is
attractive: an exchange that shows two members different commit histories is
caught by comparing two receipts for one position, which SIP-34 already
specifies.

**Why the exchange is given nothing new.** It stores a commit as a body it
cannot interpret beyond the fields above, orders it, and refuses a post under a
superseded epoch as it already does. It is not asked to validate a commit, to
know who may commit, or to track the chain. That is deliberate: the exchange is
trusted for availability and never for authenticity, and a document that made it
the authority on who is in the group would be arguing against the stack.

**Why any member may commit, rather than admins only.** Post-compromise security
is the point, and a member who suspects their own device is exactly the party who
must be able to act without permission. SIP-16's roles still govern *membership*:
an `add` or `remove` by a non-admin is a commit the exchange refuses on SIP-16's
existing rules, so an ordinary member's commit is one with `adds = 0` and
`removes = 0` — a rekey and nothing else. The asymmetry is deliberate and is the
smallest one that makes healing a member's own decision.

*Amended 2026-10-01, from the implementation.* **This is not true as written, and
the document it contradicts is SIP-17.** SIP-17 §Put says a non-admin publishing
envelopes MUST use the channel's current epoch, with exactly one exception — a
member for whom SIP-22 holds a revocation made since the current epoch was minted
— and SIP-17's own amendment for this document says "Nothing in this document
changes". An exchange implementing SIP-17 as written refuses an ordinary member's
epoch-advancing `Put` as `NotAnAdmin`, which the implementation below confirms by
test, including the converse: with SIP-17's two checks removed, the commit goes
through end to end.

Less is lost than it appears, and it is worth being exact about which part
survives. **The healing case this document exists for is reachable**: a member who
suspects a device of its own revokes it under SIP-22 and may then commit, which is
precisely the exception SIP-17 wrote and for precisely this reason. What is not
reachable is a *scheduled* rekey by an ordinary member — and that was already the
first of the three open questions below rather than a settled property. A
deployment that wants it must amend SIP-17 §Put, not this document, and should
weigh it against the denial of service in Security considerations: every commit
spends every other device a prekey, so a rule that lets anybody commit at will is
a rule that lets anybody drain everybody.

**What was considered and rejected for the joiner's carriage.** A `kind` byte on
the `Envelope` would make a `Welcome` unambiguous and would break SIP-17's
promise that the envelope wire need not change — and the wire is what two
implementations have to agree on, so changing it is the expensive kind of change.
Disambiguating by the recipient's own state costs one normative sentence about
reading the commit first. A second rejected option was a separate route for
`Welcome`, which reintroduces the ordering problem the entry was chosen to avoid.

## Compatibility

**Breaking for a private channel, and per channel rather than per exchange.** A
SIP-17 client cannot read a channel keyed this way: it would look for an envelope
carrying a channel key and find one carrying a commit secret, and derive a key
that decrypts nothing. A client implementing this document cannot read a
SIP-17-keyed channel's *new* epochs either, because there is no chain behind them.

The upgrade path is therefore a channel's own, and it is the one SIP-17 already
provides: a channel moves by an admin minting one last SIP-17 epoch, and then a
first commit whose `chain_0` is fresh. Members who hold the old epochs go on
reading history under them — history is not re-keyed and does not need to be.
**A channel MUST NOT mix the two**: whether a channel is agreed or admin-keyed is
fixed at its first commit, because a client deriving the current epoch has to
know which rule produced it.

Nothing between a peer and the exchange changes. SIP-16's request wire, SIP-31's
signing input, SIP-34's receipts and SIP-17's `Envelope` are untouched, and an
exchange needs no knowledge of this document to carry a channel that uses it —
which also means an exchange cannot tell which kind a private channel is, and
neither can a replica.

Both ends of a channel must move together. An exchange need not move at all.

## Security considerations

**An admin who is willing to be seen can still read the channel.** This is
SIP-17's objection, it is not closed, and it is the first thing to say. An admin
adds an identity, commits, and reads everything from that epoch forward. What
this document removes is the silent version, the retroactive version and the
version where membership and readership disagree. A deployment that needs
confidentiality from its own admins needs a different membership model, not a
different key schedule.

**Forward secrecy of history is still unavailable, and this document does not
change that.** SIP-23 states the reason and it is structural: *"A device that can
show you last month's conversation is a device that holds last month's keys, and
compromising it yields them whatever this SIP does."* A client MAY discard
`chain_{n-1}` once it has derived `chain_n`, which bounds what compromise of the
*chain* yields to the current epoch forward — but the epoch keys themselves are
retained to read history, so the haul from a stolen device is what it always was.
An implementation MUST NOT describe this document as giving forward secrecy.

**Removal is still prospective.** A removed member keeps every epoch key it held
and every message it read. Revocation is prospective everywhere in this design
(SIP-22), and a commit that removes somebody bounds the future, not the past. The
honest claim is narrow and worth making precisely: under SIP-17 a removal might
not be followed by a rotation at all, and here it cannot fail to be.

**A committer can equivocate about the key, which is why `contribution` is
checked.** Two devices sent different commit secrets derive different epoch keys
and each concludes the other is broken. The mandatory check against
`contribution` in the log turns that into a detectable disagreement with a named
author: the log says which public key the secret was supposed to match, the
committer signed it, and a device that was sent something else can show both.

**The exchange can still withhold, reorder and equivocate about the log**, and a
commit inherits exactly the protections an entry has — SIP-31's chaining, SIP-34's
receipts, and SIP-35's replication making equivocation findable. It gains one
thing over an ordinary entry: withholding a commit does not merely hide a message,
it stops a member posting, because the member cannot derive the current epoch.
That makes withholding *loud* where withholding a message is quiet, which is a
small improvement in the exchange's accountability and is worth stating as one.

**A member can stall the group by committing constantly.** Each commit costs
every other device an envelope to open and a prekey to spend, and SIP-23's pool
is finite — so a member committing in a loop drains every other member's pool and
is a denial of service on joining, not only on the channel. SIP-56's rate limits
are per account and per channel and are the right place for this; a deployment
using this document SHOULD limit commits more tightly than posts, because the
cost of one falls on everybody else.

**A lost chain is unrecoverable from the exchange**, by construction — it holds
no part of it. A device that has lost its chain and holds no sibling to ask must
be admitted again by a commit, which is visible to the whole channel. SIP-17
notes the same shape for a stranded device and that nothing in it lets a device
*request* an envelope; that gap is unchanged here and is not closed by this
document.

**Nothing here is deniable, and that was already spent.** A commit is signed by
a device, so who changed the group is attributable forever. The corpus took that
trade for replication's sake and SIP-31 records it; this document does not
reopen it.

## Reference implementation

*Written 2026-10-01.* `sqex` 0.119.0.

- **Wire and schedule**: `crates/sqex-proto/src/agreement.rs` — the commit body,
  the three-hash schedule, the chain, the `Welcome` slots, and the three refusals.
- **Client**: `crates/sqex-chat/src/client.rs` — `Chat::commit`,
  `Chat::create_agreed_group`, the collection path that classifies an envelope
  from the log, and `Chat::refusal` for the case where it will not.
- **Fold**: `crates/sqex-proto/src/timeline.rs` — a commit is a third thing an
  entry can be, beside a message and the exchange's own record.
- **Nothing in `sqexd`.** That is the claim §Why the exchange is given nothing new
  makes, and it held: no route, no table, no loop, with
  `crates/sqexd/tests/suite/route_coverage.rs` — a machine-checked route
  inventory — confirming the first.

Tests: the schedule and its refusals in `sqex_proto::agreement`'s own unit tests,
and the rest in `crates/sqex-chat/tests/agreed_flow.rs`. Each was shown to fail
against a deliberately broken build before it was trusted.

### What the implementation settled

**Whether a rekey-only commit is cheap enough to do on a schedule.** Answered from
an unexpected direction: for an ordinary member it is not *available* at any
price, for the reason in the amendment to §Why any member may commit above. For an
admin it costs a rotation, which this stack has never called unaffordable. So the
question changes shape — the number still missing is a sensible interval, and what
is now known is that choosing it is an admin's act, not a member's.

**Whether the `Welcome` disambiguation survives contact with a real restore.** It
does, and the failure the question feared cannot arise once the commit decides
rather than the device's own state. What a real restore does instead is
**recover**: a backup carries the SIP-23 prekey secrets it was taken with, so a
device two epochs behind opens every envelope published since and walks its chain
forward one commit at a time. The refusal the question asked for is still
reachable, and the party who arranges it is the one Security considerations
already names — an epoch whose commit secret never reaches the device, which an
exchange arranges by withholding. The negative control is that case, and the
refusal is explicit rather than silent: the chain does not move, no key is
written, and the device reports which epoch it stands at and which one it was
handed. Its only way forward is to be admitted again by a commit, which is what
this document says and which the control also exercises.

**What the client shows.** `sqex-chat` draws a commit in the transcript, in the
order the exchange gave it, as *"new key (epoch 3) — added Bob, removed Carol"*
beside who committed and when — and counts it as activity, so a channel whose only
new entry is a commit does not look asleep. That is the property made visible,
which is what the question asked for. Sigil, the graphical client, draws nothing
yet; it draws none of SIP-16's membership events either, so this is one gap in two
clients rather than a gap this document opened.

### And one thing the three questions did not ask

**A commit's body is readable in a channel where everything else is sealed, and
nothing on the wire says which entries those are.** This document says only that
the body is "one an exchange can read" and leaves the consequence unstated: a
reader has to tell a plaintext commit from a ciphertext message with no kind byte,
no flag and no new request type, because §Compatibility promises an exchange
"cannot tell which kind a private channel is". The mechanism is the signature. A
commit body decodes to a fixed shape every field of which is covered by an Ed25519
signature under `sqex-commit-v1` by the device the entry names, and a sealed body
is indistinguishable from random to anyone without the key — random does not
verify. An implementation **MUST** check the signature and **MUST** check that the
body's `committer` is the account the entry is attributed to, which catches a
commit lifted out of one member's entry and reposted by another.
