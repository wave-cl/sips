## Abstract

An account's public output, as a log of its own: append-only, signed by the
author's devices, numbered by the author rather than by the exchange, and
served to anybody who asks. The account key is the whole identifier — there is
nothing to create, nothing to join, nobody to admit. A reader follows somebody
by keeping their key in a list the exchange never sees, and reads many feeds in
one request. **A feed post is a self-contained signed artifact and is permanent
in practice**, which SIP-27 already said of an attestation and which is truer
here, because a feed's audience is everybody who asked rather than at most
SIP-16's two hundred and fifty-six members.

## Motivation

There is nowhere in this stack to say something to no one in particular.

Everything that carries words is a channel (SIP-16), and a channel is a room:
it is created, it has a membership the exchange enforces on, it caps at 256
members, and `Fetch` requires membership even where the channel is public.
SIP-16 calls that last row load-bearing and says why:

> **`Fetch` requiring membership is the load-bearing row.** [...] the directory
> shows names and topics, and reading the contents means joining first. That
> gives an exchange somewhere to apply a rate limit and gives a public room a
> membership to moderate, and it costs a stranger one request.

That is the right trade for a room and the wrong one for a publication. An
author with a thousand readers cannot have them, and if they could, the exchange
would hold the list of who reads what — which SIP-16 §The graph itself already
calls "strictly more disclosure than anything else in the stack" for the
narrower fact of who *talks* to whom.

This document was first drafted as a third channel visibility. It needed a
different answer on fifteen rows of SIP-16 §Who may call what, suppression of
the join and leave system entries, exclusion from the directory, a new
abandonment condition, an anti-squatting rule, and an amendment to §A reset
sequence space. A shape needing that many exceptions is not the shape. **A feed
is not a room**, and four of those problems do not arise once it stops
pretending to be one: there is no directory to be excluded from, no mintable
identifier to squat, no abandonment condition because an empty feed answers as
an absent one does, and a reset sequence space cannot occur because the serial
belongs to the author and never restarts.

What a feed is instead is an account-scoped record the exchange serves on
request, which is SIP-21's shape, holding signed artifacts anybody may carry
away, which is SIP-27's. Both of those already exist and neither is a channel.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. The context string is the ASCII
`sqex-feed-v1`, twelve bytes, with no terminator.

### What identifies a feed

**The account key, and nothing else.** There is no feed identifier, no
derivation and no instance. SIP-38 resolves `name@domain` to an account; the
account is the feed.

An exchange MUST answer for an account's feed whether or not that account has
ever appended to it. An account that has appended nothing is answered `found:
0`, which is the same answer as an account this exchange does not serve and the
same answer as a feed withheld by a block — SIP-4's rule and SIP-21's, for
SIP-21's reason: "answering 'exists but hidden' would itself be the disclosure
that hiding was meant to prevent."

### The post

```
Post | account[32] | device[32] | serial: u64 | prev[32] | issued_at: u64
     | expires_after: u32 | flags: u8 | body_hash[32] | body_len: u32 | body |

post_input = "sqex-feed-v1" || SHA-256( account[32] || device[32] || serial(8)
                                        || prev[32] || issued_at(8)
                                        || expires_after(4) || flags(1)
                                        || body_hash[32] )
sig        = Ed25519(device_secret, post_input)
```

A post on the wire is `Post` followed by `sig[64]`. The header is 153 bytes and
the signature 64; a signer sees 44 bytes, hash-then-sign over fixed width, as
SIP-10, SIP-20, SIP-27 and SIP-31 all do.

| field | meaning |
|---|---|
| `account` | whose feed this is |
| `device` | which of that account's devices signed, bound by a SIP-20 credential |
| `serial` | position in the feed, from 1; the author's number, not the exchange's |
| `prev` | `SHA-256(post_input)` of the post at `serial - 1`; 32 zero bytes at `serial` 1 |
| `issued_at` | the author's clock, advisory; see §What the clocks are |
| `expires_after` | seconds after which the exchange drops the body, 0 for never |
| `flags` | reserved, MUST be zero |
| `body_hash` | `SHA-256(body)` |
| `body` | a SIP-19 `Body`, always in the clear |

**`body_hash` is a field and not merely a term in the signing input.** SIP-16's
`Entry` carries one for a single reason — a tombstone's signature must still
verify once its bytes are gone — and §Withdrawal needs the same. An exchange
MUST check that `SHA-256(body)` equals `body_hash` before accepting a post and
MUST refuse a mismatch distinguishably from a malformed one. Without that check
an author lodges a post committing to bytes nobody read, and the tombstone it
leaves behind commits to nothing.

`flags` is reserved and an exchange MUST refuse a post setting any bit, which is
SIP-21's rule for SIP-21's reason: a reserved bit that is merely ignored is a
reserved bit somebody will use.

### What the signing input deliberately omits

**No exchange key.** SIP-31 binds the origin's SIP-9 key into every channel
entry, because a direct message's identifier is derived from its two accounts
and so has byte-identical channel bytes at every exchange that has ever existed
— without the binding, an entry lifts between two exchanges' copies of one
conversation. A feed has the opposite requirement. A reader MUST be able to
verify a post that arrived proxied from another exchange, read from a mirror, or
restored from a SIP-48 backup, with no reference to where it came from. That is
SIP-27's property, and SIP-27 states the consequence plainly: an artifact "is
equally valid handed over on a USB stick".

So a feed post replays into any exchange's copy of that feed, and **that is
correct rather than tolerated**: there is one feed per account and it is the
same feed everywhere. SIP-31 needed the binding because a channel is a place. A
feed is not a place; it is an account's output.

**No instance.** A feed has no incarnation. Nothing but the author's own devices
can create or destroy the serial space, and they sign it.

### One chain, across every device

`prev` links signing inputs rather than wire bytes, which is SIP-31's primitive
reused rather than re-derived — SIP-34 already requires that a chain link and an
entry hash be one function, "they are not two hashes", and a third would make
the arithmetic worse for nobody's benefit.

**The chain is per feed and not per device, inverting SIP-31.** SIP-31 chains
per device because "a client cannot know what the exchange will number next, so
it cannot link to the channel's previous entry at signing time". Here the
exchange numbers nothing, so that reason is absent — and the cost SIP-31 accepts
in exchange is much worse here than there. SIP-31 §Security considerations:
"Ordering across devices is unverifiable and always will be." In a channel that
is a residual, because the channel has an order of its own. A feed *is* a single
sequence a reader renders in order, so per-device chains would hand the reader
*n* interleaved orders and no way to choose between them, which puts the
exchange back in the position SIP-34 was written to remove it from.

A device MUST read `/feed/head`, sign at `newest + 1`, and set `prev` to the
head's `post_input` hash. An exchange MUST refuse a post whose `serial` is not
exactly `newest + 1`, and MUST refuse one whose `prev` is not the head it holds.

**A refusal costs nothing, which is what makes this affordable.** The exchange
numbered nothing and spent nothing, so a refused post is re-signable: the device
re-reads the head and signs the same body again at the higher serial. That is
the inverse of SIP-31 §Resuming, which must warn that a device "resumes from the
greater of what it remembers and what the exchange reports, never from the
exchange's report alone", because an exchange under-reporting could induce two
signatures at one position and manufacture blame. Here under-reporting produces
a refusal rather than a fork, because the exchange checks `prev` against its own
head. **A feed cannot be made to fork by its own exchange.**

The fork that remains is the author's own to avoid: a device that signs while
disconnected and hands the post to a sibling (SIP-42) before any exchange has
accepted it can put two posts at one serial into the world. So: **a device MUST
NOT serve, back up, publish, or hand to a sibling a post the exchange has not
accepted.** A client that queues posts while offline holds them unsigned, or
signs them and re-signs on acceptance.

### A dense serial space, and what a hole means

**From `oldest` to `newest` the serial space has no holes.** Every removal from
the middle leaves a tombstone (§Withdrawal); removals from the oldest end
advance `oldest`. An exchange MUST maintain this.

A reader therefore reads the following, and an implementation SHOULD name them
distinctly:

| what the reader sees | what it means |
|---|---|
| `oldest` above the reader's cursor | the window moved; a gap it cannot fill, and ordinary |
| a hole within `oldest..newest` | the exchange dropped a post |
| `prev` not matching across adjacent serials | a post was rewritten |
| two valid signatures at one serial | a fork |
| `newest` below a serial the reader held | the exchange is behind, or lying |

**This contradicts SIP-31 §Security considerations, which says a gap is not a
fork, and the contradiction is a gain.** SIP-31 must treat a gap as ordinary
because pruning, retention and a member joining without history each make one,
and an implementation that cried tampering "would cry wolf on every channel with
a retention window". A feed admits nobody, so there is no history to withhold,
and tombstones every removal that is not from the oldest end. A hole means what
it says.

A client MUST distinguish **valid**, **forged** (the signature or the SIP-20
credential fails), and **broken** (the chain does not hold), and within broken
MAY say *dropped* where SIP-31 can only say *gap*.

### What a body may be

`body` is a SIP-19 `Body` — a type byte and a payload, always in the clear, with
no SIP-17 epoch. The exchange MUST NOT parse it. SIP-19's note holds unchanged:
"an implementer looking for the server side of this document will not find one,
and if they do find one, something has gone wrong."

| SIP-19 `type` | in a feed |
|---|---|
| `0x01` Post | meaningful; every part, with the two notes below |
| `0x03` Edit | meaningful; `target` is a **serial**, not a SIP-16 `seq` |
| `0x04` Redact | meaningful; corroborates a tombstone |
| `0x02` Reaction | no home here; see §Nothing comes in |
| `0x05` Metadata | MUST be ignored; a feed has no name or topic |
| `0x06`, `0x07` Call, CallEnd | MUST be ignored; a feed cannot ring |

`Part` `0x06 Via` carries over with its claim reinterpreted — the exchange the
author sent this through, where that is not the author's home — and remains the
author's own word about the author's own act. `Part` `0x07 Said` MUST be
ignored: nothing strands a feed post, because no origin orders it.

**A third outcome, which SIP-19 does not yet have a word for.** SIP-19
distinguishes "I do not know this", which is ignored, from "this is broken",
which is an error. A feed needs "I know this and it does not belong here". A
client MUST NOT report a `Metadata` body in a feed as an unknown type: that
would make a context restriction look like a version skip, and a later reader
would conclude it had learned something about the sender's software.

### Routes

```
Append   | type: u8 = 0x01 | Post || sig[64] |
Read     | type: u8 = 0x02 | account[32] | since: u64 | limit: u16 | dir: u8 |
Withdraw | type: u8 = 0x03 | account[32] | serial: u64 |
Since    | type: u8 = 0x04 | count: u16 | count × (account[32] || serial: u64) |
Head     | type: u8 = 0x05 | account[32] |
Set      | type: u8 = 0x06 | retention_secs: u32 | max_posts: u32 |

Appended | serial: u64 | received: u64 | head_input[32] | now: u64 |
Page     | found: u8 | oldest: u64 | newest: u64 | now: u64
         | count: u16 | count × Stored |
Stored   | received: u64 | len: u32 | Post || sig[64] |
Headed   | found: u8 | oldest: u64 | newest: u64 | head_input[32]
         | posts: u32 | bytes: u64 | retention_secs: u32 | max_posts: u32
         | now: u64 | seams: u8 | seams × (at: u64 || from[32]) |
Moved    | now: u64 | asked: u16 | count: u16
         | count × (index: u16 || state: u8 || source: u8
                    || oldest: u64 || newest: u64 || head_input[32]) |
Ack      | now: u64 |
```

`POST /feed/append`, `/feed/read`, `/feed/withdraw`, `/feed/since`,
`/feed/head`, `/feed/set`. Each MUST refuse a connection advertising no
identity.

| operation | who may call it |
|---|---|
| `Append` | a device of `account`, on its own account's feed and no other |
| `Read` | any identified caller, subject to §Blocking |
| `Withdraw` | a device of `account`, on its own account's feed |
| `Since` | any identified caller; each row answered as a `Read` would be |
| `Head` | any identified caller, subject to §Blocking |
| `Set` | a device of `account`, for its own account |

**`Append` is authorised twice and neither is redundant**: by the transport,
which makes the bytes chargeable to the right account's quota, and by the
signature, which makes the post portable to a reader who never saw the
connection.

**Anonymous reads are refused, and the reason is narrow.** SIP-27 serves
attestations to unidentified callers because "a store that would only disclose
to authenticated callers would be adding a boundary the artifact does not have",
which is true of a feed post too. The reason to require an identity anyway is
not access control — under SIP-3 an identity is free to mint — but that the
exchange needs an account to apply SIP-56's limits and §Blocking against. This
bounds cost, not access, exactly as SIP-5 says of its own quotas, and an
implementation MUST NOT present it as access control.

### Reading

`dir` is `0x00` forward, serials above `since`, oldest first; or `0x01`
backward, serials below `since`, newest first, with `since` of 0 meaning from
`newest`. `limit` is bounded by `MAX_PAGE` and `MAX_PAGE_BYTES`, whichever binds
first.

**A feed pages backwards and a channel does not, which is the difference between
being caught up to and being browsed.** SIP-16's `Fetch` has no backward mode
because a conversation is read forward from where you left it. A reader arriving
at a feed of ten thousand posts wants the last twenty. Adding a direction byte to
`Fetch` would have changed the shape of the most-called route in the exchange
for a case channels do not have.

There is no long poll on `Read`. A feed's latency budget is not a conversation's;
see §Learning that a feed moved.

### Reading many feeds at once

`Since` carries up to `MAX_SINCE` pairs of an account and the serial the caller
already holds. **The reply carries only the rows that are not unchanged**, and
`index` names each row's position in the request.

| `state` | meaning |
|---|---|
| `0x00` | unchanged — never transmitted, implied by absence |
| `0x01` | moved; `newest` is greater, read it |
| `0x02` | absent, withheld or blocked; every other field zero |
| `0x03` | truncated; `oldest` is above the serial asked, a gap the caller cannot fill |
| `0x04` | reset; `newest` is below the serial asked, which is always a fault |
| `0x05` | elsewhere; the home's key in the `head_input` field |
| `0x06` | succeeded; the successor's key in the `head_input` field |

`source` is `0x00` answered from here, `0x01` the home's answer, fresh, `0x02`
the home's answer kept within `FEED_TTL`, `0x03` **the home could not be asked**.

**A row whose home could not be reached MUST answer `source: 0x03` and MUST NOT
answer `state: 0x00`.** This is SIP-60 §Saying whose list it is applied here,
and it is the most important sentence in this section. SIP-60 added its `from`
byte because an exchange answering from its own stale registry was
indistinguishable from one answering currently. A batched route that cannot
distinguish "nothing new" from "I could not ask" is a route that silently stops
delivering, and a client has no way to notice.

This route exists because the alternative does not work. SIP-50 rejected
client-side reconstruction of what it needed in exactly these words: "N+1
requests per contact per poll, and it would still not know about the wake
registration. The exchange holds all of it and can answer in one reply." A
reader following two hundred people has the same arithmetic.

Rate-limiting it needs no amendment to SIP-56, whose rule is that
`rate_limited` "is a refusal to *do* and never a refusal to *read*", on the
ground that limiting a read "would be making its members pay for somebody else's
flood". A `Since` flood is the caller's own: they asked for five hundred rows.

### The follow list

A reader follows an account by keeping its key. **The list is the reader's and
the exchange never holds it**, which is what keeps the reading graph — broader
than the talking graph SIP-16 already calls the largest disclosure in the stack
— off the exchange entirely.

It is kept across devices and across a lost device by SIP-48, which already
stores a sealed copy of a device's store under a key the exchange never sees.
SIP-48 gains one segment kind:

```
| 0x05 | follows: count: u32 | count × (account[32] | added: u64) |
```

A follow list is client state and could ride in SIP-48's `0x03`, which is
"opaque to everything but the client that wrote it". It gets its own kind for
the reason SIP-48's contacts kind has one — a person's own findings "travel with
them" — and with more force: **a follow list is the one thing in a person's
store that cannot be re-derived from anywhere.** Lose it with no backup and it is
gone; keep it only as opaque bytes and it does not survive changing client.

### What the clocks are

`issued_at` is the author's, signed by the author. `received` is the exchange's
observation of arrival, signed by nobody. `serial` orders one feed and says
nothing across two.

**Retention and `expires_after` are measured from `received` and never from
`issued_at`.** This is the first artifact in this stack whose only in-log
timestamp belongs to the author, and the rule is not optional: measured from
`issued_at`, an author sets it far ahead and the post never prunes, or far
behind and it vanishes on arrival.

A client assembling a timeline from several feeds MUST order by the lesser of
`issued_at` and `received`, and MUST NOT allow a post to sort above one that
arrived later. It MAY display `issued_at` as the author's claimed time, marked
as a claim where it differs materially from `received`. **Without the clamp an
author sets `issued_at` far in the future and pins themselves to the top of
every reader's timeline for as long as they like.** Back-dating remains possible
and buries the post, which is self-defeating.

**There is no global order across feeds and there cannot be.** A timeline is one
reader's merge of several independent logs by clocks nobody shares; two readers
may order the same two posts differently and neither is wrong. SIP-16 says the
same of its own `posted`, "the one field here that nobody signs".

### What an exchange does not do to a timeline

An exchange MUST NOT rank, filter, score or re-order what a reader sees.

**It also cannot**, and that is worth more than the rule. Ranking requires
knowing what a reader follows and having an opinion about the bodies. The
exchange holds no follow list, by §The follow list, and does not parse a body,
by §What a body may be. The property is enforced by what the exchange is unable
to do rather than by what it promises not to.

A client assembles its own timeline, in reverse chronological order by the clamp
above.

### Learning that a feed moved

A client holding a SIP-30 stream MAY name the accounts it is watching. The
exchange keeps that list **for the life of the stream, in memory, and forgets it
when the stream closes**, and emits an event when a watched feed's `newest`
advances. The list is never stored and never outlives the connection.

**There is no SIP-45 wake for a feed, and there cannot be one without storing
the follow list.** A wake is for "an event that would have gone on a stream the
device is not holding", and with nothing stored there is no such event. So:

> A device holding a stream learns of a post at once. A device that cannot hold
> one — a phone, asleep — learns of a post when it next connects, woken for
> something else or on whatever interval its platform grants. **A feed is a
> poll-latency medium on a phone**, and no arrangement short of handing the
> exchange the reading graph changes that.

This is the one place the rejected channel design was better: membership bought
wakes for free, because the exchange already held the list. The trade was made
deliberately and in that direction.

### Nothing comes in

Only the author appends. There is no reaction, no reply, no comment and no
inbound path of any kind.

A reader responds in their own feed, by quoting (SIP-89), or privately, by
direct message (SIP-16). The quoted author is not notified, and there is no
search, so a quote is invisible to its subject unless somebody tells them.

**That is both the whole of the abuse-resistance story and the reason this is a
publishing primitive rather than a social network.** The inbound path is SIP-5
and SIP-16, where it already exists and where it is already bounded.

### Withdrawal

`Withdraw` removes a post's body, sets `body_len` to 0 and **retains `body_hash`
and `sig`**. This is SIP-16's rule and SIP-31 gives the reason: the signature
commits to the hash rather than the bytes "precisely so that a tombstone still
verifies with its body gone [...] An exchange that cleared them would make every
deleted message read as a forgery."

An `expires_after` expiry in the middle of the log leaves a tombstone too.
**This inverts SIP-16, which prunes without one**, and the inversion has a
reason. SIP-16 refuses a pruning tombstone because "a shadow index of who said
something and when, long after the something is gone, is a worse disclosure than
the gap it fills". Here the index is already public: it is the author's own
signed serials, served to anybody. The tombstone discloses nothing new, and the
dense serial space it preserves is worth more than the objection costs.

Eviction from the oldest end advances `oldest` and leaves nothing, which is what
stops tombstones accumulating without bound.

A client deleting a post SHOULD issue both a `Withdraw` and a SIP-19 `Redact`
body at a higher serial naming the withdrawn one, and MUST detach any attachment
(SIP-18). A reader then reads three states:

- **withdrawn** — a tombstone with a `Redact` from this account behind it;
- **removed** — a tombstone with nothing behind it, which is the exchange's own
  act, and SIP-32 requires a reader be able to see that rather than have it pass
  as an ordinary deletion;
- not withdrawn.

**A feed's corroboration is stronger than a channel's.** In a channel a `Redact`
may come from the author or from an admin, so a reader must evaluate authority
against a membership list it may not hold, and an uncorroborated tombstone may
merely be one whose corroborating entry was pruned. A feed has exactly one
authorised party and prunes only from the oldest end, so the absence of
corroboration means what it says.

**Withdrawal does not take anything back, and a client MUST say so where a post
is composed rather than in a settings screen.** SIP-19 already says redaction is
a request and not erasure. For a feed it is weaker by orders of magnitude, and
structurally rather than behaviourally: a channel's audience is at most 256
members holding ciphertext, and a feed's is everybody who asked, each holding a
plaintext artifact that verifies with no reference to any exchange.

### Blocking

SIP-21's block gains a fourth enforcement point beside the three it already
names. A `/feed/read`, a `/feed/head`, or a `/feed/since` row naming an account
that has blocked the caller answers `found: 0`, or `state: 0x02`,
indistinguishably from absent — exactly as SIP-21's third rule answers a profile
`Get`.

SIP-21's guarantee is preserved, and so is its limit: nothing *states* the
block, and it "is not undetectable, and MUST NOT be described as though it
were." A block on a feed is weaker than a block on a direct message, because the
artifact travels: a blocked reader may hold a copy already, may read one at a
mirror, and may read through a proxy that is not this exchange.

### Where a feed lives, and moving

A feed lives at its account's home (SIP-59), and a reader finds it by asking
`/account/home`, which SIP-59 already answers "to any identified caller".

**A feed moves with the account, unlike a channel.** SIP-59 says "Its channels
live wherever they were created", and the reason is that a channel has an origin
that *orders* it — an authority that cannot be in two places. A feed has no
ordering authority at all: the serial is the author's and signed. Nothing holds
it where it was.

The new home collects it as SIP-59 §What follows the home already collects mail,
backup and wakes:

```
PullFeed | type: u8 = 0x14 | account[32] | since: u64 |
Feed     | now: u64 | oldest: u64 | newest: u64 | retention_secs: u32
         | max_posts: u32 | seams: u8 | seams × (at: u64 || from[32])
         | count: u16 | count × Stored |
TookFeed | type: u8 = 0x15 | account[32] | through: u64 |
```

`POST /peer/feed` and `/peer/feed/took`, gated exactly as `/peer/mailbox` is: a
peer that is the recorded home of `account` by the account's own signed Move. An
operator's `for` list does not confer a feed.

**After the handover the former home MUST stop serving the feed and MUST answer
`moved`**, joining SIP-59's list of handed-off routes. SIP-59 otherwise lets a
former home go on serving what it holds until it expires, and for a feed that is
equivocation by construction: the author appends at the new home, the old home
serves a shorter log, and a reader there sees a feed that stopped. **A feed is
the one thing in SIP-59 that must not be served in two places**, and the reason
is that its whole value is being the single authoritative sequence.

An author may nonetheless publish the same posts to several exchanges. The bytes
are identical and the serial space is the author's, so the copies agree by
construction unless the author equivocates deliberately. **Because the exchange
assigns nothing, a feed is trivially multi-homed** — which SIP-43 needed a whole
document to achieve for a channel. There is no discovery for it, since
`/account/home` names one home; it is a property, not a mechanism.

### Succession

On a SIP-44 succession or handover the exchange re-keys the feed to the
successor. The stored posts are unchanged byte for byte, `serial` and `prev`
continue, and the feed is thereafter served at the successor's key. **The
exchange appends nothing**: only the author's devices may sign a post, and the
old key's devices are gone.

The seam is recorded in the feed's metadata rather than in the log, and
`Headed` carries it, newest last.

A verifier reading a post whose `account` is not the feed's current owner MUST
find that account among the seams, MUST obtain the succession for that step
(SIP-44), MUST verify the will under the predecessor, and MUST then perform
SIP-31's step 2 against the **predecessor**. A post whose `account` is neither
the owner nor a seamed predecessor is forged and MUST be presented as such.

**The chain is not broken at a seam**, and an implementation must not try to
restart it: `prev` links signing inputs, and a signing input references no
credential. Only attribution needs the extra hop.

SIP-44's checks on a successor — that it has no registered devices and no names
of its own — gain **and no feed of its own**, for the reason SIP-44 added
memberships to that list: "a key that sits in a channel is an account already,
which the first draft's checks did not say."

A `Since` row for a succeeded account answers `state: 0x06` with the successor's
key, so a client follows on in one round trip.

### Attachments

A feed is an attach target, and SIP-18 gains three type bytes rather than a
field, on SIP-60's precedent where `ListDevicesFrom` sits beside `ListDevices`
at a new byte and the route dispatches:

```
BeginFeed  | type: u8 = 0x0a | account[32] | size: u64 | chunks: u32
           | expires_after: u32 |
AttachFeed | type: u8 = 0x0b | account[32] | blob[32] | expires_after: u32 |
DetachFeed | type: u8 = 0x0c | account[32] | blob[32] |
```

The caller MUST be a device of `account`. Overloading SIP-18's existing
`channel[32]` with an account key is **forbidden**: both are 32 opaque bytes, an
exchange could not tell which was meant, and the two spaces are related, since a
direct message's channel identifier is derived from two account keys.

A blob attached to a feed is served by `Get` to any caller the feed's `Read`
would be served to. Retention is SIP-18's formula with the feed's window
substituted for the channel's.

**SIP-18's no-widening refusal extends to feeds, and more sharply.** SIP-18
refuses an `Attach` naming a public channel for a blob any private channel
holds, because `Detach` answers to an admin of the channel holding the
attachment and "one member could publish another's file, and nobody could take
it back". A feed is worse on that axis: a public channel has admins and a feed
has none, so `DetachFeed` answers to the author's devices alone. An `AttachFeed`
— and the first attachment implied by a `BeginFeed` — MUST be refused for a blob
any private channel holds, and the exchange SHOULD say why rather than answering
as though the blob were absent.

A client appending a post with `expires_after` MUST attach its blobs with an
`expires_after` no greater. The exchange cannot check this, which puts it in the
same family as SIP-18's own pairing of a redaction with a detach.

### What bounds a feed

Every account has a feed, and under SIP-3 an identity is free to mint. A profile
is bounded by construction at a few kilobytes; a log with attachments is not. So
the bound is a relationship rather than a number:

> An exchange MUST NOT create a feed for an account that has not appended to it,
> and SHOULD serve `/feed/append` only to an account it already serves for
> something else — one with a registered device (SIP-22), a name (SIP-38), a
> present membership in a channel it orders, or a signed Move naming it as home
> (SIP-59). An exchange with open registration MAY serve any identity, MUST then
> apply SIP-56's limits, and SHOULD set a ceiling across all the feeds it holds.

That disjunction is not invented: it is the predicate SIP-59's `/account/home`
already uses to decide whether an account is known here.

Appending past `MAX_POSTS` or `MAX_FEED_BYTES` evicts from the oldest end and
advances `oldest` rather than refusing, following SIP-16's `max_entries`. **A
feed its author must garden is a feed that goes quiet.**

`Set` replaces the whole record — SIP-21's rule, "there is no partial update" —
and is transport-authenticated and unsigned, by SIP-32's own test: pruning is
the exchange's act, nothing a reader repeats depends on the policy, and a signed
policy would be a portable statement whose enforcement is still entirely the
exchange's.

### Limits

| limit | value |
|---|---|
| Post body | 32 KiB |
| `MAX_POSTS` per feed | 10 000 |
| `MAX_FEED_BYTES` | 16 MiB |
| Blob bytes per feed | 256 MiB |
| `MAX_PAGE` | 64 |
| `MAX_PAGE_BYTES` | 512 KiB |
| `MAX_SINCE` | 512 |
| `MAX_WATCH` per stream | 512 |
| `MAX_SEAMS` | 8 |
| Minimum retention | 1 hour |
| Default retention | 1 year |
| Maximum retention | 1 year |
| `FEED_TTL` | 60 s |
| `feed_appends` (SIP-56, per account) | 60 per hour |
| `feed_rows` (SIP-56, per account) | 20 000 per hour |
| `feed_sets` (SIP-56, per account) | 32 per hour |

SIP-16's limits are not reused where the shape differs. `MAX_POSTS` is lower
than a channel's 50 000 entries and `MAX_FEED_BYTES` far lower than its 128 MiB
because a channel is created deliberately and capped at 256 per identity, while
every account has a feed. The default retention is **a year** rather than
SIP-16's thirty days, because a feed is an archive and one that evaporated in a
month would be worse than none.

## Rationale

**Why not a channel.** Covered in Motivation, and the count is the argument:
fifteen rows of SIP-16 §Who may call what needing a different answer, plus four
structural exceptions. Each would have been defensible alone. Together they say
the shape is wrong.

**Why the account key is the whole identifier.** A derived identifier — a hash
over the account, as a direct message's is over its two — would have worked and
needed an anti-squatting rule, because SIP-16's `Create` is open to any identity
and somebody would create your feed's identifier first. With no identifier at
all the problem does not exist: nothing is created, and a feed is whatever its
account has signed.

**Why the chain is per feed.** Set out in §One chain. The short form: SIP-31's
reason for per-device chains is an exchange that numbers, and this exchange does
not number.

**Why a hole is evidence here and not in SIP-31.** SIP-31 serves channels, where
a reader may lawfully hold less than the whole log. A feed admits nobody and so
withholds nothing, which is what lets the serial space be dense and a gap mean
something.

**Why a tombstone where SIP-16 refuses one.** SIP-16's shadow index is a list of
who spoke and when, surviving the words. A feed's list of who spoke and when is
the feed, and it is public already.

**Why there is no reaction.** A reaction would have to land in the author's log,
which only the author may append to; or in the reactor's, where nobody can count
it, since no party can enumerate the feeds that hold one. A count assembled from
the feeds a client happens to read is a sample rendered as a total. Making the
exchange accept reaction-only entries from non-authors would require it to parse
a SIP-19 body to decide what to store, which SIP-19 forbids in as many words.

**What was considered and rejected.**

- *A followers-only feed.* Sealing needs an epoch, a channel key, an envelope
  per recipient, and a rotation on every departure — every one of them a
  membership mechanism. A followers-only feed is a public channel with one
  poster, and SIP-16 already builds that. The moment an author wants a bounded
  audience they want the thing that has one. Nothing on the wire stops an author
  sealing a body themselves, because the exchange never parses one; that
  capability exists with no key distribution and no support, and this document
  says so rather than pretending it is forbidden.
- *A withhold flag, as SIP-21 has.* It cannot survive proxying: a reader at
  exchange A is seen by exchange B as A, so B either over-blocks every reader at
  A or takes A's word about a third party's identity, which this stack grants
  nowhere. A visibility bit a person relies on and that fails silently at a
  domain boundary is worse than no bit.
- *A stored follow list at the exchange, so a phone could be woken.* It is the
  reading graph, in the clear and durable, and broader than the membership graph
  SIP-16 already calls the largest disclosure in the stack. SIP-21 refused a
  per-account visibility list on the same ground: it would be "an address book
  at the exchange, which is a much larger disclosure than the profile it
  protects."
- *Exchange-assembled timelines.* They need the follow list, and so are refused
  with it. The consequence is recorded in §What an exchange does not do.
- *A feed directory.* SIP-16 already wrote this argument against its welcome
  channel: "A channel that does not count towards knowing somebody must not be
  the thing that says who everybody is." One feed per account, listed with a
  last-activity time and mirrored to every peer every sixty seconds, is a
  timestamped census of every active account.
- *Open third-party mirroring, as SIP-27 allows for attestations.* An
  attestation survives open lodgement because it is a couple of hundred bytes
  capped at sixty-four per subject. A feed is a log with attachments, and an open
  mirror route lets a stranger spend an exchange's disk on an account that never
  asked to live there. Mirroring is a configured arrangement between operators.

## Compatibility

**Purely additive, and nothing existing changes.** Six new routes under a new
path, three new SIP-18 type bytes beside the existing ones, one new SIP-48
segment kind, one optional field on a SIP-30 stream request, and three rows in
SIP-56's table. No existing message gains or loses a field.

An exchange without feeds answers the new routes as it answers any unknown path.
A client MUST treat that as "this exchange does not serve feeds" and MUST NOT
take it for an empty feed: the two are different facts, and an account whose home
does not serve feeds is reachable for everything else.

An older client shown a feed post it cannot fetch is unaffected, since nothing
in a channel references a feed. A SIP-89 citation in a channel message is an
unknown part and is skipped by `len`, which is SIP-19's rule.

**Both ends need not move together.** An exchange may serve feeds to clients
that never ask. A client may follow feeds at exchanges that serve them while the
client's own home does not, by reading those homes directly — at the cost
described in §Where a feed lives.

## Security considerations

**The reading graph is the thing this design protects, and it protects it by not
holding it.** The exchange never learns what a reader follows, because no route
tells it and nothing stores it. What it does learn is every account a caller
asked about in a `Since`, for as long as it takes to answer — which is the same
disclosure a client would make by polling each feed separately, and for a
shorter time. A client's own home, which proxies for it, therefore sees the
whole list on every poll; an author's home sees only that somebody asked. **A
reader who does not want their home to know what they read must read elsewhere,
and this document gives them no better answer.**

**A feed is public and nothing makes it less so.** There is no audience control,
no withhold flag and no followers-only mode. An author who wants a bounded
readership wants a channel. A client MUST make this plain where a feed is
composed, because a person who has withheld their SIP-21 profile will otherwise
assume their feed is withheld on the same terms, and it is not.

**Every post is permanent in practice.** It is a self-contained artifact that
verifies without reference to any exchange, held by everybody who read it.
Withdrawal removes it from this exchange and from nowhere else, and this
document makes no claim beyond that. SIP-27's sentence is the honest one: nothing
published "should be treated as retractable, whatever the revocation mechanism
says."

**A `Read` discloses when an account last posted**, to anybody holding its key.
Every other presence surface in this stack has a reach rule — SIP-4's beacon has
a withhold flag, SIP-21's profile has one, SIP-30's publication event is gated to
accounts sharing a channel — and a feed has none, because a feed exists to be
read by strangers. Combined with SIP-38's `Resolve` and `Reverse`, both public to
any identified caller, an enumeration of names yields an activity census across
a federation. That is a consequence of publishing, and an author who does not
want it should not publish.

**A key thief inherits a reputation.** SIP-44 already says a stolen account key
is the end of the account. For a feed it is also the capture of everything the
account ever published, under a chain that stays unbroken across the seam. The
mitigation is a client rule and therefore the one most likely to ship missing:
**a client MUST render a seam as a visible event in the timeline and MUST NOT
present a succeeded feed as continuous.**

**Backdating is bounded only by an unsigned field.** `received` is the exchange's
word and nothing signs it, so a reader trusting a post's time is trusting the
exchange that served it. SIP-34 solved the equivalent problem for channels by
having the exchange sign position and time; a feed has no equivalent and does not
need one until somebody treats a post's time as a fact. An implementation MUST
NOT present `issued_at` as established.

**There is no moderation path and the operator's remedy is the door.** SIP-56
routes a report to a channel's admins, and a feed's only admin is its author.
SIP-56 declined operator-level action deliberately — "this document is about what
happens inside a room among people who are all admitted" — and a feed has no
inside. **An exchange serving feeds to the open internet has no in-band remedy
against an abusive author short of SIP-8 and SIP-24**, and an operator who needs
one should run a deployment where admission is controlled.

**An exchange can deny but cannot forge.** It may refuse to serve a feed, serve a
short one, or stop serving one that moved. It cannot alter a post, insert one, or
reorder two, because the order is `serial` and `prev` and both are signed. A hole
is visible, by §A dense serial space. This is SIP-28's boundary and SIP-28's
phrasing: "It can deny. This is a denial of service, not a compromise."

**Nothing here is deniable.** A post is signed, attributable and carried away by
strangers. That was already true of a public channel and is more true here.

## Reference implementation

None yet.

Where it would attach. `sqex-proto` gains a `feed` module beside `profile` and
`channel`, carrying the wire types, the context string and the signing input;
the chain arithmetic belongs beside SIP-31's in whatever module already computes
an entry input, so that the two cannot drift. `sqexd` gains a `feed` module with
the six routes and the per-account store, and `/peer/feed` beside the existing
`/peer/mailbox` in whatever serves SIP-59's handover. `sqex-chat` gains publish
and read on the client, the follow list in its store beside `contact`, and the
SIP-48 segment kind in its backup writer. No client-side key handling is needed
anywhere, which is the first thing in this stack to carry a signature and need
none.

What an implementation must settle, rather than this document arguing it.

- **Whether the re-sign loop converges under contention.** Two of an author's
  devices appending at once both read the same head and one is refused; the
  refused one re-signs at the higher serial. Measure it with two devices posting
  continuously and find out whether it starves one of them. If it does, the
  answer is a client-side lease, not a change to the wire.
- **What a `Since` costs an exchange at scale.** Five hundred rows per poll per
  client, nearly all answering "unchanged", is tens of thousands of lookups a
  second on a modest deployment. An implementation almost certainly needs
  `newest` in an index it can answer a row from without touching the log; find
  out whether that is true before shipping one that scans.
- **Whether a client can hold the offline rule.** §One chain forbids serving or
  syncing a post the exchange has not accepted, and a client that queues posts
  on a plane will want to. Find out whether holding them unsigned is workable in
  a real client, and if it is not, say so here rather than relaxing the rule
  quietly.
- **Whether the two sequence spaces can be told apart in one codebase.** SIP-19's
  `Edit`, `Redact` and `Reply` all carry a `target: u64` which is a SIP-16 `seq`
  in a channel and a feed serial here. A client rendering both in one view will
  mis-resolve one silently. Build the test that catches it before building the
  feature.
