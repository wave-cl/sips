## Abstract

A peer that a whitelisted exchange will not serve may ask to be admitted,
presenting a SIP-20 credential showing which account vouches for it. The request
is queued and nothing else happens: an administrator reviews it and approves or
denies it with a SIP-10 signed transaction, and only approval puts the key on
the whitelist. **The exchange answers every request identically**, whatever it
decides to do with it, so asking discloses nothing — not whether the credential
verified, not whether the account is known, not whether anything was queued.

## Motivation

A managed whitelist is SIP-2's closed-set match: the exchange holds a set of
Ed25519 keys, derives each forward to X25519, and compares against the key MAC1
verified on the connection. It works and it has one awkward property — there is
no way in. A key that is not already on the list cannot be put there by anything
the peer does, which is correct for a closed set and impractical the moment a
person acquires a second device.

Chat makes that acute. Under SIP-20 and SIP-22 a person is an account and each
of their clients is a separate key, so a deployment of twenty people with three
devices each is sixty whitelist entries, churning whenever somebody replaces a
phone. Every one of those additions is an administrative act performed out of
band, for a device whose right to be there is already attested by a credential
the account signed.

The obvious shortcut is to admit any device whose credential names a whitelisted
account, automatically. That was rejected, and the reason is the more
interesting half of this SIP. It would move the decision from the operator to
whoever holds an account key, so an account admitted once could enrol devices
indefinitely without anyone reviewing it — and the whole point of a whitelist is
that somebody decided. **A credential is evidence, not authority.** It tells an
administrator who is asking; it does not answer.

So the request is separated from the decision. The peer supplies evidence; an
administrator supplies authority; the two meet in a queue.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian.

### Requesting

```
Request | type: u8 = 0x01 | credential | label_len: u8 | label |
Ack     | now: u64 |
```

`POST /admission/request`. `credential` is a SIP-20 credential naming the
requesting device as its `delegate`. `label` is UTF-8 of at most `MAX_LABEL`
bytes, offered for an administrator to read.

Nothing in the request is signed by the requester, and nothing needs to be. The
connection already proves possession of the device key — MAC1 verified it and
SIP-2 exposes it — and the credential already proves the account vouched for
that key. A third signature would authenticate nothing that is not authenticated.

The exchange MUST check that the credential's `delegate` equals the caller's
verified transport key. A request whose credential names some other device is
somebody forwarding a credential they found, and it is discarded.

### The reply is always the same

**An exchange MUST return `Ack` to every well-formed request, with no field that
varies with its decision.** The same answer is returned when the credential
verifies and when it does not; when the account is whitelisted and when it has
never been seen; when the request is queued, when it is dropped because the
queue is full, when the device is already admitted, and when an identical
request is already pending.

This is the rule the rest of the SIP exists to protect, and it is why admission
is a request rather than a negotiation. The endpoint has to be reachable by a
peer the exchange will not otherwise serve — that is what it is for — so it is
the one place a refused caller gets a real answer. If that answer varied, it
would be an oracle: submit a credential naming an account and learn from the
response whether that account is admitted here. The exchange would be answering
a question the caller has not earned the right to ask.

The corpus is consistent about this and this SIP follows it. SIP-4 reports a
withheld beacon identically to one that does not exist. SIP-12 makes waiting,
never-seen and nonexistent a single answer so that opening a session cannot
probe for an identity. SIP-5 answers `unknown` to everyone but the sender.

A requester learns the outcome the only way it should: by trying a protected
endpoint later and seeing whether it works.

**This does not weaken sQUIC's silent server**, and it is worth being precise
about why. Silence is MAC1's property, at the transport layer: a peer that does
not hold the exchange's public key cannot produce a valid Initial and receives
nothing at all. Every request here arrives on a connection that already passed
that gate. What this section protects is a different and weaker property — that
a peer which got through the door learns nothing from asking.

### Deciding

Three administrative operations, carried in a SIP-10 transaction and subject to
its verification in full:

| Tag | Operation |
|---|---|
| 0x09 | `AdmissionList` — read pending requests |
| 0x0a | `AdmissionApprove { device: PubKey, label: Option<String> }` |
| 0x0b | `AdmissionDeny(PubKey)` |

`AdmissionList` returns, for each pending request: the device key, the account
key taken from its credential, the credential's `not_after`, the label, when the
request was first seen, and **how many devices of that account are already
admitted**. The last is the fact an administrator actually decides on — a third
device of an account admitted twice before is a different proposition from the
first request anybody has ever made for an unknown account.

`AdmissionApprove` adds `device` to the whitelist, recording as provenance the
approving administrator, the account the credential named, and the time. The
`label` an administrator supplies is their own; the requester's label is
evidence and is not carried over unless the administrator repeats it.

`AdmissionDeny` removes the request and records the denial, so the same device
does not reappear in the queue on its next attempt. A denial is not permanent:
an administrator may approve the device later, and the record expires with the
credential that was denied.

**Administrator means an exchange administrator** — an identity on the list
SIP-10 transactions are checked against. It does not mean a channel admin
(SIP-16), which is a role any user acquires by creating a room. Conflating them
would let anybody who made a chat room grant transport access to the server.

### Requests are not authority, and neither is a credential

An approved device is on the whitelist as a key, and stays there until an
administrator removes it. In particular:

- **SIP-22 revocation does not remove a whitelist entry.** The account revokes
  its device; the operator controls transport access; these are different
  authorities and neither implies the other. A revoked device that is still
  whitelisted can still connect, and resolves to no account, so it is a member
  of nothing and can read nothing — but it is still a peer the exchange serves.
  An operator SHOULD remove it, and an implementation SHOULD surface revocations
  of admitted devices so that they can.
- **A credential expiring does not remove a whitelist entry either.** The
  credential was evidence at the moment of the decision. If admission is meant
  to lapse with it, an administrator must say so by removing the key.

### Limits

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| Pending requests | 64 |
| Pending requests per account | 8 |
| Requests accepted per connection per hour | 4 |
| `MAX_LABEL` | 64 bytes |
| Denial records retained | until the credential's `not_after` |

Every one of these is enforced silently: an overrun changes what the exchange
stores and never what it answers.

### When a whitelist is the right tool

A whitelist is a closed-set mechanism and the chat services are open by
construction — SIP-3 exists precisely so an exchange can name callers it never
registered, and SIP-16's public channels are joinable by anyone. Running them
behind a whitelist makes sense for a private deployment with a fixed roster and
makes no sense for a public one, where it amounts to an administrator approving
the general public one device at a time.

An exchange SHOULD therefore make enforcement per-route rather than global, so
that an operator can run an open beacon and a closed chat, or the reverse, on
one deployment. This SIP does not specify which routes are protected; that is an
operator's policy, and SIP-16 notes only that its routes are the kind of thing a
whitelist would cover.

## Rationale

**Why not admit a credentialled device automatically.** It reads as the obvious
simplification and it moves the decision to the wrong party. An operator running
a closed deployment has decided which *people* may use it; automatic admission
would let any admitted person enrol arbitrarily many keys, and the operator's
list would stop describing what they had agreed to. Requiring approval keeps the
whitelist a record of decisions somebody made, which is the only reason to have
one.

**Why the requester signs nothing.** The connection proves the device key and
the credential proves the account's endorsement of it. A request signature would
prove possession of the same key MAC1 already proved. It would also be one more
thing to get wrong, and SIP-1 was written because a signature scheme that had
never worked went unnoticed for months.

**Why the queue rather than a channel to the operator.** An out-of-band flow —
mail the operator your key — needs no protocol and is what deployments do today.
It also loses the two facts that make the decision easy: that the account
vouching for the device is one the exchange already admits, and how many of its
devices are already in. The queue is worth its small wire precisely because it
carries evidence the operator would otherwise have to reconstruct.

**Why denials are remembered.** Without a record, a denied device requests again
on its next connection and the queue refills with decisions already taken.
Expiring the record with the credential keeps it from becoming a permanent
blocklist that nobody maintains.

**Why the reply carries `now`.** It is the only field, and it is there for the
reason SIP-4 gives: a client with a wrong clock has something to notice it
against. It is identical for every caller and discloses nothing.

## Compatibility

Purely additive. One new route, three new SIP-10 operation tags in a namespace
where 0x09 onward were unused, and no change to any existing wire format. An
exchange without this SIP answers 404 and a client without it never asks.

Existing whitelist behaviour is unchanged: `WhitelistAdd` and `WhitelistRemove`
still work exactly as before, and an operator who prefers to add keys directly
never has to look at the queue.

## Security considerations

**The label is text chosen by the requester and shown to an administrator at the
moment of a security decision.** That is the most dangerous field in this SIP. A
requester may call themselves anything, including something that resembles
another account's device. The verifiable fact is the account key in the
credential, and an interface **MUST** display that and MUST NOT let a label
stand in for it. SIP-10's context binding exists for the same reason and should
be read alongside this: what the administrator approves must correspond to what
executes.

**The queue is fillable by anyone holding the exchange's public key.** The caps
bound it and the constant reply means an attacker cannot tell whether they are
succeeding, but they can still displace genuine requests. Per-account and
per-connection limits make that expensive rather than impossible. An operator
seeing a full queue of unfamiliar accounts is being told something, and that is
the intended failure mode.

**Approval is a durable grant that outlives its evidence.** The credential
expires and the whitelist entry does not; the account may revoke the device and
the whitelist entry does not. Both are stated in the Specification because both
are easy to assume the other way, and an operator who believes admission tracks
the credential will hold a whitelist that quietly stops meaning what they think.

**A request tells the exchange that a device claims an account.** It learns the
same thing at SIP-22 registration, so the marginal disclosure is small — but a
request is made by a peer that has *not* been admitted, so an exchange
accumulates claims from peers it never serves. Those records should be capped
and expired, which the limits do.

**Nothing here authenticates the account to the requester.** A peer requesting
admission has no way to know it is talking to the exchange it intends, beyond
having pinned the server key to connect at all — which is the same guarantee
every other operation in this stack rests on, and is why the server key must be
obtained out of band rather than discovered.
