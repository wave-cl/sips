## Abstract

A SIP-13 room spans two or more peered exchanges: a member joins the room
at its own exchange and asks it to **share** the room with a named peer,
the exchanges exchange their rosters over the SIP-39 relay link, and every
member sees every other with the exchange each is at. A pair on different
exchanges talks through a SIP-39 bridge that neither side rings for,
because both already know the other from the roster. Nothing about the
room's secret, its proofs, or its media changes: the exchanges relay a
list and bytes they cannot read, one hop further.

## Motivation

SIP-43 put a channel's members on more than one exchange. SIP-36 starts a
call in a channel by posting an invitation carrying a SIP-13 room secret,
and SIP-13 says a room is a roster kept at *an* exchange -- so a member who
reads the channel through a copy at squic.org, and cannot reach
trunk.exchange's transport at all under SIP-8, opens the invitation, holds
the secret, and has nowhere to present it. The call rings on one exchange
and half the channel cannot answer.

SIP-39 already carries a call between two exchanges, but for one pair, with
a ring in the middle: the callee's exchange rings an account, and a device
answering is what makes the session. A room is not that. Every member is
already present and already knows every other; what is missing is that a
member at one exchange cannot see, or be seen by, a member at another. This
document adds the seeing, and reuses SIP-39's bridge for the talking with
the ring taken out.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. Every request is bound to the
transport identity (SIP-3), which for a room member is a device. Two
exchanges peered under SIP-39's allowlist are **relay peers**.

### Joining with a share

```
JoinShared | type: u8 = 0x04 | handle[32] | proof[32] | count: u8 | count × exchange[32] |
Homed      | now: u64 | count: u16 | count × (identity[32] || proof[32] || home[32]) |
```

`POST /room/join` with type `0x04` is SIP-13's `Join` that also names the
relay peers, by SIP-9 key, the member wants the room shared with. It
answers `Homed`: SIP-13's roster with each member's **home** -- the key of
the exchange the member joined at, or 32 zero bytes for this one. Sorted by
identity as SIP-13 requires; the caller is left out.

A member at a channel's copy names the channel's origin (SIP-43's
`/channel/home` says which). A member at the origin names nobody: sharing
is reciprocal, below. An exchange MUST accept a
plain `Join` (type `0x01`) for a shared room, answering SIP-13's `Roster`
with the local members only -- a client that asked the old way gets the
old answer. A share naming an exchange that is not a relay peer is
refused (403) and nothing is joined.

### Sharing on the relay link

```
RoomShare | 0x06 | handle[32] | count: u8 | count × (identity[32] || proof[32] || home[32]) |
```

An exchange **shares** a room with a relay peer once a local member has
named that peer in a `JoinShared`, or once that peer has shared the room
with it -- whichever comes first, and thereafter for as long as the room
has members at either. A share is the exchange's full view of the room:
its local members, with `home` zero, and every member it has learned from
other peers, with the home it learned them under -- except members whose
home is the peer being told, who know their own. Sent whenever that view
changes and at least every `SHARE_SECS`; a `count` of 0 is the room having
emptied here.

What arrives is **soft state**, as SIP-13's own membership is: an exchange
keeps a peer's shared members until the next share from that peer replaces
them, until `TTL_SECS` pass without one, or until the link drops -- and
then forgets them. It MUST NOT verify proofs, having no secret to verify
with, and MUST relay them unaltered. It MUST refuse a share naming more
than `MAX_MEMBERS` and MUST NOT count shared members against its own
`MAX_MEMBERS`, which bounds what *it* admits; what a peer admits is that
peer's.

The exchange learns the handle, the member identities, and the proofs,
which it already learned from its own members; it learns the same about the
peer's. It does not learn the secret, and a proof is an HMAC under it.

### Pairs across exchanges

SIP-13 has every member open a SIP-12 session toward every other, and a
session needs both to name each other. For two members at the same
exchange that is unchanged. For two at different exchanges, the **lower**
identity (by bytes) places the bridge and the **higher** answers it:

- The lower sends its exchange SIP-39's `CallOpen` with target
  `key@home`, where `key` is the higher's identity in base58 and `home`
  is the higher's home **as the exchange key in base58**. An exchange
  reading a base58 key where SIP-39 expects a domain resolves it to the
  relay link it holds with that peer, and refuses -- as SIP-39 refuses an
  exchange not on its list -- if it holds none. No discovery is needed:
  the exchanges are already talking.
- The higher sends its own exchange an ordinary SIP-12 `Open` naming the
  lower, exactly as it would for a local member.

A target whose domain is an exchange key names a *device*, not an
account, and the lower's exchange sends the higher's a new control on the
relay link in place of SIP-39's `Invite`:

```
InviteDevice | 0x07 | bridge: [16] | caller: [32] | caller_eph: [32] | device: [32] |
```

The receiving exchange **rings nobody** for it. If `device` has an `Open`
naming `caller` outstanding, it answers `Accept` at once; if not, it
records the bridge and answers `Ringing`, and the `Open` from `device`
naming `caller`, when it comes, completes the bridge as SIP-39's callee
`Open` does. Only that device's `Open` matches. SIP-39's `Invite`, and
everything it rings, is untouched.

Either order therefore works: whichever of the two asks first waits for
the other, which is SIP-12's rule as it always was.

A member whose home is not a relay peer of this member's exchange is
present, and unreachable: the `CallOpen` is refused. A client MUST show the
member as in the call and not heard, rather than as absent. The mesh is
what it was: 2(N−1) streams per member, and `MAX_MEMBERS` counts every
member wherever they are.

### Ringing at a copy

A call starts as SIP-36 says: an invitation posted in the channel, which
SIP-43 carries to the origin and SIP-35 to every copy. SIP-36 derives its
`Ringing` event from a call-state *signal*, and signals are ephemeral and
stay at the exchange they were sent to: they do not cross homes, and this
document does not make them.

**A member at a copy is rung by the invitation itself.** A client MUST
present an incoming call on reading an invitation entry younger than its
`ring_secs`, whether or not a `Ringing` event was heard, and MUST stop on
its own accept or decline, on a SIP-36 record entry for the invitation
(which crosses, being an entry), or at `ring_secs`. SIP-36's per-device
signals (accepted, declined, busy) keep doing their work among devices at
one exchange and are advisory everywhere else: **presence in the room is
the answer**, and the roster says who is in the call.

### Limits

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| `SHARE_SECS` | 10 |
| Shared members per `RoomShare` | `MAX_MEMBERS` (8) |
| Exchanges named in one `JoinShared` | 4 |
| Remote membership without a share | `TTL_SECS` (30) |

## Rationale

**Why share rosters and not media.** Media already crosses: SIP-39 relays
a session's datagrams between two exchanges without opening them, and a
room's media is sessions. Only the roster was stuck at one exchange, and
a roster is a small list the exchange holds in memory anyway.

**Why the member names the peer.** Because an exchange that shared every
room with every peer would hand each peer a list of every call on the
exchange, with who is in it. The member knows which channel the call is
in and where the channel lives; the exchange does not, and should not
learn it from the room. The origin never has to name anybody because a
copy's member names the origin, and sharing is reciprocal from then on.

**Why the lower key places the bridge.** SIP-12 needs both to open toward
each other, and SIP-39 needs one side to say which exchange the other is
at. One rule settles who says it, and byte order is a rule with nothing to
negotiate. The higher's `Open` is what it would have sent for a local
member; only the lower does anything new.

**Why an exchange key can stand for a domain.** A `CallOpen` target names
where to find the callee's exchange; SIP-39 discovers a domain to get a
key and an address. Here the key is known and the link is up. A client
that had to turn a home key back into a domain would need a directory it
may not have (SIP-46 lists domains the operator chose to record), for a
lookup the exchange does not need to make.

**Why the invitation rings.** The `Ringing` event exists so a client that
quietened its signal polling still hears a ring at once; a member at a
copy has no signal from the origin to poll. The entry is the ring: it is
signed, sealed, and carries `ring_secs`, and SIP-47 already rings a phone
from it. Carrying signals between homes would be a second replication
protocol for state SIP-16 says is worth nothing durable.

**What was rejected.** A room at the origin that copy members dial by
SIP-39 one by one -- each pair a separate cross-exchange call, rung and
answered -- makes an eight-member call a storm of rings for members who
are all already there. Mixing at one exchange: refused by SIP-13 for the
reason it gives there.

## Compatibility

Additive. An exchange from before this document refuses `JoinShared` as
malformed and a client falls back to `Join`: the call works among the
members at that exchange, as it did. A relay peer that does not know
`RoomShare` ignores an unknown control type, as SIP-39 requires, and shares
nothing back; the room is then two rooms, one at each exchange, which is
where this document started. A `CallOpen` with a key for a domain is
refused by an older exchange as a domain it cannot discover.

## Security considerations

**What a relay peer learns.** For a room a member asked to share: the
handle, and the identity and proof of every member -- which the peer's own
members would show it anyway, since every member presents every other in
its roster. Proofs are HMACs under the secret and yield nothing without
it. The peer does not learn which channel the call is in, unless it also
holds a copy of the channel, in which case it already knew.

**A peer can lie about who is present.** It can share identities that are
not there, or withhold ones that are. A shared member with a bad proof is
refused by every client, as SIP-13 requires; a withheld one is a member
the peer's own members cannot hear either. What a lying peer gains is a
call its members cannot join properly, which is the peer's operator
choosing to break their own service. A shared member is never counted
against the receiving exchange's cap, so a peer cannot fill a room from
outside.

**The bridge without a ring is not a bridge without consent.** The
addressed device must still open toward the caller; the exchange matches
two requests that both name the other, as SIP-12 always did. What is
removed is a notification the device did not need, not a step it had to
take. An `InviteDevice` to a device that never opens sits until the
bridge expires, and rings no phone on the way.

**A member unreachable through federation** -- at an exchange that is not
a peer of yours -- is in the call and cannot be heard. Shown as such; not
dropped, since the members who can reach them may be talking to them.

## Reference implementation

Planned: `sqex-proto::room::{JoinShared, Homed}` and `relay::Control::RoomShare`;
`sqexd` shares a room over the relay link and matches an `InviteDevice`
against an outstanding `Open`; `sqex-voice`'s room membership places a `CallOpen`
toward a lower-ordered remote member and answers a higher one; sigil shows
a member it cannot reach as present and silent.
