## Abstract

A device publishes single-use X25519 public keys to an exchange, signed under
its own identity, and an exchange hands each one out once. A SIP-17 envelope is
then sealed against a fresh key at both ends rather than against the recipient's
long-term identity, so an attacker who records envelopes and later obtains that
identity key cannot open what they recorded. This closes the harvest-now
decrypt-later attack on key distribution. It does not give per-message forward
secrecy and it does not make a device forget what it has already read, and the
security section is explicit about both.

## Motivation

SIP-17 distributes a channel key by sealing it to each device with SIP-5's
construction: a fresh ephemeral from the sender, and the recipient's **static**
X25519 key, derived forward from its long-term Ed25519 identity. That is
one-sided, and SIP-17 says so — compromise of an identity key opens every
envelope ever sealed to it, and rotating the channel key does not help, because
every epoch's envelopes are sealed to the same long-term key.

The consequence is a harvest-now decrypt-later attack with an unusually good
yield. Envelopes sit at the exchange for the retention window and every one of
them carries a channel key. An attacker who copies them — by seizing the
exchange, by compelling its operator, by reading a backup — holds a pile of
ciphertext that is worthless today and becomes an entire history the moment any
one device's identity key turns up. Nothing the members do in the meantime
reduces that, and frequent rotation makes the pile larger rather than smaller.

The standard remedy is X3DH's: publish one-time keys in advance, use each once,
and delete the secret on use. Applied here it changes the envelope from
ephemeral-static to ephemeral-ephemeral, and the pile stops being a liability
that ages badly.

**What this does not attempt** deserves stating in the motivation rather than
being discovered in the security section. A device that can show you last
month's conversation is a device that holds last month's keys, and compromising
it yields them whatever this SIP does. Forward secrecy of *history* is not
available to a design that has history on the device. What is available, and
what this buys, is that the exchange's stored envelopes stop being a delayed-
action disclosure.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. Every operation is bound to the caller's
transport identity (SIP-3). A connection carrying no identity has no prekeys and
MUST be refused. Prekeys belong to a **device**, not to an account: the secret
is held by the client that will use it.

`PREKEY_CONTEXT` is the ASCII string `sqex-prekey-v1`.

### Prekeys

```
Prekey | kind: u8 | id: u32 | public[32] | signature[64] |

signature = Ed25519(device_secret, PREKEY_CONTEXT || device || kind || id
                    || public)
```

| `kind` | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0x01 | One-time — served once, then gone |
| 0x02 | Fallback — served whenever the one-time pool is empty |

`public` is an X25519 public key. `id` is assigned by the device, MUST increase,
and **MUST NEVER be reused**, including across a fallback being replaced. `id` 0
is reserved and MUST NOT be published.

The signature is over the concatenation rather than over a digest of it, which
departs from SIP-10 and SIP-20. Those hash first so that a hardware key can sign
a fixed-width input; a device key is necessarily a *software* key, because it is
also a transport key and a hardware token will not release the scalar sQUIC
needs — SIP-11 exists because of that. There is no APDU to size against, and 83
bytes signs directly.

A device MUST publish prekeys before it can be sealed to. In practice that means
immediately after SIP-22 registration, and a client SHOULD do both in the same
exchange of requests.

### Messages

```
Publish | type: u8 = 0x01 | count: u16 | count × Prekey |
Take    | type: u8 = 0x02 | device[32] |
Count   | type: u8 = 0x03 |
Clear   | type: u8 = 0x04 |

Ack     | accepted: u16 | now: u64 |
Taken   | found: u8 | kind: u8 | id: u32 | public[32] | signature[64]
        | now: u64 |
Counts  | one_time: u16 | fallback_id: u32 | now: u64 |
Cleared | discarded: u16 | next_id: u32 | now: u64 |
```

`POST /prekey/{publish,take,count,clear}`.

`Publish` adds one-time prekeys to the caller's pool and replaces its fallback;
a batch may contain both kinds. An exchange MUST refuse a prekey whose `id` it
has already seen for that device, and MUST verify every signature — it cannot
tell a good X25519 key from a bad one, but it can tell that the device claiming
it signed for it, which is what stops anyone else filling somebody's pool.

A sender **MUST** verify a prekey's signature under the device's Ed25519 key
before sealing to it, and MUST NOT seal against one that fails. The exchange
verifies at `Publish` too, but the exchange is the party this signature exists
to constrain, so a sender that trusts its check has verified nothing.

`Take` returns one prekey for `device` and **consumes** it. An exchange MUST
serve a one-time prekey at most once. When the pool is empty it serves the
fallback, which it MAY serve any number of times. `found: 0` means the device
has published nothing; a caller MUST NOT then seal to it at all.

`Count` reports the caller's own remaining pool. A device SHOULD keep at least
`LOW_WATER` one-time prekeys published and SHOULD top up to `POOL` whenever it
finds itself below that. It SHOULD replace its fallback every `FALLBACK_MAX_AGE`.

`Clear` discards every prekey the exchange holds for the calling device, both
one-time and fallback, and answers with how many went and the first `id` the
device may now use. It exists for one situation, and the situation is not rare:
a device that has lost the secrets behind prekeys the exchange is still serving.

Until that device publishes again, `Take` for it MUST answer `found: 0` — which
is the whole point. A prekey whose secret is gone is worse than no prekey at
all, because `found: 0` makes a caller refuse to seal, while a stale prekey
makes it seal to something that will never open.

**`Clear` MUST NOT forget the ids.** The never-reuse rule outlives the prekeys
themselves, so an exchange retains a high-water `id` per device independently of
what it stores, and `next_id` is one above it. That is a few bytes per device
and it must survive the prekeys being discarded, for the same reason SIP-16
keeps its `msg_seq` high-water mark apart from the entries it summarises: the
record exists to stop a counter being reused, so pruning what it describes must
not lower it.

`next_id` is also the answer to a question a recovering device cannot otherwise
answer. Its own record of which ids it has used went with the secrets, and every
one of them will be refused forever; `Count` reports only the current
`fallback_id`, which is a lower bound and only when a fallback was ever
published. Without `next_id` a device in this state can do nothing but guess,
and each guess is refused permanently.

### The envelope, amended

SIP-17's envelope gains the prekey it was sealed against:

```
Envelope | channel[32] | recipient[32] | from_epoch: u32 | to_epoch: u32
         | prekey_id: u32 | ephemeral[32] | ciphertext |
```

and its derivation gains a second Diffie-Hellman term:

```
DH1 = X25519(ephemeral_secret, recipient_static)
DH2 = X25519(ephemeral_secret, recipient_prekey)
okm = SHA-512(ENVELOPE_CONTEXT || ephemeral_pub || recipient_static
              || prekey_public || DH1 || DH2)
key = okm[0..32]      nonce = okm[32..44]
```

`recipient_static` is the forward derivation of the recipient device's Ed25519
identity, exactly as before. Both terms MUST be rejected if non-contributory.

**Both terms are load-bearing and they do different jobs.** DH1 binds the
envelope to the recipient's identity, which is the property SIP-17 already had:
without the identity key, the envelope does not open. DH2 is the forward
secrecy: the prekey secret is deleted after use, so the term cannot be recomputed
later by anyone. Keeping DH1 means this construction is strictly stronger than
the one it replaces and degrades to it — rather than to nothing — if a prekey
secret is somehow retained or leaked.

**`prekey_id` 0 MUST be refused**, by senders and by recipients alike. There is
deliberately no static-only path: an optional one is a downgrade a dishonest
exchange could force simply by reporting an empty pool, and a downgrade nobody
is required to notice is not a fallback but a hole. Refusing to seal is the
correct behaviour when a device has published nothing, and it is visible.

### Using and destroying a prekey

A recipient opening an envelope MUST look up `prekey_id` among its unconsumed
prekeys, and:

- If the id is a one-time prekey it holds, use it, and **destroy the secret
  immediately** after the envelope opens. Deleting it is the entire mechanism;
  a client that keeps prekey secrets has implemented the wire format and none of
  the property.
- If the id names a one-time prekey it has already consumed, **reject the
  envelope**. That is either an exchange serving the same prekey twice or an
  attacker replaying, and both are indistinguishable from here and unwelcome.
- If the id is its current fallback, use it and keep the secret; the fallback is
  reusable by construction and is why it is aged out on a schedule.
- If the id is a fallback it has already replaced, reject the envelope.

The duplicate check is the same shape as SIP-17's `(device, epoch, msg_seq)`
rule and exists for a related reason: an exchange that hands a one-time prekey
out twice has quietly removed the forward secrecy from both envelopes, and the
recipient is the only party positioned to notice.

### Losing the secrets

Deleting a prekey secret is the mechanism, so a client that loses its whole
store has done what this SIP asks, all at once and for every prekey it ever
published. The keys are unrecoverable, which is correct. What is not correct is
what the exchange goes on doing about it: it still holds those prekeys, still
serves them, and every peer that seals to one produces an envelope the device
can never open.

Left alone that does not settle. The device rotates (SIP-17) because it cannot
read the epoch in force; its peer seals the new epoch to another stale prekey
and cannot be read either; and two clients can rotate past each other until the
stale pool drains, which at `POOL` one-time keys apiece is not a wait anybody
should be asked to sit through. Nothing in the protocol tells either side why.

A device that finds itself in this state — no prekey secrets, and an exchange
reporting prekeys for it — MUST `Clear` before it publishes again, and SHOULD do
so before its first `Publish` on a fresh store. It then mints from `next_id`.
Peers calling `Take` in the interval get `found: 0` and correctly decline to
seal, which is a visible failure rather than an invisible one.

This does not recover history, and no operation can. Envelopes already sealed to
the discarded prekeys stay shut, and the entries under those epochs stay
unreadable to this device. Recovering the *conversation* is SIP-17's rotation:
mint a new epoch, seal it to the members, and carry on. What `Clear` recovers is
the ability to be sealed to at all.

### Limits

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| `POOL` | 64 one-time prekeys |
| `LOW_WATER` | 16 |
| Prekeys per `Publish` | 64 |
| Stored one-time prekeys per device | 128 |
| `FALLBACK_MAX_AGE` | 7 days |
| `Clear` per device per hour | 4 |

A `Publish` that would take a device above the stored limit MUST be refused
distinguishably from a malformed request rather than silently discarding the
excess.

`Clear` is bounded because it is the one operation here that makes the exchange
throw work away, and a device with nothing published is a device nothing can be
sealed to. The bound is generous against the intended use — a recovering client
calls it once — and tight against a loop. An overrun MUST be refused
distinguishably; unlike SIP-24's limits there is nothing to conceal, since the
caller is asking about its own state and already knows the answer.

## Rationale

**Why one-time keys and a fallback, rather than one-time keys alone.** A pool
can be exhausted — by a device that has been offline for weeks while its
channels rotated, or by anyone calling `Take` in a loop. Exhaustion with no
fallback means a member cannot be sealed to, which means a rotation cannot
complete, which means a removal does not take effect. Making a security
mechanism able to block a *revocation* is a poor trade. The fallback bounds the
damage to a week's worth of envelopes rather than to all of them, and X3DH
reaches the same conclusion by the same route.

**Why the exchange serves prekeys rather than peers exchanging them directly.**
The whole point of an envelope is that it can be sealed to somebody who is not
there. A prekey obtained by asking the recipient would need the recipient
present, which is the requirement SIP-17 exists to avoid.

**Why prekeys are signed.** The exchange stores and serves them and must not be
able to substitute its own — a substituted prekey it generated would let it
compute DH2. It still cannot open the envelope, because DH1 needs the identity
key, so an unsigned prekey would not be catastrophic; it would merely reduce
this SIP to a no-op against exactly the adversary it was written for. Sixty-four
bytes per prekey is a small price for the mechanism working against the party
holding it.

**Why the device signs, not the account.** The device holds the secret. An
account-signed prekey would say the account vouches for a key it does not hold,
and would drag a possibly-hardware account key into a routine operation that
happens dozens of times a week.

**Why `Clear` discards rather than replaces.** The alternative is a `Publish`
that supersedes everything before it, which would make recovery one request
instead of two and is worse in the case that matters. A device recovering from
a lost store has no prekeys to publish yet — it needs `next_id` before it can
mint any — so a replacing `Publish` would have to be callable with an empty
batch, which is a request that says "discard everything" while looking like a
request that says "add nothing". Making the destructive operation say so is
worth one extra round trip that happens once in a device's life.

**Why the exchange does not also discard the envelopes.** It could: an envelope
carries `prekey_id`, so the ones sealed to discarded prekeys are identifiable,
and they are certainly dead weight. It does not, because the exchange cannot
know *why* a device cleared. A device replacing a pool it still holds the
secrets for can open those envelopes perfectly well, and an exchange that
deleted them would have destroyed readable key material on a guess. SIP-17 is
explicit that its envelope store is for collection rather than storage; letting
them expire on that schedule costs a little space and no correctness.

**Why not simply rotate identity keys frequently.** Rotating an identity means
becoming a different device: re-registering under SIP-22, re-invitation into
channels, and a new entry in every member list. Prekeys give the same forward
secrecy for the envelope without disturbing anything that names the device.

**Why this is not MLS.** RFC 9420 is the actual answer to group forward secrecy
and post-compromise security, and it is larger than everything in this set so
far combined. It also assumes a group whose members ratchet together, which sits
badly with a design where a new member is handed history. This SIP is the
narrow, well-understood fix to the specific weakness SIP-17 documented; MLS
remains the thing to reach for if per-message forward secrecy is ever the
requirement, and it would replace SIP-17 rather than extend it.

## Compatibility

`Clear` was added after this SIP became Active, and it is additive: a new type
byte and a new route, with no change to any existing message. An exchange that
does not implement it refuses type `0x04` as it would refuse any unknown type,
and a client MUST treat that refusal as "this exchange has no `Clear`" rather
than as a failure of the recovery it was attempting. An older client never sends
it. So the two can be deployed in either order.

A client that cannot `Clear` — because the exchange predates it — is in the
state this SIP described before the amendment, and its remedy is the one it
always had: wait for the stale pool to drain. Slow, and no longer silent, since
it now knows what it is waiting for.

When this SIP was written it added three routes and **one breaking change to
SIP-17**: the envelope gained `prekey_id` and a second DH term. SIP-17 was Draft
and unimplemented at the time, so that was an amendment rather than a flag day
and it was made in place — there was no deployed envelope to interoperate with.
Both are Active and implemented now, and they shipped together.

An exchange implementing SIP-17 without this SIP cannot serve envelopes at all,
because `prekey_id` 0 is invalid. That is intentional: the two ship together or
the sealing path has a silent downgrade in it.

Nothing else changes on the wire. Per-message sealing (SIP-17), entries
(SIP-16), blobs (SIP-18) and message format (SIP-19) are untouched.

**One behaviour does change, and it is not a wire format.** Before this SIP a
device could recover everything after a reinstall: refetch the envelopes from
the exchange and open them all with its identity key. That is precisely the
capability being removed — an attacker with the identity key could do the same —
so it goes, and it goes for the owner too. A device that destroys its prekey
secrets cannot reopen an envelope it has already consumed.

The consequence is that **key material must be backed up or history is lost**.
Linking a new device is unaffected: it publishes its own prekeys and is sealed
to for whatever epoch range the inviter grants (SIP-17). What no longer works is
the same device recovering itself from the exchange alone. Signal makes the same
trade and for the same reason; it is worth naming because the earlier design's
recoverability looked like a feature and was a symptom.

## Security considerations

**What this buys: the exchange's envelope store stops ageing into a
disclosure.** An attacker who copies every envelope — seizing the exchange,
compelling its operator, reading a backup — and later obtains a device's
identity key gets nothing, because DH2 needed a secret that was deleted on use.
That is the harvest-now decrypt-later attack, it is the realistic one against a
service that stores ciphertext, and it is closed.

**What this does not buy: forward secrecy of history against a compromised
device.** A device that can display last month's conversation is holding last
month's channel keys, and an attacker who takes the device takes them. No key
agreement changes that; only not having the history does. This SIP protects key
*distribution*, not key *storage*, and an implementation MUST NOT describe the
result as forward secrecy without saying which.

**Per-message forward secrecy is absent and is not attempted.** Within an epoch,
every entry from a device is sealed under one subkey (SIP-17) that does not
evolve and is not deleted. Compromising a channel key exposes that whole epoch.
Rotation bounds an epoch in time; it is not a ratchet and the two should not be
described alike.

**A dishonest exchange can degrade this and cannot break it.** It can serve one
prekey to many senders, or serve the fallback while a pool is full, and thereby
strip DH2's value from those envelopes. It cannot open anything, because DH1
still requires the identity key. The recipient's duplicate check catches the
first case; the second is invisible from inside the protocol, and a client
SHOULD notice that its pool is not being consumed while envelopes keep arriving.

**Prekey exhaustion is a denial of service anyone can cause.** `Take` is
unauthenticated by necessity — anybody who may seal to a device must be able to
fetch one — so anybody can drain a pool and force the fallback. The fallback is
what stops that becoming a failure to rotate, and it means the attack degrades
forward secrecy rather than availability. An exchange SHOULD rate-limit `Take`
per caller, which raises the cost without changing the conclusion.

**The fallback is a static key for as long as it lives.** Every envelope sealed
against it shares its fate, so `FALLBACK_MAX_AGE` is the real granularity of
this SIP's guarantee in the worst case. Seven days is a choice, not a limit of
the construction.

**Recoverability and forward secrecy are the same dial.** An implementation that
quietly retains prekey secrets so its users can reinstall without losing history
has turned this SIP off while appearing to implement it, and has done so in the
way least likely to be noticed — everything still works. If a product wants both
properties it must back up key material deliberately, under the user's control
and encrypted to something they hold, and it must say that this is what it is
doing. It must not achieve it by not deleting.

**Deleting a secret is harder than it looks.** The mechanism is entirely the
deletion, and a client that writes prekey secrets to a synchronised backup, a
crash dump, or a filesystem that does not overwrite has kept them where an
attacker will look. This is the implementation detail most likely to silently
void the whole document.

**`Clear` is a denial of service a device can only inflict on itself.** It is
bound to the caller's transport identity, so nobody can clear anybody else's
pool; what it can do is leave the caller unsealable until it publishes again.
That is the same state as a device that has never published, which this SIP
already has to handle, and the rate limit bounds the write load rather than any
disclosure.

**Clearing races a `Take` that has already happened.** A peer can be handed a
prekey and seal to it moments before the device discards it. Nothing prevents
this and nothing should: the envelope is either openable, because the device
still held that secret, or it is not, because the device had already lost it —
and in the second case the envelope was doomed before `Clear` was called. The
remedy is the one that already covers an unopenable epoch, which is rotation.

**Prekey pools disclose activity.** The rate at which a device's pool drains
tells the exchange, and anyone who can call `Count` against it, how often that
person is being added to channels or rotated. It is a modest signal and it is
one the exchange largely had already from the envelopes themselves.

## Reference implementation

Implemented in [sqex](https://github.com/wave-cl/sqex) v0.9.0, and `Clear` in
v0.9.2:

- `sqex-proto::prekey` holds `Prekey`, `Publish`, `Take` and `Counts`, with the
  device in every signing input so a prekey cannot be lifted from one pool and
  republished under another name.
- `Pool` is the receiver's side: it owns the id counter, so ids cannot be
  reused, and it applies the four-way rule on opening — spend a one-time secret
  and destroy it, refuse one already spent, keep a fallback until it is replaced,
  refuse one that has been. It is a shipped type and not a note in this document
  for the same reason SIP-17's `Replay` is: an exchange that serves one one-time
  prekey twice has quietly removed the forward secrecy from both envelopes, and
  the recipient is the only party positioned to notice.
- `sqexd::prekey` serves a one-time prekey at most once and falls back when the
  pool is dry, verifying every signature at `Publish`. Its high-water `id` is a
  field beside the pool rather than a property of it, so `Clear` empties the one
  and cannot touch the other.
- Routes: `POST /prekey/{publish,take,count,clear}`.

The reference client destroys the secret when the pool hands it over rather than
after the envelope opens. A failed open is not grounds to keep it for a second
attempt: the exchange served that id, so it is spent, and holding it back for a
retry is exactly the replay window the check exists to close.

`Clear` was written because running the client found the gap rather than because
reading the specification did. Two clients that had both lost their stores
rotated past each other in a live trial — each sealing the new epoch to a stale
prekey the other could no longer open — and the regression test for it fails
against the pre-amendment client and passes against this one, which is the only
evidence worth having that a fix fixed something.

Tests cover a device that has published nothing being unsealable to; a one-time
prekey served exactly once; a pool served down to its fallback; another device
unable to fill somebody's pool; a batch with one bad signature publishing
nothing; an id never reused, including across a `Clear`; a one-time prekey
refused on its second use; a cleared device answering `found: 0` until it
publishes again, and publishing successfully from `next_id`; and —
the one that earns the second Diffie-Hellman term — an envelope that opens under
neither the identity key nor the prekey alone. The private-channel integration
flow drives the pool through a live exchange for every seal it makes.
