## Abstract

Video inside a SIP-12 session, on the same carriage and under the same keys as
SIP-15's audio. A coded picture is larger than a datagram, so the mechanism this
document adds is **fragmentation with a visible failure**: a picture is split
across datagrams, reassembled by the receiver, and discarded whole if any part
is missing — never handed to a decoder in pieces. A **track descriptor** says
what the pictures are of, and it is the same descriptor for a phone's camera and
for a shared screen: a source, a facing, a rotation and a content hint, rather
than two mechanisms that would drift apart. SIP-36 reserved bit 1 of its `media`
byte for this and said "video needs its own document"; this is that document.

## Motivation

Everything video needs is already here except the thing video is: SIP-12 relays
opaque frames over unreliable datagrams, SIP-13 makes a room, SIP-15 puts a
timestamped frame inside the payload, and SIP-36 starts a call from a
conversation. SIP-36 states the gap in terms this document has to satisfy:

> There is no video framing in this stack: SIP-15 defines audio and says nothing
> about a second stream, and a client MUST NOT infer one. Video needs its own
> document, and this byte is where it will attach when somebody writes it.

**Why audio's framing does not stretch to cover it.** SIP-15's frame is a type
byte, a timestamp and a body, and one Opus packet is about sixty bytes. That
whole design rests on a coded unit fitting in a datagram with room to spare.
A coded picture does not: SIP-12 caps a datagram's plaintext at 1024 bytes, and
an ordinary 720p keyframe is two orders of magnitude larger than that. Splitting
it is not a detail of the encoding — it is a new failure mode, because a picture
now arrives in pieces over a path that is explicitly allowed to lose them, and
what the receiver does with three quarters of a picture is the substance of this
SIP.

**Why one document for a camera and a screen.** They differ in three things a
byte each can carry — which way a camera points, which way the device is held,
and whether the content is motion or detail — and in nothing else. They produce
coded pictures, at a frame rate, with a size and a rotation, over the same
session. Writing them separately would mean two descriptors, two sets of
reassembly rules and two places to fix the next bug; and the interesting case is
the one where a person shares a screen *and* their camera at once, which needs
them to be the same mechanism with two tracks anyway.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. This framing occupies the **plaintext of a
SIP-12 frame**, on either carriage, and registers four new types in the space
SIP-15 opened when it said "any other type MUST be ignored". The exchange sees
only ciphertext and is unaffected.

### The frame types

SIP-15's frame is unchanged:

```
| type: u8 | timestamp: u32 | body |
```

| `type` | meaning | body | direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| `0x01` | audio (SIP-15) | one codec packet | sender → receiver |
| `0x02` | comfort (SIP-15) | `\| level: u8 \| tilt: u8 \|` | sender → receiver |
| `0x03` | picture fragment | below | sender → receiver |
| `0x04` | track descriptor | below | sender → receiver |
| `0x05` | track state | below | sender → receiver |
| `0x06` | track request | below | **receiver → sender** |

`0x06` travels back along the same session, in the other direction, under
SIP-12's other nonce direction. It is the only frame in this stack that a
*receiver* of media sends about media, and the reason is in "Recovering from
loss" below.

### The clock

`timestamp` for every type in this document is **milliseconds** from an
arbitrary origin chosen by the sender. It wraps, and a receiver MUST handle
wrapping; at a millisecond a `u32` lasts forty-nine days.

**A sender that sends both audio and video MUST take both clocks from one origin
instant**, so that a SIP-15 timestamp multiplied by 20 and a timestamp here are
the same millisecond scale. That is the whole of lip synchronisation and it
costs no bytes: a receiver that renders each stream against its own timestamp is
already synchronised, and one that does not is relying on the two arriving at
the same rate, which is the thing a network does not do.

Milliseconds rather than SIP-15's 20 ms ticks because a frame rate is not a
divisor of 50: 30 fps is 33⅓ ms and 24 fps is 41⅔ ms, and rounding either to a
20 ms grid puts a visible judder on every picture.

### A picture fragment

```
| 0x03 | timestamp: u32 | track: u8 | flags: u8 | picture: u16 |
                          frag: u16 | of: u16 | payload |
```

| field | meaning |
|---|---|
| `track` | which track of this sender's, from `0x01`. `0x00` is invalid |
| `flags` | bit 0 `KEYFRAME` — this picture decodes without reference to another. Every other bit is reserved, MUST be sent zero and MUST be ignored on receipt |
| `picture` | increments by one for every picture the sender emits on this track, and wraps |
| `frag` | this fragment's index within the picture, from 0 |
| `of` | how many fragments the picture has in total |
| `payload` | that slice of the coded picture |

Every fragment of one picture carries the same `timestamp`, `picture`, `flags`
and `of`. The coded picture is the concatenation of the payloads in `frag`
order, and nothing else: there is no per-fragment header inside the payload and
no padding.

The header is 8 bytes on top of SIP-15's 5, so **a fragment's payload MUST NOT
exceed 1011 bytes** — SIP-12's 1024-byte plaintext limit, less both headers. A
sender SHOULD fill every fragment but the last.

`of` MUST NOT exceed `MAX_FRAGMENTS`, RECOMMENDED **256**, which bounds a
picture at about 253 KiB and a receiver's reassembly buffer with it. A fragment
naming a larger `of` MUST be discarded.

A `picture` counter that increments once per picture, rather than the timestamp
alone, is what lets a receiver tell a dropped picture from a lowered frame rate.
Both are a gap in time; only one is a fault.

### Reassembly, and what is never handed to a decoder

A receiver collects fragments by `(track, picture)`. A picture is complete when
all `of` fragments have arrived.

- A complete picture is handed to the decoder as one coded picture.
- An **incomplete picture MUST NOT be handed to the decoder**, not even with the
  missing bytes zeroed or the received prefix alone. A truncated coded picture
  is malformed input, and SIP-18 says what that is: video decoders "are among
  the most attacked code any client runs". Handing them a half picture on every
  lost datagram is a decision to exercise that path continuously.
- A receiver MUST hold at most `REASSEMBLY_PICTURES` incomplete pictures per
  track, RECOMMENDED **4**, discarding the oldest.
- An incomplete picture MUST be discarded once a later `picture` on that track
  has completed, or after `REASSEMBLY_TTL`, RECOMMENDED **200 ms** — whichever
  is first. A fragment for a picture already discarded MUST be discarded too and
  MUST NOT restart reassembly of it.

A receiver detects a lost fragment from `(picture, frag, of)` alone and needs
nothing from SIP-12's `seq` to do it. That is deliberate, and the next section
says why it had to be.

### What sharing a session does to SIP-15's classification

SIP-15 classifies a hole in the audio by arithmetic over two counters:

```
missing slots = ts_n - ts_p - 1
lost packets  = seq_n - seq_p - 1
```

**That arithmetic assumes audio is alone on the wire, and video breaks it.**
SIP-12's `seq` counts every frame sent in a direction, so once video fragments
are interleaved, the gap in `seq` between two consecutive audio frames counts
the video datagrams sent between them. `lost packets` becomes far larger than
the audio actually lost — and SIP-15 permits concealing up to that number, so a
receiver applying it unchanged conceals deliberate silence as loss. That is
precisely the failure SIP-15's two counters exist to prevent, reintroduced by
putting a second stream in the session.

So, for a session carrying both, this document narrows the rule:

- A receiver MUST NOT use the `seq` arithmetic to classify an audio gap. It MUST
  classify from the last audio-bearing frame it received on the session: after a
  **comfort** frame the missing slots are silence, and after an **audio** frame
  they are loss.
- `missing slots` is unchanged — it is arithmetic on the audio timestamp, which
  video does not touch.
- A receiver that has not seen video on a session MAY keep SIP-15's rule as
  written.

SIP-15's rendering table is otherwise unchanged, and the descriptor-driven rows
in it already carry the whole of the new rule: a receiver that has a `level` and
`tilt` synthesises, and one that does not decodes nothing. What is removed is
only the arithmetic that video made unreliable.

### Recovering from loss

A coded picture that references a picture the receiver never assembled decodes
to nothing useful, and a decoder fed a broken reference chain produces smears
and blocks that persist until the chain is repaired. So:

- After discarding a picture, a receiver MUST NOT display subsequent non-keyframe
  pictures of that track until it has decoded a `KEYFRAME`. It MAY keep decoding
  them to keep the decoder's state current, and MUST NOT put the result on
  screen.
- A receiver in that state SHOULD send a `keyframe` track request.

```
| 0x06 | timestamp: u32 | track: u8 | request: u8 |
```

| `request` | meaning |
|---|---|
| `0x01` | keyframe — I cannot decode this track; send an independently decodable picture |
| `0x02` | pause — stop sending this track |
| `0x03` | resume — send it again |

A receiver MUST NOT send more than one `keyframe` request per track per
`KEYFRAME_MIN`, RECOMMENDED **one second**, and a sender MUST ignore requests
arriving faster than that rather than honouring them. A keyframe is the most
expensive thing a sender emits, so an unthrottled request is both a feedback
loop under sustained loss — every dropped picture asking for the burst that
drops the next one — and the amplification lever named in the security
considerations.

`pause` and `resume` are advisory. A sender MAY ignore them; a receiver MUST NOT
assume a track has stopped until a track state says so.

### The track descriptor

```
| 0x04 | timestamp: u32 | track: u8 | codec: u8 | source: u8 | facing: u8 |
                          content: u8 | rotation: u8 | width: u16 | height: u16 |
```

| `codec` | |
|---|---|
| `0x01` | H.264, constrained baseline profile. A picture is one access unit as an Annex B byte stream |

| `source` | |
|---|---|
| `0x01` | camera |
| `0x02` | screen — a whole display |
| `0x03` | window — one application's window |
| `0x04` | other — a file, a virtual camera, a document camera |

| `facing` | |
|---|---|
| `0x00` | unspecified, and the only legal value when `source` is not a camera |
| `0x01` | user — the front camera, pointing at the person holding the device |
| `0x02` | environment — the rear camera |

| `content` | |
|---|---|
| `0x00` | unspecified |
| `0x01` | motion — a scene that moves, where temporal smoothness matters |
| `0x02` | detail — text and edges, where a blurred character is the failure |

`rotation` is `0x00`, `0x01`, `0x02` or `0x03` for 0°, 90°, 180° and 270°
**clockwise, to be applied by the receiver before display**. `width` and
`height` are the coded dimensions, before rotation.

A value a receiver does not know MUST be treated as the unspecified or "other"
member of its field, never as an error — the same promise SIP-18 makes for a
blob kind and SIP-19 for a body type.

**A descriptor MUST be sent before the first fragment of a track, again on any
change to any field, and at least once per `DESCRIPTOR_INTERVAL`, RECOMMENDED
one second.** The repetition is not redundancy: a SIP-13 room is joined at any
moment, and a member who arrives between descriptors would otherwise have
pictures it can decode and cannot place.

Changing `width` or `height` changes what the decoder is decoding. A sender
MUST send the new descriptor and a `KEYFRAME` picture together, in that order.

### Track state

```
| 0x05 | timestamp: u32 | track: u8 | state: u8 |
```

| `state` | |
|---|---|
| `0x01` | live |
| `0x02` | paused — the sender has stopped, and intends to resume |
| `0x03` | ended — the track is gone; its `track` number MUST NOT be reused in this session |

**A receiver MUST NOT display the last picture of a paused or ended track as
though it were live.** A frozen face when somebody has turned their camera off
is a picture of a person who is no longer there, presented as if they were, and
it is the default behaviour of every implementation that simply stops updating a
surface. Show that the track has stopped.

A sender that stops sending without a track state leaves the receiver to infer
it from silence; a receiver SHOULD treat a track with no fragment and no
descriptor for `TRACK_STALE`, RECOMMENDED **three seconds**, as paused.

### Rate, and the signal that is not there

A sender MUST implement rate control. SIP-12 says the exchange "forwards
packets; it does not manage a call", and this is where that bites: video is two
to three orders of magnitude more traffic than SIP-15's audio, and in a SIP-13
room every member sends its own stream to every other, so an eight-person call
is fifty-six video streams through one exchange.

**The exchange has no way to say "slow down".** It can only drop datagrams, and
a dropped datagram is indistinguishable at the receiver from one the network
lost. The only congestion signal available to a sender is the receiver telling
it about gaps, which means an implementation that wants to adapt must carry that
report itself; nothing in this document provides one, and a sender that does not
adapt will simply fill whatever it is given until pictures stop arriving.

A sender SHOULD hold a keyframe's fragments to a pace rather than emitting them
as a burst. A 253 KiB picture is 256 datagrams, and a burst that size is dropped
by queues that would have carried it spread over a frame interval.

## Rationale

**Why fragment here rather than raise SIP-12's datagram limit.** The 1024-byte
limit exists so that "the whole datagram fits a path MTU whole — a datagram is
never fragmented and never retransmitted". Raising it does not remove
fragmentation, it moves it into IP, where losing one fragment loses the entire
datagram, the loss is invisible to this layer, and the reassembly timeout is the
kernel's. Fragmenting in the payload costs the same bytes and makes the failure
legible: this document can say which picture was incomplete and ask for a
keyframe, which IP cannot.

**Why not SIP-12's reliable path, which already carries 32 KiB.** It is the
wrong guarantee twice over. It retransmits, and a picture that arrives after its
moment is worse than one that never arrives; and it is drained by polling, which
SIP-12 measures at about 100 ms per frame against roughly 0.5 ms for a datagram.
A keyframe would still need splitting at 32 KiB, so the fragmentation here does
not go away — it only stops being visible.

**Why one session carries both audio and video.** Two sessions would be two key
agreements, two sequence spaces and two TTLs at the exchange, for two streams
between the same pair of identities — and the shared clock origin that makes lip
sync free would have to be negotiated instead. The objection to one session is
head-of-line blocking, and it does not apply: these are datagrams, delivered
unordered and never retransmitted, and the receiver keys on timestamps. A burst
of video fragments delays audio by the time it takes to send them and by nothing
else.

**Why a track byte rather than a session per track.** A person sharing a screen
and a camera at once is the case that motivates video in a meeting at all, and
it is one encoder's worth of state on one clock. A byte costs less than a second
session and keeps the two tracks provably from the same sender — the session key
already proves that, and a second session would need the two tied together.

**Why no negotiation.** There is no capability exchange in this stack and none
is introduced here. A sender states what it is sending in the descriptor and a
receiver decodes it or does not; `codec` is the extension point, and a receiver
that does not know a codec shows the track as one it cannot display rather than
failing the call. Negotiation buys agreement before the first picture, at the
cost of a handshake and a round trip on a path that has neither.

**Why H.264 constrained baseline as the one codec.** Three reasons, and the
third is the one that is easy to miss:

- It is the profile with hardware decode on every phone and every laptop in
  reach, and hardware decode is what makes a mesh of streams survivable on a
  battery.
- The decoder is already here. `sigil-video` decodes H.264 through `openh264`,
  which builds Cisco's implementation from source on every release target and
  under the Android NDK, so it needs no system package on any platform. VP9 and
  AV1 encode better per bit; neither has a real-time encoder on this hardware.
  (An AV1 encoder — `rav1e`, by way of AVIF still images — is already in the
  dependency graph and is not a real-time encoder, which is the point.)
- **Constrained baseline has no B-frames, so decode order is presentation
  order.** That matters more for a call than for a file: `sigil-video` records
  that `openh264` returns pictures reordered and says nothing about which sample
  each was, and had to label output by counting from a keyframe. A profile with
  no reordering removes that problem from the live path entirely, along with the
  frame of latency that reordering costs.

The choice is pragmatic rather than principled, which is why it is a byte.

**Why not simulcast or scalable coding.** They exist to let one upstream stream
serve viewers with different capacities, and they are what a selective
forwarding unit needs. There is no SFU here and SIP-36 explains why there cannot
be: an SFU "forwards streams it cannot read only if the keys are per-pair, which
is the mesh again, or it is given the keys, which is the refusal". In a mesh
each pair already has its own session and its own encoder settings, so a sender
that wants to send less to one peer simply does.

**Why not forward error correction.** It is the right answer to loss on a path
with no retransmission, and it is deliberately deferred. Useful FEC costs 20–50%
overhead continuously, to protect a keyframe that is a small fraction of the
bytes; the keyframe request costs nothing at all when there is no loss and one
keyframe when there is. FEC is worth revisiting with a measurement of real loss
rates behind it, which is exactly what this stack does not have yet.

**Why rotation is carried rather than applied before encoding.** A phone rotates
mid-call. Rotating the picture before the encoder changes the coded dimensions,
which forces a new descriptor and a keyframe every time somebody turns their
wrist. Carrying the angle costs two bits and lets the decoder keep its reference
chain across a rotation.

**Why `content` is separate from `source`.** A shared window showing a video
player wants motion; a camera pointed at a whiteboard wants detail. Deriving the
hint from the source gets the common case right and the interesting case wrong,
and the interesting case is the one somebody complains about.

**Why the receiver sends anything at all.** Every other frame in this stack goes
one way, and a keyframe request is the first that does not. The alternative is a
sender emitting keyframes on a timer, which spends the bandwidth whether or not
anybody needed one and still leaves a receiver waiting up to a whole interval
after a loss. One 2-byte frame on demand is cheaper in both directions, and the
rate limit is what keeps it from becoming the timer it replaced.

## Compatibility

**Purely additive, and it degrades to audio.** SIP-15 requires that a frame type
a receiver does not know "MUST be ignored", so a peer that implements only SIP-15
receives video fragments, descriptors and states and drops every one of them.
The call is an audio call. Nothing errors, nothing desynchronises, and the audio
timestamps it does understand are unchanged.

SIP-36's `media` bitfield gains its meaning for **bit 1**: the caller intends to
send or receive video. SIP-36 already requires that "a receiver MUST ignore bits
it does not know", so a callee that predates this document joins the call as an
audio call, which is the behaviour that byte was written for.

Nothing between a peer and the exchange changes. SIP-12 and SIP-13 are untouched
and an exchange cannot tell whether video is in use, except from the volume of
what it is carrying.

Both ends must implement this document to see video, and neither breaks without
it.

## Security considerations

**Sharing a screen discloses more than the sharer chooses.** A camera points
where it is pointed. A display carries notifications as they arrive, the
contents of whatever is in front, a second monitor the sharer has stopped
thinking about, and whatever a password manager puts on screen. This is the one
place in this stack where the disclosure is *accidental by default*, so the
requirement is on the interface rather than the wire:

- A client MUST show, continuously and unmistakably, that a screen is being
  shared and which screen or window it is.
- A client MUST make stopping reachable in one action, from wherever the sharer
  is looking.
- A client MUST NOT begin sharing without an explicit action for that session,
  and MUST NOT remember the permission across calls.
- A client SHOULD offer a single window before a whole display, and SHOULD say
  what the difference discloses.

**Bitrate is an activity signal, and a sharper one than voice.** SIP-15
records that packet presence tells an exchange when each party speaks.
Video is worse in kind: at any fixed target the bitrate still tracks the content,
so the exchange learns when somebody moves, when a room is empty, when a slide
advances, when a screen is being typed into and when it is idle. A keyframe is a
spike it can count. Continuous transmission does not repair this the way it
repairs the voice case, because the rate varies with the picture and not with
whether anything is sent. The content stays sealed; the shape of the activity
does not, and an implementation should not claim otherwise.

**The descriptor is an assertion, not a demonstration** — the same class of field
as SIP-15's `level`, and it needs the same treatment. `width`, `height` and `of`
are numbers a peer states and a receiver allocates against. A receiver MUST
bound dimensions it will accept (`MAX_DIMENSION`, RECOMMENDED **8192**), MUST
discard a fragment whose `of` exceeds `MAX_FRAGMENTS`, and MUST bound its
reassembly buffers per track rather than per picture claimed.

**`facing` is a claim about the physical world and nothing checks it.** A peer
saying `environment` may be sending the front camera, or a still image, or a
recording. A client MUST NOT present it as a fact about what the far person is
doing — "their rear camera" is a label the far client chose, and a label is all
it is.

**A keyframe request is an amplification lever.** Two bytes cause a response that
may be a quarter of a megabyte, from a sender whose bandwidth the requester does
not pay for. The rate limit is normative for that reason as much as for the
feedback loop, and a sender MUST enforce it on receipt rather than trusting the
requester to.

**A reassembled picture is hostile input.** SIP-18 already states the general
case; it is sharper here because a picture arrives sixty times a second from a
peer, rather than once from a blob the person chose to open. A receiver SHOULD
isolate the decoder from the rest of the client, and MUST treat a decode failure
as an ordinary event — a request for a keyframe — rather than an error path
nobody has exercised.

**The exchange still cannot read it.** SIP-12's three-term agreement is unchanged
and the fragments are sealed under it, so the descriptor, the dimensions and the
pictures are all opaque to the relay. What it gains over the audio case is
volume: fragment counts per picture are picture sizes, and picture sizes are
scene complexity over time.

**Recording is not addressed and cannot be.** Any receiver can keep what it
decodes, and no signal in this document changes that. A client MUST NOT imply
otherwise — there is no "do not record" bit here, deliberately, because a bit
that cannot be enforced reads as a promise.

## Reference implementation

None yet. Nothing in any of these repositories encodes video or captures a
camera or a screen, so this is a Draft with no running half.

What does exist is the decode side and the scars from it. `sigil-video` in
[sigil](https://github.com/wave-cl/sigil) plays an MP4 attachment in the
transcript: `openh264` for H.264, `symphonia` for AAC, its own demuxer and
playback clock. It decodes a **file already in memory**, not a stream, so none
of it is reusable as it stands — but three of its recorded findings apply
directly to a live decoder and are why the specification reads as it does:

- `openh264`'s default is to flush after each decode, and flushing breaks the
  stream: the tenth picture of an ordinary 720p file came back "out of memory"
  and every one after it "no parameter sets". The live path wants the same
  `NoFlush` configuration.
- The decoder returns pictures **reordered**, and says nothing about which
  sample each was. Constrained baseline is specified here partly to make that
  impossible.
- The decoder holds one or two pictures at the end of a stream. A call ends by
  a track state rather than an EOF, and the same trailing pictures will be
  sitting in it.

Where the rest would go:

- The framing, reassembly and track state belong beside `sqex-voice::media`,
  which already owns the SIP-15 frame and whose type byte is the extension point
  this document registers in — its decoder returns "not for me" for an unknown
  type today, which is what makes the compatibility claim above true rather than
  hoped for.
- The send path hangs off `sqex-voice::engine`'s `Carrier`, which already
  abstracts the relayed (SIP-12) and direct (SIP-25) datagram paths. One
  constraint there is load-bearing and not obvious: **a connection's datagrams
  have a single reader**, so a video loop cannot open its own — the existing
  read loop has to demultiplex by type byte and hand video on.
- `sqex-voice::jitter` keys one buffer on one 20 ms clock. A video track needs
  its own buffer at its own rate; it is a second instance, not a shared one.
- Capture is a platform trait with an implementation per target, and
  `sigil-platform`'s existing rule is the one to follow: nothing is ever
  silently inert, and a platform without a capture path says so in a sentence
  rather than appearing to work.

**One thing to decide before any of it, because it is a policy question and not
a coding one.** sigil-android has no `CAMERA` permission today, and its absence
is deliberate and documented in the manifest — "a permission nothing uses is a
question at review". Implementing this adds `CAMERA`, and on a newer Android a
`FOREGROUND_SERVICE_CAMERA` type beside the microphone one already there; screen
sharing adds `MediaProjection` and its own foreground service type. Those are
three review questions and a visible change to what the app asks for.

Two things should be measured before this leaves Draft, because both are
recommendations above that were reasoned to rather than observed:

- **`MAX_FRAGMENTS`, `REASSEMBLY_TTL` and the keyframe pacing**, against a real
  loss rate through a real exchange. The numbers here bound memory and look
  right; none has been watched under loss.
- **Whether a picture that loses one fragment is better discarded or better
  decoded with the loss concealed.** This document takes the strict position —
  discard, and ask for a keyframe — on the safety argument. Other stacks are
  more permissive, and the difference is visible on screen and measurable in
  keyframe rate.

SIP-15 was marked Active only after it was verified by ear. The equivalent here
is an eye: a rotation, a paused track and a screen share of moving text are
where a framing looks wrong long before a test does.
