## Abstract

An exchange retiring its key publishes, beside SIP-33's records, a **handover**:
a statement signed by the outgoing key naming its successor and an expiry. A
client whose pinned key has been withdrawn from DNS moves its pin to the
successor only when the zone publishes that successor *and* the pinned key has
signed for it; otherwise SIP-33's refusal stands exactly as written. Rotation
becomes something pinned clients follow rather than something they are stranded
by, without granting the zone any authority over a pin it does not have today.

An exchange also **keeps every handover it has signed** and serves the
chain -- its *lineage* -- to anyone who asks, so a verifier that meets
it after the window closed can check receipts and signatures under
every key in it; and an exchange that holds another exchange's key
follows that exchange's rotation wherever it holds it, on the retiring
key's own signed word, re-keying what it holds with an account's signed
Move's signature cleared.

## Motivation

SIP-9 says of key rotation: *"There is none."* SIP-33 gives an operator an
overlap window — more than one `k=` record in the RRset — and is precise about
what the window buys: clients with no pin take the new key, clients that have
pinned the old one keep working while it is published, and *"when the old key is
finally withdrawn, those clients stop, and a person decides."* Every deployed
exchange carries the consequence as a comment in its config: *replacing this key
strands every client that has pinned it.* The only recovery is a person running
`sqex discover --forget` on every client that ever connected, which on a public
exchange is a population the operator cannot reach.

SIP-33's Rationale considers the seamless alternative — let the pin follow a key
the client has *witnessed* beside its pinned one — and rejects it, correctly: an
adversary who can write the zone publishes their key beside the real one, waits,
withdraws the real one, and carries every existing client across in two
publishes. A witnessed key is only a key that was in the same answer. This SIP
does not reopen that decision.

What it adds is narrower. A client holds exactly one thing it trusts about an
exchange: the key it pinned. Nothing in the corpus lets that key *say* anything
about its own succession. Once it can, the analysis changes: a pin that moves
only to a successor the pinned key has signed for cannot be moved by a zone
compromise, because the zone cannot sign; and it cannot be moved by a stolen key
alone, because the client also requires the zone to publish the successor, and
the thief cannot publish. Two independent authorities, and the pin moves only
when both agree. That is the "act the operator takes deliberately" SIP-33 asks
for, made verifiable instead of manual.

DNS is where the statement belongs, not the exchange's own connection: the
client is already reading the RRset, already validating it, and — in the case
that matters — no longer has a pinned key it can connect to. Serving it over the
wire would mean connecting to an unpinned key to fetch the evidence for trusting
it, which is a second channel where one suffices (see Rationale).

the position of an account's home at an origin that had rotated four

told the laptop to look, and a week later nothing would have been there.

§Consumers other than pins says how a holder of the earlier key verifies: under
the current key first, then each key the current one moved from. It
says where a *client* gets that history (its pin store, which watched
the handover happen) and where a *configured replica* gets it (the
operator's `predecessors`). It has no answer for a verifier that met
the exchange after the window closed, and says so: "a holder that has
forgotten it cannot, which is the same position a new client is in
today". SIP-59 and SIP-60 made that verifier the ordinary case -- a home
finds an origin by an account's hint, a client's exchange locates a
domain it never listed -- and every one of them meets an origin with a
past it cannot read.

The fact needed is one the retiring key already signed. A handover is
a statement by the old key that the new key is its successor, bound to
the domain, and it does not stop being true when the window it was
published for closes; what closed was the period in which a *pin* would
move on it. The exchange that holds the successor key held the old one
when it signed, and can keep the statement. Serving it costs nothing and
asserts nothing the old key did not.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-66, via SIP-64.* The live run below (2026-09-18) put a laptop exchange in the position of
a home that had met trunk.exchange after trunk.exchange rotated, and

pins it on every connection; the next pull finds a domain answering as
a key it does not hold and stops ("a pull reported an origin we did not
pin"). The account's copy goes stale, the account's posts through the
home are refused, and nothing in the log says why -- the same silence
§Consumers other than pins fixed for the client store, one layer out.

The old home is worse off. It holds the account's signed Move naming
the home by key, and opens SIP-35's acts-for gate to that key alone. The
home's successor pulls and is refused as a stranger, and nothing but a
fresh Move from the account -- who may be on a phone that followed the
pin silently and sees nothing wrong -- reopens it. the pin rule made a
rotation invisible to a client; SIP-59 made the client's home an
exchange; this is where the two meet.

§Consumers other than pins gives the rule: a key held for a domain SHOULD follow a
valid handover for that domain. It did not say how a holder that is not
looking at the zone at the right moment learns of one, and §Lineage
answers that: the successor serves the chain the retiring key
signed, for as long as the history it covers. What is left is to apply
the rule at each place, and to say what following means for a record
the account signed.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. Keys are Ed25519 public keys, rendered
base58 in DNS and 32 raw bytes in signing input. **Origin** and **replica** are SIP-35's; **place** is
SIP-31's; **home**, **Move** and **hint** are SIP-59's; **peer** is SIP-35's.

### The handover record

A handover is a `TXT` record at the same name SIP-33 uses, `_sqex.<domain>`, in
SIP-33's tag-value grammar, distinguished by its version tag:

```
_sqex.example.com.  IN  TXT  "v=sqex1h; from=<key>; to=<key>; until=<secs>; sig=<sig>"
```

| tag | required | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `v` | yes | MUST be exactly `sqex1h` and MUST be the first tag. |
| `from` | yes | The outgoing key, base58, 32 bytes decoded. |
| `to` | yes | The successor key, base58, 32 bytes decoded. |
| `until` | yes | Expiry, decimal seconds since the Unix epoch. |
| `sig` | yes | Ed25519 signature by `from`, base58, 64 bytes decoded. |

SIP-33's parsing rules apply unchanged: a parser MUST ignore tags it does not
recognise, MUST reject a record with a duplicate tag, and MUST treat a record
whose first tag is neither `v=sqex1` nor `v=sqex1h` as somebody else's. **A
SIP-33 parser that predates this document therefore ignores a handover in
silence**, which is what makes the record additive.

The signature is over the domain-separated input

```
"sqex-handover-v1"      : 16 bytes, ASCII, no terminator
domain_len              : u8
domain                  : the domain as queried, lowercased, no trailing dot
from                    : [32]
to                      : [32]
until                   : u64
```

The domain is bound so a handover signed for one zone cannot be replayed under
another that the same key happens to serve.

A record MUST be rejected — silently, as somebody else's — if any of the
following holds: `from` or `to` is not 32 bytes of base58; `sig` is not 64;
`from == to`; `until` does not parse; or the signature does not verify under
`from`. A verifier MUST NOT report a rejected handover as an error: the RRset is
shared with everything else published at that name, and a malformed record
there is not a fault in the client's own configuration.

**`until` is bounded.** A client MUST reject a handover whose `until` is more
than `HANDOVER_MAX_SECS = 2 592 000` (thirty days) after the current time.
The bound exists for the signer's protection, not the client's: a handover is
irrevocable once published (see Security), and a bound on its life is a bound
on what a pre-signed one is worth to a thief.

### Resolution

SIP-33 §Resolution lists four outcomes on a later resolution. The first two are
unchanged. The third and fourth become:

- If the pinned key is no longer among the offered `k=` keys, the client looks
  for a handover in the same RRset with `from` equal to the pinned key, `to`
  equal to one of the offered `k=` keys, `until` in the future, and a valid
  signature. If **exactly one** such record exists, the client **moves its pin
  to `to`**, MUST record that the pin was moved and from what, and MUST tell the
  user it has done so. Two or more qualifying handovers naming different
  successors is a fault, not a choice: the client MUST refuse as SIP-33
  specifies and name every key involved.
- Otherwise — no handover, or a handover the rules above reject — the client
  MUST refuse exactly as SIP-33 §Resolution specifies. Nothing here weakens that
  refusal.

Three consequences are normative:

1. **A handover whose `to` is not also published as a `k=` record grants
   nothing.** The signature proves the outgoing key's intent; only the zone
   proves the successor is live. Both are required, always.
2. **While the pinned key is still published, a handover is not acted on.** The
   client proceeds with its pin as today. A published handover is a statement
   of what will happen, and an operator who withdraws it before withdrawing the
   old key has changed nothing for anyone.
3. **A handover is followed once.** `from` MUST equal the client's current pin.
   A client holding `A` that finds `A→B` and `B→C` moves to `B`, and only on a
   later resolution — with `B` pinned and withdrawn — to `C`. Chains are not
   walked (see Rationale).

The moved-from key is kept beside the new pin as history, not as a pin: it
authenticates nothing afterwards. A client MAY use it to name the change to the
user on later resolutions, and SHOULD forget it after `until` has passed.

### Producing a handover

The operator's sequence, each step separately observable:

1. Generate the successor key on the exchange host. The old key is not
   touched.
2. Publish `k=<new>` beside `k=<old>` (SIP-33's overlap window opens).
3. Sign and publish the handover `from=<old> to=<new>`. `sqexd handover --to
   <new> --until <days>` signs with the key the daemon is configured with and
   prints the record verbatim; it MUST refuse `to` equal to its own key and a
   `--until` beyond `HANDOVER_MAX_SECS`.
4. Switch the daemon to the new key and restart, **and withdraw `k=<old>` in
   the same breath.** These two are one step, not two: a client still pinned
   to the old key follows the handover only once the old key is gone from
   DNS (rule 2), but the daemon stops answering for it the moment it
   restarts. Between the two, that client fails its handshake — a key nothing
   answers for, and a pin that has no reason yet to move. The gap is real
   and lasts however long the operator leaves it, plus one record TTL; done
   together it is a TTL and no longer. Withdrawing the old record *before*
   the restart has the mirror-image gap (pins move to a key the daemon does
   not hold yet), so neither order is better and the answer is not to leave
   a gap.
5. After `until`, withdraw the handover. The window has closed.

A client that resolves the domain at any point between step 4 and step 5
migrates. A client that first resolves after step 5 hits SIP-33's refusal
exactly as it would today, and a person decides — and when the person decides
the new key is the same exchange, the client MUST offer a way to say so that
records the old key as the new pin's predecessor (`sqex discover --replace`),
so a store scoped by the old key follows exactly as it would after a signed
handover. Forgetting the pin outright is a different statement — *a different
exchange* — and a client MUST say, when asked to forget, that what is held
under the old key will not be shown. Nothing checks the person's assertion;
that is why it is a flag typed and not a prompt answered. **This SIP shortens the set of
stranded clients to those that did not connect during a window the operator
chooses; it does not empty it**, and an operator should choose the window with
that population in mind.

### Consumers other than pins

A key pinned for a domain is not the only key held for one. A SIP-39 relay-peer
entry and a SIP-35 origin both name an exchange by key and find it by SIP-33
discovery of its domain. Such a holder SHOULD apply the resolution rule above to
the key it holds, moving it on a valid handover for the domain it was discovered
through, and MUST log that it did so. In sqexd that store is the runtime relay
peer list (SIP-39); an administrator may equally update it by hand with the
same command that populated it. A followed entry's provenance names the
outgoing key as what authorised it — no administrator signed a transaction,
the retiring exchange signed a handover — and MUST NOT read as a
configuration seed, which is a different fact with a different lifetime.

**A client store scoped by exchange key is such a store, and MUST follow.**
A chat client files every row — channel keys, prekeys, history, cursors —
under the key of the exchange it talked to, because a direct message's channel
identifier is the same on every exchange and the key is what keeps two
exchanges' copies apart. After a pin moves, that client connects under the
successor, finds nothing filed there, and starts over: a fresh prekey pool
published, a second history begun beside the real one, and every conversation
on that exchange apparently empty. That is what the first live rotation did
to the operator's own client. A store MUST re-file rows held under the
moved-from key under the successor before its first request to the exchange,
and MUST do so on any later open that still finds rows under the moved-from
key — a store that moved before it knew how to follow is repaired the same
way. Where the successor scope already holds a row with the same primary key,
newer state (a cursor, a cached profile, an entry received twice) wins and
the old row is dropped; key material (a channel key, a chain mark, a prekey)
is never dropped silently — the collision is left in place and reported,
because two keys for one epoch are two histories and choosing one is how a
counter gets reused. The pin store keeps the moved-from key as a field for
exactly this lookup; it is history, not a pin.

A SIP-8 transport whitelist is **not** such a store. It holds keys, not keys
held for a domain, and nothing in it says which entry a domain's handover
would move; an exchange that whitelists a federation peer's key has to update
that entry by hand when the peer rotates, and this SIP says so rather than
pretending otherwise.

Nothing already signed is re-signed. SIP-34 receipts issued under the old key
verify under the old key, and a holder of the moved-from key as history can
verify them; a holder that has forgotten it cannot, which is the same position a
new client is in today. The same holds for SIP-31 signatures, whose place
names the exchange key the device saw when it signed. A verifier -- a client
reading, a replica pulling (SIP-35) -- therefore checks under the current key
first and then each key its pin store says the current one moved from, and
understands the entry under the key that verified: the SIP-31 chain link is
taken under that key, so a device's chain that crosses the handover still
links. A verifier that checks under the successor alone calls everything from
before the handover forged, which is what the first implementation did.

### Lineage

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-64.* An exchange's lineage is the ordered list of handovers from its oldest
key to its current one, each link's `to` being the next link's `from`,
and the last link's `to` its current key. An exchange with no rotation
behind it has an empty lineage.

```
Link    | dom_len: u8 | domain | from[32] | to[32] | until: u64 | sig[64] |
Lineage | count: u8 | count × Link |
```

`domain`, `from`, `to`, `until` and `sig` are the handover's fields as
§The handover record defines them, `domain` being the one the signature is bound to,
lowercased with no trailing dot. `count` is at most `MAX_LINEAGE = 16`.

`GET /exchange/lineage` (a `POST` with an empty body is the same
request, for a peer whose client only posts) answers with the exchange's
lineage, to any caller, identified or not: every link in it was published in a public
zone, and the current key is published there today. An exchange MUST
keep every handover it signs (§Producing a handover, step 3) and
serve it here from the moment the successor key is in use; an exchange
MAY also be given a link by its operator, for a rotation done before this
document. An exchange from before §Lineage (0.81.0) answers SIP-21
`not_found`, and a verifier treats that as an empty lineage.

### Verifying a lineage

A verifier holds a key `K` for an exchange -- pinned by SIP-33, named by
an account's Move (SIP-59), configured -- and, usually, the domain `D` it
holds it for. It accepts a lineage when:

1. every link's signature verifies under its `from` for the link's
   `domain`, by §The handover record's signing input;
2. no two links share a `from`, and each link's `to` is the next link's
   `from`;
3. the last link's `to` is `K`;
4. where the verifier holds `D`, every link's `domain` is `D`.

`until` is **not** checked against the clock. It bounded the window in
which a pin would move on the record (§Resolution); the record's
statement -- this key handed to that one -- has no expiry, and a lineage
is history. A lineage that fails any rule is discarded whole and the
verifier proceeds with what it had, which is what §Resolution already
requires of a bad handover: silently, and never as a fault in the
verifier's own state.

The accepted lineage's `from` keys, newest first, are the exchange's
**predecessors**, and a verifier uses them exactly as §Consumers other than pins
says a holder of the moved-from key does: a receipt (SIP-34) verifies
under the current key or any predecessor; a signed entry (SIP-31)
verifies under the place naming the key it was signed under, and its
chain link is taken under that key; **a key envelope (SIP-32) likewise**,
since its signature names the place it was published to. Predecessors
learned this way are
added to any the verifier already holds -- an operator's `predecessors`,
a pin store's moved-from key, a channel's origin history (SIP-53) -- and
replace none of them.

### When a replica asks

A replica (SIP-35, and a home acting as one under SIP-59) SHOULD ask an
origin's lineage before its first pull from it, and MUST ask again when
a pull refuses an entry as repudiated: a rotation since the last ask is
the ordinary reason for one. What it learns it keeps with the origin,
so a restart does not ask again and an origin that has since gone away
still has its past verified. An entry refused before the lineage was
learned is a hole a later pull fills (SIP-44 §The handover's holes), and
a replica
that learned a lineage SHOULD pull once from the lowest hole in each of
that origin's channels.

A client (the store scoped by exchange key, §Consumers other than pins)
MAY ask its exchange's
lineage, and SHOULD when it holds a channel whose origin is not its own
exchange (SIP-43) and no history for that origin; a client that moved
its pin by the operator's word (`sqex discover --replace`) has the same
gap and the same remedy.

### What an exchange holds another exchange's key for

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-66, via SIP-64.*

An exchange holds a key `K` *for a domain `D`* in each of these, and
each is a **holding**:

1. a channel it copies, whose origin is `K`, reached at `D` (SIP-35's
   `[[replicate]]`, SIP-43's shape, SIP-53's rehome hint);
2. an origin hint an account gave when it moved here: `(account, K, D)`
   (SIP-59 `Moving.origins`, SIP-60 `/peer/invited`);
3. an account's recorded home: `(account, K, D)` -- by the account's
   signed Move, or learned from the domain's exchange (SIP-60);
4. the lineage it learned for `K` (§Lineage);
5. the way it reaches `K`: an address and the domain, brought up on
   first contact.

A holding with no domain -- a key known by itself -- cannot follow, since
nothing says where to look; an exchange SHOULD record the domain
wherever it learns a key.

### Recognising a rotation

Whenever an exchange resolves `D` (SIP-33) to reach `K` and finds the
domain publishing a different key `K'`, it does not fail at once. It
takes `K'` as `K`'s successor when **either**:

- the resolution itself moved the pin from `K` to `K'` on a handover in
  the zone (§Resolution: `from = K`, `to = K'`, valid, within its
  window), or
- `K'`'s lineage (§Lineage, asked at `K'`) verifies for `K'` and `D` and
  names `K` among `K'`'s predecessors.

Both rest on the same two facts §Resolution requires: the zone says `K'` is
live for `D`, and `K` signed the way to it. The lineage adds only that
the second fact is fetched from the successor rather than read from the
zone, which is what makes a rotation followable after the window. An
exchange MUST NOT follow on the zone's word alone -- a domain that
merely publishes a new key has changed hands, not rotated -- and MUST
NOT follow on a lineage that does not end at the key the zone publishes.

A chain of rotations is followed in one step: a lineage `K → K1 → K'`
names `K` among `K'`'s predecessors, and the holding moves to `K'`.

### Following

On recognising `K'` as `K`'s successor for `D`, the exchange re-keys
every holding of `K` for `D` -- and, where the holding carries no
domain, every holding of `K` -- to `K'`:

- **Copies.** The channel's origin becomes `K'`; the earlier origin `K`
  joins the channel's origin history (SIP-53), so entries the copy
  already holds go on verifying under it. The exchange asks `K'`'s
  lineage (§When a replica asks) and keeps it, so what `K` receipted before verifies
  at a copy that never held it either.
- **Hints.** `(account, K, D)` becomes `(account, K', D)`.
- **Learned homes.** `(account, K, D)` becomes `(account, K', D)`.
- **Signed homes.** The account's Move named `K`; the record becomes
  `(account, K', D)` **with its signature cleared**, exactly as SIP-44
  clears the signature of a record it re-keyed on a handover the account
  did not sign: the exchange acts on it -- SIP-35's acts-for gate opens
  to `K'`, `/account/home` answers `K'` with `since = 0` -- and does not
  carry it (`/peer/moved` carries only a Move the account signed). The
  next Move the account signs, naming `K'` or anything else, replaces
  it.
- **Lineage.** What was learned for `K` is dropped; `K'`'s is learned.
- **Reach.** The way to `K` is dropped; `K'` is reached at the address
  the zone gave.

The follow is logged once with `D`, `K` and `K'`: it is the one time an
operator can see it happen.

### What does not follow

- A configured origin (`[[replicate]]`) is the operator's, and `predecessors`
  (§Consumers other than pins) is how the operator says a rotation happened; the
  exchange logs the mismatch and goes on refusing the pull until the
  configuration says otherwise.
- A relay-peer entry follows as §Consumers other than pins already says, on the
  zone's handover only; §Following does not extend it to the
  lineage, since a relay peer is an operator's listing and a listing
  is not a holding for an account's sake.
- A transport whitelist entry, as §Consumers other than pins says.

### Limits

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-64.* `MAX_LINEAGE = 16` links. A `Link` is at most 1 + 255 + 32 + 32 + 8 + 64
bytes; a `Lineage` at most 1 + 16 × that.

## Rationale

**Why a signature changes an analysis SIP-33 already made.** SIP-33 rejects
following a witnessed key because witnessing proves only co-publication, and
the zone alone controls that. A signature by the pinned key proves something
the zone cannot produce. The property this document rests on is that the two
failure modes require different secrets — the zone's signing key for the
`k=` records, the exchange's own key for the handover — and an adversary
holding one but not the other can do nothing a client will act on. SIP-33's
concern was a zone compromise; under this SIP a zone compromise is exactly as
harmless to an existing pin as it was before.

**Why DNS carries the statement and not the connection.** The obvious
alternative is a `/handover` route: connect, ask, verify. It was rejected for
v1 on two grounds. The client that needs the statement is the one whose pinned
key has been withdrawn, so it has no key to connect *to*; it would have to
connect to the unpinned successor to fetch the evidence for trusting it, which
is safe in principle (the statement is self-authenticating) but puts a
connection to an unverified peer on the path to a trust decision, for no gain.
And the RRset is already fetched, already validated, and already the thing the
client believes about this domain; a second channel would have to be at least
as trustworthy and would be at most equally so. A wire route remains a possible
later addition for a zone whose operator cannot add records, and the signing
input here is designed to be carried unchanged by one.

**Why chains are not walked.** `A→B→C` walked in one step would let a client
that has not resolved a domain in a year cross two rotations at once, which
sounds like a feature. It is not: each handover's `until` bounds its life for a
reason, and walking a chain means honouring a handover the operator published
and let expire, under a key they have since retired. Two rotations are two
resolutions; a client that missed both is in SIP-33's position and should be.

**Why the bound on `until`.** An unbounded handover is a permanent capability
to move every client to a key of the signer's choosing, and an exchange key is
a long-lived, online, high-value secret. Thirty days is long enough for the
slowest realistic client population and short enough that a pre-signed handover
sitting in a thief's pocket is worth little. It is a constant and not a tag on
the record because a signer that could choose it would choose it.

**Why exactly one.** Two valid handovers from the same key to different
successors cannot both be the operator's intent, and the difference between
"the operator is confused" and "one of these was signed by a thief" is
invisible to a client. A refusal that names every key is the honest answer.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-64:*

**Why the handover record and not a new statement.** A new "these were
my keys" record signed by the current key would prove only that the
current key says so. The handover is signed by the *retiring* key, and
a chain of them is a chain of consents: each key vouched for its
successor at the time. The current key cannot forge a link into that
chain without the old key, which is this document's whole property, kept.

**Why the current key is the anchor and not the oldest.** A verifier
knows the current key -- that is what it holds, discovered or named --
and knows nothing older; a chain is checked from what is known toward
what is not. Rule 3 is what makes a stolen old key inert here as it is
above: a thief holding a retired key can sign a link from it to any
key they like, and no verifier accepts it unless the thief's key is the
one it already holds for the exchange, which no lineage granted.

**Why `until` is not checked.** §The handover record bounded `until` for the signer's
protection during the window: a handover in the zone moves pins, and a
pre-signed one in a thief's pocket must be worth little. A link served
as history moves nothing; it lets a verifier read receipts under a key
that no longer signs anything. Checking `until` here would make every
lineage expire thirty days after the rotation, which is precisely the
population the lineage exists for.

**Why the exchange keeps the record rather than the zone.** The zone is
where a handover does its work and the operator withdraws it when the
window closes, as §Resolution tells them to. The exchange is the party that
signed it, holds the successor, and answers for the history; asking it
is asking the right party, and costs a round trip a peer is already
making.

**Why not walk chains in §Resolution.** §Resolution refused to walk
`A→B→C` on resolution because a pin should move one deliberate step at a
time and each step is a live key in the zone. A lineage is not
resolution: nothing moves, every key in it is retired but the last, and
the verifier's own key is the anchor. The two rules answer different
questions and do not conflict.

**Why the replica keeps what it learned.** An origin can be unreachable
later, or gone (SIP-53), and its copies still hold entries under its old
keys. A lineage learned once is as durable as the copy it verifies.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-66, via SIP-64:*

**Why the lineage counts as a handover.** §Resolution's rule needs
a handover in the zone because that is where a client, on its own,
could find one; it withdraws after the window because a pin must not
move on a stale record. A holding an exchange keeps for an account is
not a pin: the zone has already been resolved and says `K'`, and the
question is only whether `K'` is the same exchange. the chain above
answers that with `K`'s own signature, which is the signature §The handover
record carries, and the zone's currency is checked by the resolution
that raised the question. Nothing is trusted that §Resolution would not
have trusted; the window's purpose -- bounding what a stolen pre-signed
record is worth -- is served by rule 3 of §Verifying a lineage (the chain must
end at the key the zone publishes), which a thief with a retired key cannot
satisfy.

**Why a signed Move is re-keyed rather than left.** The account said
"this exchange is my home" and named it by the key it then had; the
exchange rotated, as §Producing a handover lets it, and the account's
client followed the pin without a word, as §Resolution requires. If the old home did not
follow too, the account's own choice would be undone by a rotation the
account never saw, and the only remedy would be a Move signed from a
client that believes nothing is wrong. Clearing the signature is SIP-44 §The handover's
precedent for exactly this shape -- a record the exchange acts on and
does not vouch for as the account's word -- and it means the re-keyed
record is never carried as if the account had signed it.

**Why not re-sign at the home.** The home could ask its accounts, on
their next connection, to sign a fresh Move naming the new key, and a
client SHOULD do that when it notices (SIP-59's `ensure_home` sees a
home record with `since = 0` and presents one). But the origins hold
the old record now, and a client that is not connected does not sign
anything; following at the origin is what keeps the account's copies
live in the meantime.

**Why one step for a chain.** A holding is looked at when it is used,
which may be long after several rotations; §Resolution's one-step rule is for
a pin moving in the zone, where each step is a key that was live. The
lineage is the whole chain, checked from the end, and there is nothing
to gain from walking to an intermediate key nobody answers for.

## Compatibility

Additive. A client predating this SIP parses the RRset, ignores the handover as
a record whose version it does not know, and behaves exactly as SIP-33
specifies — it works through the overlap window and refuses after it. A client
implementing this SIP behaves identically against a zone that publishes no
handover. Nothing on the sQUIC connection changes; the daemon's serving path is
untouched, and its only new surface is a command that signs and prints.

One implementation exists (sqex). The transport layer has two implementations,
but this SIP adds nothing squic-go or squic-rust carry — discovery and the pin
store live above the transport, in `sqex-discovery` — so SIP-1's rule that both
implementations agree does not bind here, as it did not for SIP-33.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-64.* Additive. An exchange from before §Lineage (0.81.0) answers `not_found`
and
a verifier proceeds with the predecessors it has, which is today's
behaviour; a verifier from before it never asks, and the
exchange's answer is never sent. A `Link` is §The handover record in fixed
fields; nothing about the record, its signing input or its resolution
rules changes. An operator who rotated before it gives the
exchange the record by hand; it is the line `sqexd handover` printed.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-66, via SIP-64.* Additive. An exchange from before §Following fails as it did before
-- the pin check, the key check -- and an origin or home from before
§Lineage serves no lineage, so a rotation after the window cannot be
followed to it, which is today's behaviour. During the window, the
zone's handover suffices and a successor serving no lineage is followed too.
Nothing on the wire changes.

## Security considerations

**Old key and zone compromised together is total, and unchanged.** An adversary
holding both publishes a successor, signs for it, and every client follows.
This is exactly the adversary who could already have run the exchange under the
old key indefinitely and served any record they liked; this SIP hands them
nothing they did not have, and nothing short of an offline authority could take
it away.

**A handover cannot be revoked, only outlived.** Once published it is in
resolvers' caches for its TTL and in any client that read it; withdrawing the
record stops new clients acting on it and nothing else. An operator who signs a
handover to the wrong key has committed to that key for up to thirty days, and
the only remedy is a second handover from the wrong key — which the operator
must hold. Sign carefully; `sqexd handover` prints what it is about to sign.

**A stolen old key can sign a handover and cannot publish one.** The thief's
statement is inert until it appears in the zone beside a matching `k=` record,
and the `until` bound limits how long a pre-signed one stays useful. An
operator who learns their key is stolen should rotate through this mechanism
*immediately*, because the same signature the thief could make is the one the
operator makes first — and once clients have followed the operator's handover,
the thief's names a `from` nobody holds any more.

**The handover is public and says a rotation is happening.** So does the
overlap window it accompanies; this adds the successor's identity, which the
window already publishes as a `k=` record. No new disclosure.

**A compromised pin store still wins.** SIP-33's note stands: an attacker who
can write the client's pin store installs any key they like and no rule in this
document is consulted. This SIP moves pins by rules; it does not protect the
place they are kept.

**What this does not do.** It does not authenticate the *first* contact, which
remains SIP-33's window and is unchanged. It does not help a client that resolves
a domain only after the window has closed. It does not rotate anything an
exchange has signed — receipts, SIP-38 assignments made under the old key's
authority — and does not claim to.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-64.* **A lineage grants nothing.** It lets a verifier accept, under an earlier
key, what it would already accept under the current one: receipts and
entries the exchange served. A forged lineage could at most make a
verifier accept a receipt under a key that never was the exchange's --
and to serve such a lineage the forger must hold the current key (rule
3) and a signature from the claimed earlier key (rule 1). Holding both,
they could have signed the receipt under the current key instead.

**A stolen retired key.** The considerations above say a thief with the
old key and the
zone is total and that a thief with the old key alone can sign and not
publish. Here a thief with a retired key can sign a link and serve it
only from an exchange whose current key a verifier already holds, which
means an exchange the thief runs and the verifier chose -- by discovery
of the thief's own domain, or by an account's Move naming it. What the
thief gains is the ability to present, under that exchange, entries the
real exchange receipted under the stolen key: entries the thief had to
have obtained as a member or a peer, presented to peers of the thief's
own exchange. That is the thief's own copy of what they could read,
served as their own history, and the same disclosure the member made by
choosing them.

**The lineage is public.** So was each link, in the zone, for up to
thirty days. What this adds is the duration, and that the rotation
history of an exchange is readable by anyone at any later time. An
operator who rotates because a key was stolen publishes, by this, that a
rotation happened on that date; §Resolution's window already did.

**Domain binding.** Rule 4 keeps a link signed for one zone from being
served as another zone's history by an exchange that holds the same key
for both, where the verifier knows which zone it came through. A
verifier holding a key alone accepts the domain the chain states, which
is the current key's own account of itself -- the same trust a receipt
already places in it.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-66, via SIP-64.* **What can move a holding.** A valid handover (§The handover record) from the held key,
and nothing else: the zone publishing `K'` without one is treated as a
different exchange and refused, as SIP-33 refuses a changed key. A thief
with a retired key can sign a link and cannot make the zone publish
their key; a thief with the zone can publish a key and cannot sign a
link from the retired one. Both together is the total compromise above,
unchanged.

**A re-keyed signed home opens the acts-for gate to `K'`.** That is the
point, and it is bounded as the original was: `K'` may pull what the
account may read, and the account chose `K`, which vouched for `K'`. The
record is marked as re-keyed (signature cleared) and is not carried,
so no other exchange takes it as the account's signature.

**A lineage is asked of the successor over a connection pinned to
`K'`.** The chain verifies under `K`'s signature and ends at `K'`; the
transport authenticates `K'`. A lineage served by anybody else does not
verify.

## Reference implementation

`sqex` 0.58.0; verification under predecessors in 0.64.1; 0.81.0, 0.81.1
(lineage); 0.83.0 (following); 0.97.1. Handover: the record -- `sqex-chat` reads
them from the pin store for its exchange and for any origin a channel names
(SIP-43), and `sqexd` takes them from `predecessors` in `[[replicate]]`. `sqex-discovery` parses the `v=sqex1h` record beside SIP-33's,
verifies it against the queried domain in the lookup (a failure is dropped,
never reported), and `decide()` gains `Moved` beside `Pinned` and `Changed`;
the pin-store entry keeps the moved-from key in its comment as history.
`sqexd handover --to <key> --domain <d> --days <n>` signs and prints the
record, refusing to run without an existing key, refusing its own key as the
successor, and capping `--days` at thirty. `sqexd`'s relay peer list follows a
handover for a domain it discovered a peer through. `sqex discover` shows the
handovers a domain publishes and what connecting would do; `sqex`, `sqex-chat`,
`sqex-voice` and sigil each say when a pin moved, as they already did for a
first contact.

Unit-tested for the positive case and four negative controls (a handover from
another key, a successor the zone does not publish, expired, signed for
another domain — the first shown to fail with its rule removed), the
pinned-still-published case, two successors, a duplicate, and a chain.

**Carried out for real on 2026-09-14**: `trunk.exchange` rotated from
`7tbBEPxK…` to `3kHiw569…` through this mechanism. A client pinned to the old
key kept its pin while both records were published, refused with a deliberately
wrong pin as the negative control, and — once the old `k=` was withdrawn —
moved its pin on the next connection with the notice above, then went quiet.
The squic.org exchange, holding the old key as a relay peer, discovered the
domain on the next cross-exchange call and rewrote its peer entry, logging
both the move and the follow; calls then crossed in both directions. The
operator sequence's step 4 was rewritten from what that run showed: the
daemon switch and the old record's withdrawal left a pinned client stranded
for the minutes between them. And the rule on client stores was added the
next day, when the operator's own chat client showed the rotated exchange
empty: its store was filed under the old key. sqex 0.58.3 follows on open,
and repaired that store — and two others on the same machine — on first use.

**Lineage** (§Lineage to §When a replica asks, sqex 0.81.0).
`sqex-proto::lineage::{Link, Lineage, MAX_LINEAGE}` with
`Lineage::predecessors_for(current, domain)` applying the four rules
(the signing input is §The handover record's, checked byte-for-byte against
`sqex-discovery`'s). `sqexd handover` appends the record it prints to
`lineage` beside the key file; the daemon reads that file on start,
verifies the chain ends at its own key, logs the count, and serves
`GET /exchange/lineage`. A replica asks before its first pull from each
origin and again on a repudiated entry, keeps what it learned in the
channel store (`lineage(origin, predecessor, position)`), folds it into
`take_under`'s predecessors, and pulls from the lowest hole once it has
learned something new. `sqex exchange lineage` shows an exchange's
chain. Tests: a rotated origin's pre-rotation entries are repudiated at
a home learned by hint and verified once the lineage is served; a link
whose signature fails, whose chain breaks, whose last key is not the
origin's, or whose domain is another's is discarded whole and the
verifier stays where it was; the daemon refuses to serve a lineage that
does not end at its own key.

**Following** (§Recognising a rotation, §Following, sqex 0.83.0):
`sqexd`'s `Server::reach_by` follows a rotation where a
domain names another key -- on the discovery's `moved_from`, or on the
successor's lineage naming the held key -- through `follow_exchange
(from, to, domain)`, which re-keys the registry's `home` (signature
cleared), `home_origin` and `learned_home`, the channel store's
`replicated` (with an `origin_history` row) and `lineage`, and the
forwarder; the home task and the telling use it through `reach_by`. A
configured origin is not followed. Tests: a home learned by hint goes on
pulling after the origin rotates and the window has closed (the lineage
path), and reads what the old key receipted; an origin holding an
account's signed Move admits the home's successor and answers
`/account/home` with the new key and `since = 0`; a domain that changed
hands without a handover is refused; a configured origin is not
followed.

Run live on 2026-09-18 against trunk.exchange, which had rotated on
2026-09-14 and whose handover record was still in the zone. The laptop
exchange of open peering's run, homed by the same account, refused the first
four entries of trunk.exchange's oldest channel as repudiated on every
pull while trunk.exchange ran 0.80.0; the record was written to
trunk.exchange's lineage file from the zone, and ten minutes after the
laptop's first ask -- the bounded re-ask a repudiated entry earns -- it
learned the earlier key and the next pull stored the four. `sqex
lineage` at trunk.exchange shows the one link, verified back from the
pinned key.

One finding beside the document. trunk.exchange's key is a systemd
credential, so the lineage file's default beside the key resolved to a
path nothing writes, and a missing file is legitimately "no rotation":
the first start looked healthy with nothing served. An operator sets the
path explicitly in that case, and 0.81.1's startup line says where the
daemon looked.

A second finding, five days later (2026-09-19, sqex 0.97.1). The lineage
section said "receipts and signatures" and the reference implementation
read it as receipts and entries: key envelopes went on being checked
under the current key alone, at the copy and in the chat client. The
account of trunk.exchange's own operator moved home to ex, ex pulled a
direct message trunk.exchange had ordered before its rotation, and
refused both of its envelopes on every pull -- 640 warnings in three
hours, and a copy with no key for its members. Envelopes now verify
under predecessors as entries do, the sentence above says so, and the
lineage test publishes one under the earlier key.
