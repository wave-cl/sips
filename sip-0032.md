## Abstract

SIP-31 signed channel entries and membership actions. A route-by-route audit of
the exchange afterwards — asking of each route *what does this let somebody
assert about somebody else* — found six surfaces still resting on the exchange's
word. Two of them are in the device registry and undercut SIP-31 itself: the
credential binding a device to an account is verified once and discarded, so the
second of SIP-31's two verification steps cannot be performed by anybody; and a
revocation carries no signature, so the undoing of a signed, portable credential
is not itself portable. This SIP closes those two, signs a channel's founding
constitution and its renames, makes a profile a versioned record in the shape
`sqns` already replicates, and lets a member name who handed it a channel key.
It also records what stays unsigned and why, including one thing it deliberately
corroborates rather than signs.

## Motivation

SIP-31 requires a verifier to perform two steps: check an entry's signature
under the device it names, then check a SIP-20 credential binding that device to
the account it names. It says so plainly, and says step one alone proves a key
signed and nothing about whose key it is.

**Step two is currently impossible.** `sqexd::device` verifies a credential when
a device registers and does not keep it; `POST /device/list` answers with
`device`, `added` and `not_after` — the exchange's summary of an artifact it
discarded. No route serves the credential. So every party verifying a SIP-31
signature is resting the attribution on this exchange's memory of having once
checked, which is the thing SIP-31 exists to stop being the answer.

The same registry has the mirror-image problem. A credential is a signed,
portable artifact that anybody can verify with the account key alone — and a
revocation, which withdraws it, is `Revoke { device }` over an authenticated
connection and nothing else. That asymmetry is a defect on its own: the
mechanism SIP-22 advertises as the thing "a portable credential structurally
cannot" do is itself unable to travel. It is worse in company, because a party
repeating the registry can suppress a revocation and the absence is
indistinguishable from a device never having been revoked.

SIP-31's security section says dating a signature against a revocation is hard
because `posted` is unsigned. That is true and it is not the sharp end: **there
is no signed revocation to date it against.**

The remaining four are smaller, and the test that gathers them into one document
is the same one. Not "is this important" but: *can a party holding a copy,
rather than a connection, check it?*

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian.

### The registry keeps the credential

`Devices` gains the credential it verified:

```
Devices | now: u64 | count: u16
        | count × (device[32] || added: u64 || not_after: u64
                   || cred_len: u16 || credential) |
```

An exchange **MUST** retain the credential a `Register` presented and serve it
here. A credential is 145 bytes plus its scope; SIP-22 caps an account at 8
devices, so a listing grows by under 1.3 KiB.

Nothing about verification changes — SIP-20 already specifies it — and no new
authority is granted, because a credential names both keys in the clear to
anyone who verifies one and SIP-22 already answers `List` to anybody for that
reason. What changes is that the evidence is reachable, so SIP-31's step two is
a check rather than an aspiration.

A verifier **MUST NOT** treat the registry's mapping as a substitute for the
credential. The mapping is what this exchange concluded; the credential is what
the account said.

### A revocation is a signed artifact

```
REVOCATION_CONTEXT = "sqnr-revoke-v1"

Revocation | account[32] | device[32] | issued: u64 | signature[64] |

signing_input = REVOCATION_CONTEXT || SHA-256(revocation without signature)
signature     = Ed25519(account_secret, signing_input)
```

Signed by the **account**, in the shape of SIP-20 and under a context in the
same family, because it is the same kind of statement — one identity speaking
about another, meant to be repeated — and because the authority that issued a
credential is the authority that withdraws it. Hash-then-sign over a fixed-width
input, so a hardware key signs 46 bytes.

The context string is `sqnr-` rather than `sqex-` for SIP-20's reason: an account
key is an sqnr identity and may be held in hardware. A revocation belongs to the
signer, not to the service that stores it.

Verification, in order, stopping at the first failure:

1. `revocation.account` is the account the verifier is asking about.
2. Ed25519-verify `signature` over the signing input under `account`.
3. `issued` is not in the future by the verifier's clock, allowing small skew.

`Revoke` carries one:

```
Revoke | type: u8 = 0x02 | revocation |
```

An exchange **MUST** verify it, store it, and serve it beside the device it
retires. A revocation travels the way a credential does, so suppressing one is
visible as a missing artifact rather than invisible as a non-event.

**Device-initiated revocation survives, and is not portable.** SIP-22 permits any
registered device of an account to revoke another, subject to seniority, and
permits a device to revoke itself — which is how a client signs itself out. A
device does not hold the account key and cannot produce the artifact above, and
the seniority rule rests on `added` times only the exchange holds, so a
device-initiated revocation would not be checkable by a third party even if it
were signed.

So there are two kinds, and an implementation **MUST** distinguish them:

- **Attested** — an account-signed `Revocation`. Verifiable by anyone holding the
  account key, with no reference to any exchange.
- **Local** — a device-initiated revocation, recorded by the exchange on its own
  authority. Correct here, and worth nothing to anybody repeating it.

A client recovering from a lost device **SHOULD** use the attested form, which is
the case SIP-22's security section already names the account key as the recovery
for. Signing out a client it still holds is the local form's proper use.

### A channel's constitution

Two events join SIP-16's system space, both written by the exchange and both
carrying the actor's SIP-31 action:

| `event` | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0x09 | `actor` created the channel under this constitution |
| 0x0a | `actor` changed the public name or topic |

```
CONSTITUTION = SHA-256( visibility(1) || retention_secs(4) || max_entries(4)
                        || name_len(1) || name || topic_len(2) || topic )
```

and the action's `arg` is that digest. **`MAX_ARG` rises from 8 bytes to 32.**
This costs nothing on any wire: `arg` is never transmitted. Both sides
reconstruct it — the exchange from the request it received, the client from the
request it sent — which is exactly what makes it a binding rather than a claim.

`created` is written before any invitee's `added`, so it takes the creator's
chain position 0. A channel now always opens with a signed event, including one
created with no invitees, which until now produced a channel whose origin nobody
had signed for at all.

`POST /channel/directory` gains an action and writes `renamed`. It records
nothing today, so a public channel could be renamed with no trace — a gap that
does not need a second exchange to matter.

`Retain` is left alone: it already signs `retention_secs || max_entries`
directly, and eight bytes is exactly what that needs. The constitution digest
therefore states the *founding* policy, and the retention events state every
change to it.

### Profiles are versioned records

```
PROFILE_CONTEXT = "sqex-profile-v1"

Record | account[32] | device[32] | serial: u64 | issued_at: u64
       | flags: u8 | name_len: u8 | name | title_len: u8 | title
       | avatar_len: u16 | avatar |

signing_input = PROFILE_CONTEXT || SHA-256(record without the signature)
wire          = Record || signature[64]
```

Signed by a **device**, chained to the account by SIP-20 — a profile is edited
far too often to want a hardware touch, and a linked device does not hold its
account's key at all.

`serial` is a monotonic counter and **the highest wins**. An exchange MUST refuse
a record whose serial is at or below the one it holds for that account, and a
reader comparing two records MUST take the higher.

**A counter, not a clock, and the distinction is the whole mechanism.** SIP-31
declined a client-asserted time for entries because a second clock beside
`posted` is a second answer to a question that already had one. Here there is no
sequence number to fall back on and "which of these is current" is the entire
question. A counter makes a stale record *lose*; a timestamp would only make it
look old, and two clients disagreeing about the time would disagree about the
profile.

`issued_at` is the subject's own clock and is advisory — for display, and for a
reader deciding whether a profile is stale. Nothing orders by it.

An exchange keeps its own `updated` clock for SIP-21's rate limit, which is
unchanged: ordering is by serial, rate limiting stays wall-clock, and the two do
not interact.

`Got` returns the whole signed record. A reader verifies the signature, then the
SIP-20 credential binding `device` to `account` — the same two steps, and the
same warning: the first alone says a key signed and nothing about whose.

**Blocking is untouched** and stays transport-authenticated. It is the exchange's
own access rule about who may reach whom, not a claim anybody repeats — and a
signed block list would be a portable, non-repudiable statement about somebody a
person wants nothing to do with, which is the opposite of what it is for.

### Envelope publication

Each SIP-17 envelope names its publisher and carries a signature:

```
ENVELOPE_PUB_CONTEXT = "sqex-envelope-v1"

signing_input = ENVELOPE_PUB_CONTEXT
                || SHA-256( exchange[32] || instance[32] || channel[32]
                            || epoch(4) || recipient[32] || from_epoch(4)
                            || to_epoch(4) || prekey_id(4) || ephemeral[32]
                            || SHA-256(ciphertext) )
```

The first three terms are SIP-31's, for SIP-31's reasons: an envelope must not
lift between exchanges or between incarnations of a channel whose identifier is
derived and therefore stable.

Per envelope rather than per `Put`, because `Get` serves one recipient the
envelopes addressed to it and must hand over something verifiable on its own. A
recipient can then answer "who gave me this key", which today it cannot — SIP-31
names this as its nearest residual gap and this closes it.

**`MAX_ENVELOPES` drops from 256 to 128.** SIP-17's arithmetic is 80 bytes of
header plus `16 + 32 × range` of ciphertext: 128 bytes at the current epoch, and
256 of those come to 32 KiB inside a 64 KiB request. The publisher and signature
add 96, taking an envelope to 224 and 256 of them to 56 KiB, which leaves no
margin against a body that must also carry its own header. Halving the count
restores it. A fully populated channel already needed several `Put` calls and now
needs twice as many, which is what the same-epoch `Put` exists to permit.

### Redaction is corroborated, not signed

`POST /channel/redact` stays unsigned. The removal is the exchange's own act — it
is the only party that can take the bytes — so a signature on the request would
attest that somebody *asked*, which the paired SIP-19 `Redact` body already does,
in the log, where a reader is looking anyway.

What changes is that the pairing becomes what a reader checks. A reader holding a
tombstone **MUST** look for a `Redact` body naming that entry, from an account
SIP-16 would have authorised — the entry's own author, or an admin — and report
three states rather than two:

- **Deleted**, corroborated by a signed entry from an authorised account.
- **Removed**, with nothing corroborating it: the exchange did this on its own
  authority, which it is able to do and which a reader should be able to see.
- Not deleted.

SIP-19 already tells a client to issue both, for a different reason. This makes
the absence of the second one mean something.

## Rationale

**Why the registry gaps outrank the rest.** The other four are about what a
reader is shown; these two are about whether SIP-31's signatures mean anything.
A signature verified under a device key is a fact about a key until something
binds that key to a person, and the binding was the one link in the chain that
nobody could check. Fixing presentation while attribution rests on an
unverifiable mapping would be building on the wrong floor.

**Why a revocation is account-signed and not device-signed.** Signing with a
device would keep every existing caller working and produce an artifact a third
party still could not act on: the seniority rule that makes a device-initiated
revocation legitimate is evaluated against `added` times only the exchange holds.
A signed statement whose authority cannot be evaluated is worse than an
unsigned one, because it looks like evidence. So the portable form is the one
whose authority is self-contained, and the local form is named as local.

**Why not sign the redaction request.** It was the obvious symmetry and it
attests to the wrong thing. The interesting question about a tombstone is not
whether somebody asked but whether the request was authorised, and SIP-16's
authority — the author, or an admin — is exactly what a reader can already
evaluate from the signed entries it holds. Adding a signature would put a second,
weaker answer beside a good one.

**Why profiles get a counter and entries did not.** A log has an order and a
record has a winner. SIP-31 refused a client-asserted time because entries are
ordered by the exchange and a second clock would be a competing answer. A profile
has no such order and needs a winner, which is what `sqns` records already do —
and `sqnsd`'s replication says why that shape works: a peer altering a record
breaks its signature, and a peer replaying an old one loses to the higher serial
already held. Reusing the shape imports the reasoning.

**What is deliberately still not signed.** Recorded so it is not re-derived, or
"fixed":

- The **beacon** (SIP-4) works precisely because it needs no signature.
- **Session ephemerals** (SIP-12): the two static Diffie-Hellman terms already
  prove possession of both identity keys.
- **Typing signals** (SIP-19): worthless a minute later, and an exchange that
  dropped every one would still conform.
- The **mailbox sender** (SIP-5): authentication signs inside the plaintext,
  where the exchange cannot read it.
- **Cursors**: `delivered` is the exchange's own observation, and `read` is an
  assertion — signing it would produce a signed assertion.
- **Blob attachment** (SIP-18): bookkeeping about retention and quota, not a
  statement about a person. The reference that matters travels inside a signed
  entry body and the blob is named by the hash of its ciphertext.
- **Channel destruction**: an exchange that will not serve a channel cannot be
  made to by any signature. That is denial, and denial and forgery want different
  remedies.
- **Room rosters** (SIP-13): presence is an observation of a live connection and
  evaporates in thirty seconds.
- **Prekeys** are already signed with the device bound into the input (SIP-23),
  and the reference client verifies one when it takes it.
- **`posted`, `seq` and `delivered`** cannot be signed by anybody; SIP-31 records
  the two alternatives it rejected.

## Compatibility

**A wire change; both ends move together.** `Devices` grows a credential,
`Revoke` carries an artifact instead of a key, the profile `Put` and `Got` carry
a record, and every SIP-17 envelope grows 96 bytes with `MAX_ENVELOPES` halved to
pay for it. `MAX_ARG` widens, which changes no bytes on any wire but changes
every action signing input that uses a longer `arg` — in practice only the two
new events.

Channel entries are untouched, so a deployment does **not** need its log wiped:
clearing the stored envelopes and profiles is sufficient. A member that already
holds its epoch key is unaffected; one that does not appears in SIP-17's
`Missing`, which is the mechanism that exists for exactly this.

Registrations survive with no credential attached, and an exchange **MUST**
report such a device as one whose credential it cannot produce rather than
inventing one or omitting the device. A client re-registers to supply it, which
SIP-22 already makes idempotent and calls renewal.

## Security considerations

**Retaining the credential publishes nothing that was private.** A credential
names both keys in the clear to whoever verifies it, and SIP-22 already answers
`List` to anybody on exactly that reasoning. What it does add is a durable,
portable artifact tying a device to an account, held by the exchange rather than
only by the device — so an exchange's stored registry becomes slightly more
useful to somebody who takes a copy of it. The mapping was already there; the
proof now is too.

**An attested revocation is non-repudiable, permanently.** It says an account
disowned a key, signed, forever, to anybody. That is the point, and it is also a
thing a person cannot take back: a device revoked in anger is revoked in a
document that outlives the anger.

**A local revocation looks like nothing at all to a third party.** This is
stated rather than solved. A client that signs itself out produces no artifact,
so a party repeating the registry cannot distinguish that from a device that was
never revoked — or from an exchange that suppressed an attested one and reported
the device as merely absent. Implementations MUST show which kind they hold.

**A profile's serial leaks how often somebody edits their profile**, to anybody
who may read it. It is a small disclosure and it is monotone: a reader can tell
that a name changed twenty times without being told what it changed from. The
alternative — ordering by an unsigned exchange timestamp — is what this replaces,
and it let whoever held the copy choose the winner.

**Signing a profile makes impersonation harder and does not make it hard.**
SIP-21's security section is right that the danger is a chosen name resembling
somebody else's, and a signature does nothing about that: it proves an account
chose the name, not that the name is not confusable with another account's. What
it buys is that the name cannot be changed by anybody but its subject, which is a
different and smaller property than the one a reader will assume.

**The constitution digest is a commitment, not a record.** It proves what a
channel's founding policy was to somebody who already knows the values and wants
them checked. It cannot reveal them: a reader who does not hold the name and
topic learns nothing from the hash, which is why the values continue to travel in
the clear alongside it for a public channel and remain absent for a private one.

**Envelope signatures make key distribution attributable, which cuts both ways.**
A member can now name who handed it a channel key — and so can anybody who
obtains that envelope, including an exchange operator producing evidence that a
particular admin admitted a particular person to a particular channel at a
particular epoch. The graph was already visible to the exchange (SIP-17 says so);
what is new is that a piece of it is now transferable.

## Reference implementation

Implemented in [sqex](https://github.com/wave-cl/sqex) v0.20.0.

- `sqex-proto::credential` gains `Revocation` beside `Credential`;
  `sqex-proto::profile` gains `Record`, following `sqns-core::record::Record`
  rather than inventing a shape; `sqex-proto::channel` gains `constitution` and
  the two events; `sqex-proto::channel_key` gains `sign_envelope` and
  `verify_envelope`.
- `sqexd` retains the credential, verifies attested revocations, writes
  `created` and `renamed`, orders profiles by serial, and verifies an envelope's
  publisher before storing it — the same reasoning SIP-31 gives for checking at
  the exchange what every receiver must check anyway.
- `sqex-chat` performs both of SIP-31's verification steps against credentials
  it fetches, signs its own profile records, names who published each key it
  collects, and distinguishes a tombstone somebody asked for from one nobody
  did.

**Three things the work turned up that were not in the design.**

`destroy` cleared six tables and not the SIP-31 chain, so a channel destroyed
and rebuilt under a derived identifier refused its own creator's next signature
as a broken chain — permanently, which is SIP-16's "goes silent for good" in a
place nobody had looked. The client had the mirror of it: `reset_sequence_space`
missed the same table. Both are fixed, and the client now compares the
incarnation before it signs anything rather than inferring a reset from a cursor
above the exchange's newest entry, which only fires after a fetch.

`Got` omits an envelope's recipient — it only ever answers one — so a recipient
verifying a publication signature has to substitute itself. Checking the zeroes
it arrives with fails on every honest envelope.

And the envelope cap had to halve for a sharper reason than margin: at 256, a
full `Put` of two-epoch envelopes is 65 576 bytes, over the 64 KiB request limit
outright. A test that asked only whether the narrowest case fits passed at the
old cap and said nothing — which a control caught it doing.
