```
SIP: 5
Title: Store-and-forward mailbox
Author: Colin Lyons <wave-cl@pm.me>
Status: Active
Type: Standards Track
Layer: Exchange
Created: 2026-08-24
Requires: 2, 3
```

## Abstract

An identity leaves a message for another at an exchange; the recipient collects
it when next connected. Both ends are authenticated by their connection, so
nothing is signed. The payload is sealed to the recipient with a fresh ephemeral
key, so the exchange handles ciphertext only and a later compromise of the
sender's key reveals nothing.

A person has several keys since SIP-20 -- an account and the devices
that act for it -- and the account's key may be one nobody connects as
at all. An exchange serves a device **its account's mailbox with its
own**: a device registered to an account (SIP-22) lists, fetches and
deletes what is addressed to the account as well as what is addressed
to itself. Sealing is untouched, and a device that collects what it
cannot open MUST NOT delete it.

## Motivation

Two identities that both know each other's keys still cannot exchange anything
unless they are online simultaneously, and sQUIC's silent-server property means
neither can even reach the other without prior knowledge. An exchange both can
reach solves the scheduling problem without either running a reachable service.

Naming is the same problem the beacon had: a mailbox belongs to an identity, and
the exchange must know which identity is acting without having registered it
first. SIP-3 supplies that, which is why this SIP now `Requires: 3` — the
original draft predated it and assumed SIP-2 alone.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-70.* The SIP-59 §Collecting mail live run sent two messages to a throwaway account at
trunk.exchange, and its device could not list them: the account had
handed over the day before (SIP-44 §The handover), so its key is a seed in that
device's store and connects as nothing. The messages are still there,
and will expire unread. That gap is older than the handover: a phone linked to
an account by a credential (SIP-20) could never see mail sent to the
account, because the mailbox knew only the key that connected. SIP-38 made
the account the thing a name resolves to, so a sender who looks
somebody up by name addresses their account, and a person reading on
their phone never sees it.

The registry already holds the answer to "whose device is this"
(SIP-22), and every other service consults it -- a device posts as its
account, reads its account's channels, is rung for its account. The
mailbox is the last service that does not.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. Every operation is bound to the caller's
transport identity (SIP-3): a **sender is whoever connected**, and a **mailbox
belongs to whoever can connect as its key**. A connection carrying no identity
has no mailbox and cannot send; it MUST be refused rather than served an empty
result.

### Sealing

```
shared = X25519(ephemeral_secret, recipient_x25519)
okm    = SHA-512(SEAL_CONTEXT || ephemeral_pub || recipient_x25519 || shared)
key    = okm[0..32]      nonce = okm[32..44]
ciphertext = ChaCha20-Poly1305(key, nonce, plaintext)
```

`SEAL_CONTEXT` is the ASCII string `sqex-mailbox-v1`. `recipient_x25519` is the
forward derivation of the recipient's Ed25519 identity — the conversion the
exchange cannot run in reverse runs perfectly well forwards.

The sender MUST generate a **fresh ephemeral keypair for every message** and
MUST NOT use its long-lived identity key in this exchange. The nonce is derived
rather than transmitted: a fresh ephemeral key makes the message key unique, so
there is nothing for a nonce to disambiguate. Both sides MUST reject a
non-contributory (small-order) exchange.

### Messages

```
Send        | type=0x01 | recipient[32] | ephemeral[32] | ciphertext      |
SendAck     | id: u64   | now: u64      |
List        | (empty body)              |
Listing     | now: u64  | count: u32    | count × Entry                   |
Entry       | id: u64   | sender[32]    | received: u64 | len: u32        |
Fetch       | type=0x03 | id: u64       |
Fetched     | found: u8 | sender[32]    | received: u64 | ephemeral[32] | ciphertext |
Delete      | type=0x04 | id: u64       |
Deleted     | deleted: u8               |
Status      | type=0x05 | id: u64       |
StatusReply | state: u8 | received: u64 | collected: u64 | now: u64       |
```

`state` is 0 unknown, 1 waiting, 2 collected. `sender` in an `Entry` or
`Fetched` is the identity **the exchange observed connecting** — see Security
considerations; it is not a cryptographic fact.

### Delivery

Delivery is **at-least-once**. A recipient lists, fetches, then deletes by id.
Fetching MUST NOT remove the message: a connection lost mid-collection must cost
a retry, never a message. Deleting is what completes collection.

Messages are a **queue**: `Listing` returns them oldest first by arrival.

### Collection is visible to the sender

Deleting drops the payload but MUST retain a record — id, sender, recipient and
the time of collection — until the message's TTL expires. A sender MAY ask
`Status` about a message **it sent**; any other caller MUST receive `unknown`,
which is the same answer given for a message that never existed.

### Collection by a device

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-70.* Where the caller's transport identity is registered as a device of an
account (SIP-22), and is not that account itself:

- `List` answers the items waiting for the **account** and the items
  waiting for the **device**, together, oldest first by arrival, each
  with its own id;
- `Fetch` and `Delete` by id act on an item in either queue;
- `Status` is unchanged: a sender asks about what it sent, and the
  caller's account has no bearing.

A caller registered to nobody sees its own queue, as today; an account
that is its own device likewise. Quotas (§Quotas) are per queue and
unchanged: an account's queue is one queue however many devices read
it, and a device's is its own.

Nothing on the wire changes. `Listing`, `Fetched` and the rest are as
above, and an item does not say which key it was sealed to: a client
that holds several keys opens with each, and §Sealing's AEAD says which
was right.

**What a device MUST NOT do.** An item a device fetched and could not open with any key it holds is
addressed to a key another device of the account holds. The device MUST
NOT delete it: deleting completes collection (§Delivery) and would take
from the device that can read it what it never read. A client SHOULD
say that such an item waits for the account's key, and where it holds
no such key, that a device which does can read it (SIP-44 §Entrusting the key).

### Quotas

An exchange MUST bound storage. This SIP fixes:

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| Plaintext per message | 32 KiB |
| Messages waiting per recipient | 64 |
| Stored bytes per recipient | 1 MiB |
| TTL (message and its collection record) | 7 days |

A send that would exceed a recipient's message count or byte allowance MUST be
refused and distinguishable from a malformed request. Because the exchange
cannot see plaintext, it enforces the size limit on **ciphertext**, allowing for
the AEAD tag.

The numbers are deliberately small. A mailbox is a rendezvous for messages, not
a file host, and the ceiling should make that structural rather than a matter of
operator restraint.

### Durability

*Added 2026-09-18.* The TTL above is a promise measured in days, and a daemon's
life between deploys is measured in hours; a mailbox that a restart empties has
not made the promise. An exchange MUST keep, across its own restarts and for
the item's TTL:

1. every waiting message -- sender, recipient, time received, ephemeral and
   ciphertext -- under the id it was answered with;
2. every collection record -- id, sender, recipient, time received, time
   collected -- as §Collection is visible to the sender requires;
3. the id sequence, so that no id answered before a restart is answered again
   after it: collection and `Status` are by id, and a sender asking after an
   old message must not be told about somebody else's;
4. what it has collected for an account from a former home (SIP-59 §Collecting mail) -- the
   origin, the id there and the recipient -- so a pull answered twice across a
   restart stores nothing twice. Such a queue is the account's queue at the
   home, and its devices read it as §Collection by a device has it.

Expiry is measured from `received` as before and applies across a restart: a
message received six days before one expires a day after it. Nothing on the
wire changes; a client cannot tell a durable exchange from one that never
restarted, which is the point.

An exchange run with state in memory only MUST still serve the mailbox, and
MUST say on `/status` (SIP-8) that its mailbox is not durable, so an operator
who reads it knows. A client is given no way to ask: an exchange that cannot
keep mail should not be public.

## Rationale

**Why seal to the recipient rather than trust the exchange.** The premise of the
whole stack: the exchange is a rendezvous point, not a confidant.

**Why an ephemeral sender key.** The draft left forward secrecy as "the main
cryptographic decision outstanding". Three options were weighed. Static-static
ECDH between the two identity keys is simplest and authenticates the sender in
the crypto, but has no forward secrecy in either direction — either key
compromised later opens every message. Published one-time prekeys (X3DH-style)
give real forward secrecy on both sides, at the cost of prekey upload, serving,
and exhaustion policy, and make the exchange trusted to hand out honest prekeys.
The ephemeral-sender construction was chosen: it costs nothing beyond one
keypair per message, keeps the sender's long-lived key out of the exchange
entirely, and bounds the recipient-compromise window by the TTL. Its price is
stated plainly below.

**Why fetch-then-delete rather than delete-on-read.** Delete-on-read is
self-cleaning but loses a message whenever the network fails between the
exchange sending and the recipient storing. Requiring an explicit delete makes
the failure mode a retry. The cost — a recipient that never deletes holds its
allowance until TTL — is bounded by the quota, which is why both exist.

**Why collection is visible to the sender.** This is a genuine trade, taken
deliberately. Knowing whether what you left was taken is useful for retry and
for not re-sending; the cost is that it discloses recipient behaviour. It is
scoped as tightly as it can be: only the sender of a specific message, only for
that message, and never as a way to enumerate. An exchange that considers even
this too much may report `collected` as `waiting`, at the cost of conformance.

**Why a queue rather than a set.** Order of arrival is information the exchange
has for free and a recipient usually wants; discarding it would be a choice, not
a simplification.

**Why durability is normative and not an implementation note.** The reference
implementation kept the mailbox in memory for its first year and this document
called persistence "the next step". On 2026-09-18 three deploys restarted both
public exchanges and each restart emptied every queue on them; two live runs
(SIP-59 §Collecting mail, and §Collection by a device) lost their messages between being sent and
being read, and the reason was in neither -- it was in a line of this one's
reference section. A second implementer reading the specification alone could
have done the same and conformed. What changed is that later documents built on
the queue -- a home collecting it, a device sharing it -- so the limitation of
one service became a hole under two.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-70:*

**What the exchange does not do.** It does not fold a device's queue into its account's, or an account's
into a device's: an item stays addressed to the key it was sent to, and
is listed to that key's holder and, where that key is an account, to
its devices. It does not re-seal, cannot, and does not say which key an
item is for; §Sealing binds the sender's choice of key into the
ciphertext, and that is where it stays.

**Why the registry and not a new grant.** A device is its account's by
the account's own signature (SIP-20), recorded at the exchange (SIP-22),
and consulted for everything else the device does in the account's
name. A separate grant for the mailbox would be a second thing to
sign, kept in a second place, for a service that is not more sensitive
than the channels the device already reads under the same binding.

**Why not seal to the devices.** A sender could look the account's
devices up (`/device/list`) and seal one copy to each; that is a
sender's choice today and remains one. Requiring it would make mail to
an account depend on the sender knowing the account's devices at the
moment of sending, and a device linked afterwards would never see it.
The account key is the stable address; SIP-44 §Entrusting the key is how the person decides
which devices can open what is sealed to it.

**Why no delete of the unopenable.** Collection is one act per item
(§Delivery is at-least-once, completed by delete) and the exchange
does not know which device can open what. If any device could delete
any item, a phone without the account key would clear mail the laptop
was going to read. The rule costs nothing a correct client would do
anyway -- a client deletes what it has read -- and it is the one rule
that makes shared collection safe.

**Why `Status` is unchanged.** A sender asked the exchange it sent to
about the item it sent; which of the recipient's devices collected it
is the recipient's affair, and the reply has no field for it (SIP-59 §What the home does with mail
says the same of a home's collection).

## Compatibility

New protocol; nothing to break. Requires SIP-2 and SIP-3 — the latter is what
lets an exchange serve identities it has not registered, which is the difference
between a mailbox anyone can use and one with a membership list.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-70.* Additive. An exchange from before §Collection by a device (0.86.0) lists
a device its own queue only; a client from before it opens with the one
key it has and, fetching an item sealed to the account, fails to open it
and -- if it follows §Delivery -- does not delete it, which is the rule
above. A client of that section at an old exchange sees no account items and loses
nothing.

## Security considerations

**The exchange sees metadata even though it cannot read payloads.** Sender,
recipient, size and time, for every message. Over a period that is a social
graph with timing. Sealing the payload does not conceal that anyone is talking
to anyone, and **this SIP must not be described as private communication.**

**The ciphertext does not prove who sent it.** This follows directly from the
ephemeral-sender construction: the sender's long-lived key is not in the ECDH,
so the recipient learns the payload but not, cryptographically, its author. The
`sender` reported alongside is the **exchange's observation of a connection** —
the same class of claim as SIP-4's `last_seen`, and equally dependent on the
exchange being honest. An application that needs end-to-end sender
authentication MUST sign inside the plaintext; this SIP deliberately does not,
because doing so would put a signature on every message for the benefit of the
applications that need one.

**Forward secrecy is one-sided.** A later compromise of the *sender's* identity
key decrypts nothing. A compromise of the *recipient's* key opens every message
still stored under it — which the TTL bounds, and nothing else does. Deployments
where that residual window matters want the prekey construction rejected above,
and should say so rather than assume this one covers them.

**Storage is attacker-reachable.** Anything that can connect can consume a
recipient's allowance, and under SIP-3 that is anyone. The quotas are the whole
defence: they are per-recipient, so filling one mailbox cannot deny another, and
a recipient can always recover its allowance by collecting. There is no
per-sender limit, so a determined sender can occupy a target's mailbox until
TTL; an exchange that needs more than this should gate sending on its own
policy.

**Collection timing is disclosed to senders**, by design — see Rationale. A
recipient cannot suppress it.

**Deletion is not erasure.** Collecting drops the payload, but the record that a
message existed, from whom and when, survives until TTL.

**Ciphertext at rest.** With §Durability the queue is on disk: a copy of the
disk is a copy of every waiting message for every recipient, sealed to each,
plus the metadata above. An operator's disk was already the log, the registry
and the prekeys, and the same care applies; the one-sided forward secrecy above
now bounds a recipient-key compromise on disk rather than in a process. An
exchange MUST NOT keep a payload past collection, and SHOULD overwrite rather
than merely unlink where its store lets it.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-70.* **What a device learns.** The senders, times and sizes of its account's
waiting mail -- the metadata the mailbox already shows the recipient, shown
to the recipient's devices. A device the account revoked (SIP-22) sees
nothing from the moment of revocation, since the registry no longer
binds it.

**What a device can do.** Delete what it cannot read, if it breaks the
rule above -- a hostile device of one's own account is a hostile
device, and it could already leave the channels it is in, post as the
account, or ring its correspondents; SIP-22's revocation is the remedy.
It cannot open what is sealed to the account without the account's
key, which is the point of sealing and unchanged.

**Nothing is re-sealed.** The exchange holds ciphertext it cannot read
and hands it to more callers than before, all of them the account's by
the account's signature. The set of keys that can open an item is
exactly the set that could before.

## Reference implementation

Shipped in [sqex](https://github.com/wave-cl/sqex) v0.4.0, on squic v0.15.0
(SIP-3):

- `sqex-proto::mailbox` holds the layouts above and the sealing. `sqexd` links
  that module but never calls `seal` or `open` — the exchange handles ciphertext
  only, and the code layout says so.
- `sqexd::mailbox` stores messages keyed by recipient identity, enforces the
  quotas, expires on every operation (no background task), and keeps the
  collection record after the payload is dropped.
- Routes: `POST /mailbox/{send,list,fetch,delete,status}`, each refusing a
  connection with no advertised identity.
- `sqex mail send|list|fetch|delete|status|collect`. `collect` fetches, prints
  and deletes each waiting message in order, deleting only once the plaintext is
  in hand.

Integration tests cover two unregistered identities exchanging a message without
ever being connected simultaneously, the sender seeing collection, a third
identity being unable to list, fetch, delete or ask after someone else's
message, anonymous connections having no mailbox at all, and oversized or
malformed sends being refused.

**Durability** (§Durability, sqex 0.87.0): the store is SQLite beside the
exchange's other stores (`mailbox.db`, WAL, `synchronous = FULL`), with the
payload columns set to NULL on collection and ids that never repeat; the
in-memory form is the same code over an in-memory database, and `/status`
reports `mailbox_durable`. Storage was in memory before that, and a restart
dropped undelivered mail. Tested: a message, its collection record and the id
sequence are there after the store is opened again, and a collected payload is
gone from the file.

**Collection by a device** (§Collection by a device, sqex 0.86.0, 0.86.1).
`sqexd`: `/mailbox/list`, `/mailbox/fetch` and
`/mailbox/delete` consult the registry and serve the caller's account's
queue beside its own (`Mailbox::list_for`, `fetch_for`, `delete_for`).
`sqex-chat mail list|read|delete`: reads through the chat store, which
is where an account key a device made or was entrusted with lives
(SIP-44 §Entrusting the key); `read` opens with the device's key and then the
account's, and says when neither fits; `delete` refuses an item it has
not opened. `sqex mail fetch` (the plain client, one key) says when an
item is sealed to another key rather than reporting it as damaged.
Tests: a linked device lists and reads mail sent to its account beside
mail sent to itself, and a stranger sees neither; an entrusted device
opens what a device without the key can only list, and the latter
refuses to delete it.

Run live on 2026-09-18 at trunk.exchange with the throwaway account of
the SIP-44 entrusting run, whose key one device holds: mail sent to the account and
mail sent to that device were listed together by it and read with the
right key each; the account's other device listed the account's item,
was refused the read and the delete, and the plain one-key client said
the item was sealed to another key; the holder collected both and the
sender's `Status` said collected. One finding: the client refused the
holder's delete too, because "opened" was kept in the process and a
command-line client is one command per process -- delete opens the item
first now (0.86.1). And the exchange's mailbox is in memory, so the
items of the SIP-59 §Collecting mail run did not survive the restart that deployed this;
that was this document's choice until §Durability, older than the
device rule.
