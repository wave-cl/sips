## Abstract

An account is its key, and today a key that is lost takes the account with
it: its devices, its names, its place in every channel. This SIP lets an
account name its successor **in advance**, under its own signature, so that
when the key is gone the successor presents that statement and the exchange
moves everything the account held -- device registrations, names,
memberships -- to the new key; and lets an account instead name **guardians**,
a quorum of whom may name the successor when no advance statement was kept.
Either way the exchange verifies and carries out; it decides nothing, and it
cannot move an account nobody signed for.

And an account may move to a new key while the old one is **still
held** -- a handover: it signs the same statement a will is and presents
it itself, with fresh credentials from the new key for the devices it
keeps, so the devices follow instead of being dropped and the new key
may hand over again. A device learns which account it is from the
registry and follows its own account's succession; a device that holds
the account key can entrust it to a sibling, when and only when the
person says so on the holding device.

## Motivation

SIP-40 gave an *exchange* key a way to hand over: the outgoing key signs for
its successor, and pins follow. SIP-28 records, of an *account* key, that the
same is not provided -- its successor pointer is set by whoever holds the
key, "which after a theft is the attacker", and "a future SIP that signs a
retirement with an offline key would" close the gap. SIP-22 lets an account
revoke a device, which recovers from a lost *phone*; nothing recovers from a
lost *key*, and a key on one device is the ordinary case.

The pieces exist. SIP-20 already has the account sign statements about other
keys; SIP-31 already carries signed statements through the log where members
and replicas verify them; SIP-27 already lets one identity state something
about another under its own signature, verified by anyone. What is missing is
the statement "this key succeeds me", signed while the account could still
sign it, and the rule for what an exchange does when it is presented.

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-62.* An account key is the one secret in this stack that never changes. A
device can be revoked (SIP-22), an exchange's key can be handed over
(SIP-40), a channel's key is rotated every time somebody leaves
(SIP-17); the account key is signed with for years and, if it is ever
suspected, the account is lost -- the will above is the answer to a key that
is *gone*, and this document says plainly that a stolen key is the end. A person who
would rather rotate their key every year than trust it for ever has
nothing, and neither does one who is moving from a software key to a
hardware token, which is a new key by definition.

The succession above built everything a handover needs except the
intent. A will is the
statement; the exchange knows how to carry a succession through names,
channels, blocks and resolution; SIP-31's log carries the entry that
every copy verifies under the old key's signature. What it could not
do, because its signer was gone, is keep the devices -- a credential is
the account's signature over a device, and only the new key can sign
one -- and it could not allow a second succession, because a will that
leaked after use would have moved the account again. Both are settled
by the old key being present: it signs the new credentials by proxy of
the new key it holds, and it presents the statement itself, at once, so
there is nothing left over to leak.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-67.* The first live handover (below) moved an account from one device. The
account's other device -- registered under the successor by the
handover, as the document says -- came back with a store whose account
was the old key, a credential the exchange no longer honoured, and a
direct message it could not find, because its identifier is derived from
a key the device thought it still was. Nothing had told it. A
correspondent's device learns of the succession from the log
(`succeeded`, above); the account's own device saw the same entry and
did nothing with it, because it was about itself.

The second half is older. Before the handover an account was a key on one
device and every other device held a credential, so one device could
sign a Move or a will and the rest could not; SIP-58 put the key on a
token so no device held it and a credential is all any device has. The handover
made the key a stored secret and stored it exactly once. Lose that
device -- the ordinary reason to want a handover -- and no device can
sign one; the will lodged under §The will is the only way out, and it was
signed before the last handover by a key that no longer counts. SIP-51
named a device-to-device carry as work for later; this is it. The
person, not the protocol, decides which devices hold the key: a phone
that holds it cannot be revoked out of the account by SIP-22, and that
is a trade the person makes knowingly, on the device that has the key
to give.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. Keys are Ed25519 public keys, 32 raw
bytes in signing input, base58 in text.

```
SUCCESSION_CONTEXT = "sqex-succession-v1"   (ASCII, 18 bytes, no terminator)
``` **Sibling**, the **session** and its
message framing are SIP-42's; **credential** is SIP-20's; **account key**
is the Ed25519 seed the account signs with.

### The will

An account signs, at any time while it holds its key, a **will** naming a
successor:

```
will_input = SUCCESSION_CONTEXT || 0x01 || account[32] || successor[32] || issued[8]
Will       | type: u8 = 0x01 | account[32] | successor[32] | issued: u64 | sig[64] |
```

`sig` is Ed25519 by `account` over `will_input`. A will is portable and
self-contained: anybody holding it can verify it with no record of the
account, exactly as a SIP-20 credential is. It is kept by the person -- on
another device, sealed on paper, wherever a key's backup would be -- and it
names a key whose secret must exist somewhere the account's does not, or the
two are lost together.

A will does nothing until it is presented. An account may sign several; the
one presented is the one that counts, and an exchange that has carried one
out refuses every later one for the same account (below).

### Guardians

An account that would rather not keep a successor's secret in advance may
instead sign a **policy** naming guardians and a threshold:

```
policy_input = SUCCESSION_CONTEXT || 0x02 || account[32] || threshold(1)
               || count(1) || guardian[32] * count || issued[8]
Policy       | type: u8 = 0x02 | account[32] | threshold: u8 | count: u8
             | guardian[32] * count | issued: u64 | sig[64] |
```

`1 <= threshold <= count <= 8`, guardians distinct and none the account
itself. A policy is kept as a will is, and MAY be lodged at the exchange in
advance (`Lodge`, below) so that the guardians and the successor can find
it when the account is gone.

A guardian, asked by the person, signs a **vouch** for a particular
successor:

```
vouch_input = SUCCESSION_CONTEXT || 0x03 || account[32] || successor[32]
              || guardian[32] || issued[8]
Vouch       | type: u8 = 0x03 | account[32] | successor[32] | guardian[32]
            | issued: u64 | sig[64] |
```

`threshold` vouches from distinct guardians named in the policy, all naming
one successor, are together as good as a will. A vouch is a guardian's word
about the person and nothing else; a guardian who vouches for the wrong
successor has done what a guardian can do wrong, and the threshold is what
bounds it.

### Presenting

The successor connects as itself -- the transport identity (SIP-3) MUST be
`successor` -- and presents:

```
Claim      | type: u8 = 0x04 | proof |
proof      = Will
           | Policy | count: u8 | Vouch * count
Succeeded  | now: u64 |
```

`POST /account/succeed`. The exchange MUST check: the connection's identity
is the successor every part names; the will's signature verifies under
`account`, or the policy's does and at least `threshold` vouches verify
under distinct guardians it names; `account` has not already been
succeeded; `successor` is not itself succeeded and is not `account`; and
`successor` has no registered devices (SIP-22) and no names (SIP-38) of its
own -- a succession carries an account into a fresh key, not into another
account's. Anything else is refused, and a claim from an identity that is
not the successor is refused identically to a malformed one: a will is a
statement about who may act, and presenting somebody else's proves nothing.

On success the exchange records the succession -- `account`, `successor`,
`now`, and the proof as presented -- and carries it out:

- **Devices (SIP-22).** Every device registered to `account` is removed, as
  though revoked, and `account` itself resolves to no account: a connection
  as `account` or as one of its devices is a client of nobody. Chat routes
  answer it with SIP-21 code `succeeded` (63) whose detail is the successor's
  key, so a client that still holds the old key learns where it went. The
  successor registers devices as any account does.
- **Names (SIP-38).** Every name held by `account` is held by `successor`,
  leases and administrator assignments alike, from `now`.
- **Channels (SIP-16).** In every channel `account` is a present member of,
  the membership row becomes `successor`'s, role and all, and the exchange
  writes a system entry:

  ```
  event = 0x0d  succeeded   subject = successor   actor = account
  ```

  whose body carries the will's `issued` in `chain_seq`, zeros in `prev`,
  and the will's `sig` in `sig`; or, for a succession by guardians, the
  policy's `issued` and the policy's `sig`, with the vouches served as the
  entry's `arg` is never served -- a reader that wants them asks
  `/account/succession`. A verifier of the entry (SIP-31 §system entries)
  checks the signature it carries under `actor` over the corresponding
  input, and a replica (SIP-35) moves the membership only when it verifies:
  an origin cannot tell a replica that Bob is now Mallory without Bob's
  signature. A direct message is a channel whose identifier derives from
  two accounts (SIP-16); it is carried like any channel and keeps its
  identifier, and a client that derives the identifier from the successor
  finds a different one. That was stated, not solved, until §A handover at a client settled
  it: a client finds the conversation among its channels before it
  derives.
- **Blocks (SIP-21).** `account`'s block list is `successor`'s.
- **Resolution (SIP-28).** The exchange sets `account`'s successor pointer
  to `successor`, reason `succeeded`, so a resolver that followed SIP-28
  alone is pointed on.
- **Keys (SIP-17).** Nothing. The successor's devices are new devices and
  hold no epoch key; they get one as any device linked after the fact does
  (SIP-17, SIP-22 §"Sealing to a newly registered sibling"), or by rotation.
  History on the old key's devices is where it was; a device re-linked under
  the successor becomes a sibling again and SIP-42 carries it.
- **Mailbox (SIP-5), prekeys (SIP-23), beacons (SIP-4).** Nothing: each is
  the key's, not the account's, and the successor has its own.

`POST /account/succession { account[32] }` answers, to any identified
caller, the recorded succession -- `successor`, `now`, and the proof -- or
SIP-21 `not_found`. `POST /account/lodge { Policy }` keeps a policy at the
exchange ahead of need, from the account itself, replacing an earlier one;
`POST /account/lodged { account[32] }` hands it to any identified caller. A
claim by guardians always carries the policy: the exchange verifies what it
is shown, and a lodged copy is a convenience for finding it, not a record
the exchange vouches for.

### Chains

Succession does not mint an entry under the old key and does not touch its
SIP-31 chains: the old key's devices signed what they signed, and the
successor's devices start chains of their own, as any new device does. A
succession entry is the exchange's, receipted like any system entry, and
its authority is the signature inside it.

### The handover

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-62.*

```
Handover | type: u8 = 0x05 | Will | count: u8 | count × Credential |
```

`POST /account/handover`, presented by the account itself -- the
connection's identity (SIP-3) resolves (SIP-22) to `Will.account` -- or
by one of its registered devices. `Will` names the successor under the
account's own signature, exactly as §The will has it; a will signed for
a succession and a handover's will are one statement, and either may be
kept for the other purpose. Each `Credential` is a SIP-20 credential
signed by `successor`, scope chat, naming a device that is currently
registered to `account` (or `account` itself, which SIP-22 makes its
own device); at most `MAX_DEVICES` of them. The exchange MUST check:
the will verifies under `account` and names a `successor` that is not
`account`, has not been succeeded, and has no devices, no names and no memberships
here -- a key that sits in a channel is an account already, which
the first draft's checks did not say and this section adds to both
routes;
`account` has not been succeeded; every credential verifies under
`successor` at the exchange's clock and names a device of `account`.
Anything else is refused with SIP-21 code `not_yours` (46), as a claim
is.

On success the exchange records the succession as §Presenting records
one
-- `account`, `successor`, `now`, the will as the proof, so that
`/account/succession` answers for it and a copy that pulls the entry
can check it -- and carries it out as §Presenting carries a succession out,
with these differences:

- **Devices.** Every device of `account` is removed as §Presenting
  removes them, and then each device a credential was carried for is registered
  to `successor` under that credential, in the same transaction, so a
  connection from any of them resolves to `successor` from the next
  request on. A device with no credential carried is gone, as §Presenting
  has it; the account chose not to keep it. A device kept is not
  revoked: its old credential is retired with the old key, and its new
  one is what it holds.
- **Homes (SIP-59).** Where the exchange holds a Move for `account` --
  as its home, or as an exchange it lives away from -- the record is
  re-keyed to `successor`, with its signature cleared: the statement
  stands, since the account that made it is this one, but a signature
  under the old key is not a signature under the new, and the exchange
  MUST NOT carry a cleared one to anybody. `/account/home` answers a
  re-keyed record with `since = 0`, so a client of the successor signs
  a fresh Move on its next connection (SIP-60 §A client says where it
  lives) and the record is whole again. The origin hints follow with
  the record; a learned home (SIP-60) follows likewise.
- **Once more, later.** `successor` is a fresh account that may itself
  hand over, or be succeeded by will or by guardians. Each key is
  succeeded at most once, which is the rule above and what makes the
  chain a chain: `A` to `B` to `C`, each step signed by the key it
  leaves, each answerable at `/account/succession` under the key it
  names.

Names, channels (the `0x0d` entry carrying the will's signature),
blocks and the SIP-28 successor pointer are exactly §Presenting's. Mailbox,
prekeys and beacons are the key's and are not carried; the successor's
devices publish fresh prekeys, which they hold the secrets for, on
their next connection, and a sender who took a prekey of the old key
before the handover sends to a key that can still open it -- the
devices did not change.

### A handover at a copy

A replica pulls the `0x0d` entry as §Presenting has it and moves the seat
when the entry verifies under the actor. It MUST also re-key what it
holds of the account under SIP-59 and SIP-60 -- a Move naming it or
another exchange, its origin hints, a learned home -- with the same
signature-clearing rule, so that a home's pulls for the successor are
served where the account's were, and an origin that told the old key's
home tells the new key's.

### A handover at a client

A client of the account that hands over holds the new key or does not.
Where it does -- it made the key, was given the file, or was entrusted it
(§Entrusting the key) -- it signs the
credentials and presents the handover, and from then on acts for the
successor as a linked device acts for its account (SIP-22): the device
key is unchanged, the account is the new one, and the store is opened
by the device key as before. Where the account is held elsewhere (a
token, SIP-58), the person signs the will and the credentials there and
hands them to the client to present.

A client that reads a `succeeded` entry for a correspondent -- in any
channel they share -- SHOULD carry its **contact** across: the same
person, the same label, the new key. A direct message whose party
changed key keeps its channel: the exchange moved the seat, and SIP-16's
derivation no longer names the channel, which makes it an ordinary
private channel of two admins and changes nothing about reading or
writing it. A client MUST find the conversation with a correspondent
among the channels it is in before it derives an identifier for a new
one, so that a rotation does not open a second conversation beside the
first. This settles what §Presenting stated and did not solve.

### Which account a device is

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-67, via SIP-62.*

```
Whose | account[32] | device[32] |
```

`GET /device/account`, from any identified caller, answers with the
account the caller's transport identity is registered to (SIP-22), and
the identity itself: the caller's own key twice where it is registered
to nobody, since an account with no linked device is its own device
(SIP-22). Never `not_found`: every key is an account until the registry
says otherwise.

A client MUST ask this on connecting, before it signs anything, and
where the account answered differs from the one its store names, it
MUST follow before going on:

1. the store's account becomes the one answered, and so does the
   client's;
2. its credential becomes the one the registry lists for its device
   under that account (`/device/list`), which the handover registered
   (§The handover) -- or none, where the device is now the account itself;
3. every direct message it holds whose identifier derives from the old
   account and a correspondent is aliased (§A handover at a client) to that
   correspondent, so the conversation is found under the new account;
4. a Move it would sign (SIP-59 `ensure_home`) is signed by the account
   key it holds, if it holds one, and otherwise left to a device that
   does. A key it kept whose public key is not the account answered is
   a retired account's and MUST NOT sign anything: a Move it signed
   would be valid, for an account that no longer is one.

This is what the device that presented the handover does for itself
(§A handover at a client); this section has every device do it, from the
registry's answer rather than from its own act. A device answered an
account it has never heard of is in the same position as one linked
this minute: it is that account's device, and its store follows.

### Entrusting the key

The SIP-42 session gains one message:

```
Key | type = 0x08 | seed[32] |
```

`seed` is the account's Ed25519 seed. A device MUST NOT send it unless
the person asked, on that device, for this sibling to hold the key; a
device MUST NOT send it before the sibling's `Hello` has been verified
as SIP-42 §The exchange step 1 requires; and a device that does not
hold the key has nothing to send. It is sent once, after `Hello` and
before `Done`, and needs no answer.

A device receiving `Key` MUST check that the public key of `seed` is its
own account (as `Hello` established it, and as `/device/account` said)
and end the session on anything else; it then keeps the seed as the
handover keeps the successor's, sealed in its store, and from then on holds the
account key: it signs Moves, wills, credentials and handovers as the
device that made the key does. It SHOULD tell the person, once, that this
device now holds the key and what that means (below). A device already
holding the key takes a `Key` naming the same one as nothing.

The frame carrying `Key` is sealed as every SIP-42 frame is; the seed is
in the clear inside it, as SIP-42's epoch keys are, and for the same
reason: the session is between two devices of one account, both
verified, and the exchange relays ciphertext.

### What holding the key means

SIP-22 lets an account revoke a device: a lost phone that held a
credential is out of the account the moment the account says so. A
device that holds the account key is not put out by revocation -- it
*is* the account, and can sign a new credential for itself, a Move
anywhere, a handover to a key of a thief's choosing. The remedy for a
lost device that held the key is a handover (§The handover), signed from
another device that holds it, which is why a person entrusts the key to a second
device at all: so that there is another. A client MUST say this when a
person entrusts the key, and MUST NOT entrust it without the person
saying so on the holding device; a sibling cannot ask for it.

## Rationale

**Why the will is signed in advance and not at the moment.** At the moment
the key is gone. Everything that can be signed under the old key has to be
signed while it exists, and a will is the smallest such thing: one key,
signed once, kept anywhere. It is SIP-40's handover with the exchange's role
reversed -- there the signer publishes and clients follow; here the signer
keeps and the exchange follows.

**Why guardians are a quorum and not one person.** One guardian is a second
key that can take the account, which is a will with the successor chosen by
somebody else. A threshold of several is the property a person wants from
"my friends can get me back in": no one of them can, and the person chooses
how many it takes.

**Why the exchange verifies rather than deciding.** An exchange that could
move an account on its own judgement could move it on anyone's request that
it found persuasive, and could be compelled to. Here it moves an account on
a signature it can check and on nothing else; the record it keeps is the
proof it was shown, so a member who doubts a succession asks for it and
checks it themselves.

**Why the succession is an entry in each channel.** The same reason SIP-35
puts replication authorisation in the log: the members are the ones it
happens to, and a membership that changed by a table update the log did not
record would be a roster a replica could not derive and a history a reader
could not account for. The entry carries the will's signature so that it is
checkable by anybody holding the log.

**Why the successor must be a fresh key.** Merging two accounts -- their
names, their channels, their devices -- has no single right answer and
several wrong ones; a succession into a key that already is an account
would have to pick one. A fresh key has nothing to merge.

**Why direct messages are not re-derived.** A direct message's identifier
is a fact about two keys, and SIP-16 made it so on purpose. Re-deriving it
would mean an exchange rewriting channel identifiers, which every client's
store is keyed by. The cost is one conversation that shows up under an old
identifier; the alternative was a rule in every client's store.

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-62:*

**Why a will and not a new statement.** It is the same fact -- this
key names that one as its successor -- and §Presenting already has every
copy verify it under the old key inside the entry. A person who signed
a will for the day their key is lost may present it themselves on the
day they decide to rotate; the exchange does the same thing either way,
and does more only because the signer is there to give it more.

**Why the devices follow by credential and not by fiat.** A device is
registered under a credential, SIP-32 keeps it so that copies can bind
the device's signatures to the account, and a credential is the
account's signature. The exchange could re-point the rows; the rows
would then carry credentials naming the old key, and every verifier
would be right to refuse them. The new key signs; the exchange
registers what it signed.

**Why the home records are re-keyed with the signature cleared.** The
alternative is to drop them and have the successor start from nothing:
its home would stop pulling, every origin would close its gate, and the
person's copies would go dark until they presented a new Move at each.
Re-keying keeps everything serving; clearing the signature keeps the
exchange honest about what it holds -- it will act on the record, and
it will not carry it, until the new key has signed one.

**Why once per key.** A chain where each link is signed by the key it
leaves is checkable link by link; a key that could be succeeded twice
would be a fork, and the second succession a way to steal an account
from its successor with the old key. The reason above holds for the
living case exactly.

**Why the conversation keeps its channel.** A direct message's
identifier is derived so that two people find one channel without
speaking. Once they have it, the identifier's job is done; the channel
is the conversation, the seats were moved by an entry both can verify,
and re-deriving would throw the history into a channel nobody reads
and start an empty one. The client's rule -- look among your channels
before you derive -- is the same rule that makes returning to a
conversation find it.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-67, via SIP-62:*

**Why the registry says which account a device is.** The device's own
store is what went stale, and the log's `succeeded` entry is about the
account, not the device; several devices see it and each would have to
work out that it means itself. The registry is the one place the
handover wrote the new binding (§The handover registers the kept devices
under the successor in the same transaction), and it already answers "whose
device is this?" for every route (SIP-22). Asking it once on connect is
one round trip and the answer is authoritative.

**Why follow from the registry rather than the entry.** A device that
was off during the handover and comes back after the channel's
retention window never sees the entry; the registry still knows. And a
device answered an account it never heard of behaves exactly as a device
linked a moment ago, which is the shape SIP-22 already has.

**Why over SIP-42's session.** It is the one channel two devices of an
account already have that the exchange cannot read, and it already
carries epoch keys in the clear inside sealed frames on the same
reasoning. A new route at the exchange would put the account key in the
exchange's hands; a QR code or a file is what a person does when there
is no session, and remains available.

**Why the holder decides and the sibling cannot ask.** The key is the
account; whoever holds it holds everything. A request from a sibling
would be a request from whatever device holds that sibling's key, and
a stolen phone with a valid credential could ask. The act is the
person's, made where the key is, and it is deliberate: one device, one
time, named by key.

**Why not automatic on every sync.** Then every device would hold the
key, and SIP-22's revocation would protect nothing. A person with a
phone they might lose and a laptop they will not wants the key on the
laptop and a credential on the phone, which is what SIP-20 was for.

**Why the key and not a fresh handover to a shared key.** A handover
changes the account's key for every correspondent; entrusting
changes nothing anybody else sees. They compose: a person who loses the
laptop hands over from the phone that was entrusted.

## Compatibility

Additive: three routes and one system event. An exchange from before this
document refuses the routes with SIP-21's `not_found`; a client from before
it ignores event `0x0d` under SIP-19's rule and sees a member it did not
know appear with the old one's role, which is what happened. SIP-28's
successor pointer is set as a courtesy to resolvers that read nothing else.

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-62.* Additive: one route. An exchange from before §The handover (0.79.0)
refuses it `not_found`; the account rotates by `/account/succeed`
instead, losing its devices and re-registering them, and cannot hand
over again. A client from before it sees a succession as §Presenting
has it -- a member it did not know in the old one's seat -- and,
deriving a fresh identifier for the correspondent, opens a second
conversation, which is what the first draft said would happen and
§A handover at a client avoids.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-67, via SIP-62.* Additive. `GET /device/account` is new; a client before §Which account a
device is never asks and behaves as today. `Key` is a new SIP-42 message type; a
device from before §Entrusting the key (0.84.0) that receives one MUST
end the session as
SIP-42 says of an unknown type, and a holder SHOULD not send it to a

learns the sibling is older, and says so.

## Security considerations

**A will is as sensitive as the key it names.** Whoever holds the will and
the successor's secret holds the account, from the moment they present it;
the will alone holds nothing. Keep them apart, or keep the will with the
person who will hold the successor.

**A stolen account key can sign a will.** A thief who holds the key holds
the account already, and a will they sign moves it to a key of theirs. This
SIP changes nothing about a stolen key; SIP-22's revocation is for a stolen
device, and a stolen account key was always the end. What a will adds is
that the person, having kept one, moves first.

**A guardian is a trust the person placed.** A quorum of guardians can move
the account to a key of their choosing. That is the arrangement, chosen by
the person and bounded by the threshold they set; a person who trusts no
quorum keeps a will instead, or neither.

**A succession is irreversible at the exchange**, and deliberately: an
account succeeded once cannot be succeeded again, so a thief who obtains a
will after the person has used it gains nothing, and a person who is moved
by their guardians against their wishes has the recourse a person always
has against those they trusted.

**What the exchange learns.** Who succeeds whom, which it must to carry it
out. The will's existence is disclosed only when presented; a lodged policy
discloses the guardians to the exchange, which is the price of the
guardians holding nothing.

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-62.* **A stolen key can hand over.** As it could sign a will, and as it
could do everything else. The difference from a succession is timing: a thief
holding the key today moves the account today, where a will they found
would have waited. What a handover gives the person is the same move,
first -- and once made, the old key is nobody's, so a thief who obtains
it afterwards obtains nothing.

**The credentials are the new key's word, verified.** A handover
carrying a credential the successor did not sign is refused whole; one
naming a device the account does not have is refused whole. The
exchange registers nothing it did not check.

**What copies learn.** What §Presenting already disclosed: who succeeded
whom, in every channel they share. A re-keyed home record discloses
nothing new; it was the same person's.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-67, via SIP-62.* **The registry's answer is trusted as SIP-22 already trusts it.** The
exchange says whose device this is on every request; this route says it
to the device itself. A dishonest exchange could answer another account
-- and could already refuse the device's posts, register it to nobody,
or serve it any log it liked. What the device does with the answer is
re-key its own store, which affects what it signs next; an exchange that
lied would find those signatures refused by every honest verifier,
which is SIP-31's check, unchanged.

**The seed crosses one sealed session between two verified devices.**
SIP-42's admission -- a credential from the account for exactly the key
the session was opened to, current at the exchange -- is what stands
between the seed and a stranger, and SIP-12's key agreement is what
stands between it and the exchange. A device that would accept `Key`
from a session it did not verify has broken SIP-42 first.

**Entrusting is irreversible except by handover.** Said above and to be
said to the person. A client SHOULD keep a note of which devices were
entrusted, so that the person deciding on a handover after a loss knows
whether the lost device held the key.

**A stale device signs under a retired key.** A device that skipped the
registry's answer and went on as the old account would sign entries
naming an account the registry no longer binds its key to; every
verifier refuses them (SIP-31 step 2). The rule that a client MUST ask
before signing is what keeps that from being the first thing it does.

## Reference implementation

`sqex` 0.65.0; 0.79.0–0.79.3 (the handover); 0.84.0, 0.84.1 (among its
devices). `sqex-proto::succession` (the will, policy, vouch, claim, and
their verification); `sqexd` records a succession in its device registry
and carries it through devices, names, channels, blocks and resolution;
`EVENT_SUCCEEDED` in `sqex-proto::channel`, derived by replicas only under
a verifying signature; `sqex succession {will,policy,vouch,claim,show}`;
clients word `succeeded` and the system entry. Tests
(`succession_flow`): an account is carried to its successor by its will,
and guardians carry one as a quorum with a replica following. *(This
section said "Planned" from 2026-09-17 until the fold below; the code
had shipped the same day.)*

**The handover** (§The handover to §A handover at a client, sqex 0.79.0).
`sqex-proto::succession::Handover`; `sqexd` verifies a
handover and carries it out through §Presenting's path with the devices
registered under the carried credentials in the same transaction, re-keys
the SIP-59/60 home records with the signature cleared (at the origin, and
at every copy that pulls a verified `0x0d` entry -- for successions by
will and by guardians too), answers `/account/home` with `since = 0` for
a re-keyed record and takes any Move the new key signs over it. Both
routes now refuse a successor that is a member of anything here.
`sqex-chat handover` makes the new key and keeps it sealed in the store
(or presents a will and credentials signed elsewhere), acts for the
successor on the same device key, and remembers its direct messages by
the new key; a client reading a correspondent's `succeeded` entry
re-keys the contact and the conversation. Tests: the device, name, seat
and home record follow and the new key hands over again while the old
one cannot; a stranger, a forged credential and an existing account are
refused; a copy follows from the log; and at the client, a sealed direct
message keeps its channel on both sides across the rotation, with a
restarted client signing a fresh Move with the key it kept.

The first live handover turned up a fault older than it: a
copy performing SIP-31's step 2 asked the origin's registry by the
*device* key, which SIP-22 lists by account, and so found nothing for
any linked device and refused every entry one signed -- unnoticed,
because until SIP-59 no test read a linked device's post at a copy, and a
handover makes every account's own key a linked device of the new one.
Fixed in 0.79.1, along with SIP-35 §Waiting's wait, which keyed on what the copy
had stored and so fired at once, for ever, on an entry the copy refused;
it now keys on the highest position the copy was shown. And the entry
the copy had refused was below what it held, so no later pull would ever
ask for it: 0.79.2 has a replica note the holes a pull leaves and the
ones it finds in its store on starting, pull from them, and give up on a
hole after `HOLE_TRIES` pulls. The client had the same hole for the same
reason -- its fetch cursor had passed the position while the copy still
refused it -- and 0.79.3 has it ask once, per channel per run, from the
first gap in what its store holds.

**Among its devices** (§Which account a device is, §Entrusting the key,
sqex 0.84.0): `sqex-proto::device::Whose`; `sqexd` answers
`GET /device/account` from the registry. `sqex-chat`: `Chat::whose`
asks it, `Chat::follow_account` re-keys the store, credential and direct
messages, run on every start before `ensure_home`; `sync::Message::Key`
and `Sync::entrusting(seed)`, which queues the key once the sibling is
admitted; a receiver checks the seed against its account and keeps it
sealed; `sqex-chat device entrust <device>` meets that sibling and gives
it the key, saying what that means; the entrusted device's store
records it. Tests: a linked device with a stale store follows the
handover on its next start and finds the direct message; a device
entrusted with the key can sign a Move and hand over; a key for another
account ends the session; a sibling cannot obtain the key by asking.

Run live on 2026-09-18 at trunk.exchange with a throwaway account and
two devices. The account was handed over from the device that held the
key; the other device, started with its stale store, asked whose device
it was and followed (`this device's account is now …; the store
followed`). The holder then entrusted it the key over a sibling session
the exchange relayed; the entrusted device handed the account over
itself, and the first device -- now the stale one -- followed on its
next start. Two findings. The first device still held the retired
account's seed and would have signed a Move with it, a valid Move for a
dead account: a client now uses a stored seed only where its public key
is the account the device is (0.84.1). And the history trade that
follows the key ended on a fault of its own (SIP-42, an incarnation the
exchange no longer serves), which the holder reported as the entrusting
failing; the key is reported on its own line now, and the trade's end
separately.
