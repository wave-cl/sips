## Abstract

Every entry a member posts to a channel, and every membership change an actor
causes, carries an Ed25519 signature by the **device** that caused it, and each
device's contributions to a channel form a hash chain. Until now the author of
an entry was the exchange's observation of a connection, which SIP-16 states
plainly is not a cryptographic fact — so an entry repeated to anybody who did
not witness that connection carried no proof of who wrote it, and any member
could forge one. This SIP supplies the proof, and the chain makes a dropped or
rewritten entry visible rather than silent. It buys the ability to replicate a
conversation to a party that must verify rather than trust, and it pays for that
with the deniability the design had before it, which is not recoverable.

## Motivation

SIP-16 says it itself. An entry's `account` and `device` are *"both the
exchange's observation of the connection that posted — the device from SIP-3,
the account from the registry — and neither is a cryptographic fact"*.

The seal does not fill the gap and is not meant to. SIP-17 derives a per-sender
subkey so that several senders may share one channel key without sharing a nonce
space, and it is explicit that **every member can derive every other device's
subkey from the entry header**. That is a distribution convenience, and its
consequence is that a valid ciphertext under Bob's subkey proves only that
*some* member produced it. Any member holding the channel key can mint a
well-formed entry attributable to any other member's device, at any `msg_seq`
that device has not used.

The only thing standing in the way is that the exchange stamps the header from
the connection it observed, and refuses to write Bob's device onto Alice's
connection. That check is real and it is enough while the entry stays where it
was written. It is worth nothing the moment the entry is repeated: to a second
exchange, to an archive, to a restored backup, to a client migrating its store.
There is no connection left to have observed anything.

SIP-27 already set the test for when this stack signs, and this is the case it
describes:

> An attestation is meant to be repeated to third parties who never saw the
> connection, so it must carry its own proof. This is the one exchange feature
> where transport authentication is insufficient.

SIP-20 concedes the same from the other direction. Of the delegate key in a
credential it says *"whether the holder of it is present is a question the
transport answers"*. Correct, and the whole difficulty: an entry outlives the
transport, and there is then nobody to ask.

Signing one entry is not sufficient on its own, and a design that stopped there
would mislead. A signature makes a single entry unforgeable and does nothing
about an exchange that drops entries, reorders them, or shows one log to one
party and a different log to another — every entry in every version verifies.
Hence the chain, and hence signing membership changes as well as messages,
because membership is the authority for everything else and was previously the
exchange's uncorroborated word.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian.

```
ENTRY_CONTEXT  = "sqex-entry-v1"     (ASCII, 13 bytes, no terminator)
ACTION_CONTEXT = "sqex-action-v1"    (ASCII, 14 bytes, no terminator)
```

### What is signed

```
entry_input  = ENTRY_CONTEXT  || SHA-256( exchange[32] || instance[32]
                                          || channel[32] || account[32]
                                          || device[32] || epoch(4)
                                          || msg_seq(8) || expires_after(4)
                                          || chain_seq(8) || prev[32]
                                          || SHA-256(body) )

action_input = ACTION_CONTEXT || SHA-256( exchange[32] || instance[32]
                                          || channel[32] || actor[32]
                                          || actor_device[32] || event(1)
                                          || subject[32] || arg_len(1) || arg
                                          || chain_seq(8) || prev[32] )

signature    = Ed25519(device_secret, input)
```

The signature is a raw RFC 8032 Ed25519 signature over the input, which is 45
bytes for an entry and 46 for an action whatever the body's size. Hash-then-sign
over a fixed-width input follows SIP-10 and SIP-20, for the reason both give: a
signer sees a small fixed message regardless of what it is signing. The two
context strings differ, so an entry signature can never be presented as an
action signature or the reverse.

`body` is the body **as posted** — ciphertext in a private channel, a SIP-19
message in the clear in a public one. A verifier therefore needs no key of any
kind, which is what lets a party that cannot read a channel still verify who
wrote what in it.

`arg` carries the operation's own parameters and is at most `MAX_ARG` bytes: the
role byte for added, promoted and demoted; `retention_secs(4) || max_entries(4)`
for a retention change; `epoch(4)` for a rotation; empty for left, joined and
removed.

### The terms that stop a signature travelling

Three of the terms above exist only to bind a signature to the place it was
made, and each closes a replay that is otherwise available.

**`exchange` is the origin's Ed25519 identity — the key a client pins under
SIP-9.** A direct message's identifier is derived from its two accounts
(SIP-16), so one direct message has byte-identical channel bytes on every
exchange that has ever existed. Without this term a signed entry lifts out of
one exchange and into another's copy of the same conversation and verifies
there. SIP-10 binds `transaction.server` against exactly this and makes it a
numbered verification step; this is the same defence for a different artifact.
It costs nothing on the wire, because a client already holds the key in order
to have connected at all.

**`instance` is 32 bytes the exchange generates uniformly at random for each
*incarnation* of a channel**, distinct from the channel identifier. SIP-16
describes a destroyed direct message being recreated under the same derived
identifier with its numbering restarted, and says an exchange cannot avoid the
situation in general. Without this term every entry of the first incarnation
replays into the second, where the channel, the epoch numbering and the empty
chain all agree with it.

An exchange MUST generate a fresh `instance` whenever it creates a channel,
including each recreation of a direct message. It reports it in `Created` and in
`Channel`, and it **MUST withhold it exactly where SIP-16 withholds `epoch`**:
a `Create` naming an existing channel the caller is not a member of returns
`instance` as 32 zero bytes, because a distinguishable value would make the
field an existence oracle for private channels, which is the disclosure that
rule was written to prevent.

**`account` is bound as well as `device`.** The device is implicit in being the
key the signature verifies under, and binding it is belt and braces. The account
is not implicit at all: nothing in SIP-20 forbids two accounts credentialing one
device, so without this term one signed entry is attributable to either of them
and a verifier has no way to choose.

### The chain

Each device keeps one chain per channel, spanning its entries and its actions
together.

```
chain_seq  : u64, starting at 0, incremented by one for every entry or action
             that device signs in that channel
prev       : SHA-256 of the previous signing input by that device in that
             channel; 32 zero bytes for its first
```

**`prev` links signing inputs, not entries.** A client cannot hash an entry
before the exchange has numbered and timestamped it, so a chain over entries
would be uncomputable by the only party able to sign it. A signing input is
fully determined at the moment of signing.

**`chain_seq` is a chain position and is deliberately not `msg_seq`.** SIP-17's
`msg_seq` is an AEAD nonce, and SIP-16 requires it to be 0 in a public channel
where nothing is sealed. A chain must run in both visibilities and must advance
on actions as well as entries, so it needs a counter of its own. An
implementation that conflates the two either breaks public channels or reuses a
nonce, and the second is unrecoverable.

The exchange records, for each `(channel, device)`, the highest `chain_seq` it
has accepted and the hash of that signing input, and **MUST keep this
independently of the entries themselves**. Pruning removes entries, so the
highest surviving position understates what was used; a device resuming from an
understated mark forks its own chain. This is the same rule and the same
reasoning SIP-17 gives for its `msg_seq` high-water mark, and it fails the same
way when ignored. The pair is reported to that device as `my_chain_seq` and
`my_chain_head` in SIP-16's `Info`.

**A device MUST resume from the greater of what it remembers and what the
exchange reports, never from the exchange's report alone.** An exchange that
under-reported the position would otherwise induce the device to sign twice at
one `chain_seq` — producing a fork with two valid signatures on it, which is
indistinguishable from the device having misbehaved. That is an exchange forging
*blame* without forging a signature, and taking the maximum is the whole defence.

An exchange MUST refuse an entry or action whose `chain_seq` is not exactly one
greater than the mark it holds, or whose `prev` is not the head it holds.

### Verifying an entry

A verifier, given an entry, the channel's `instance`, and the exchange identity
it obtained the entry from, MUST perform these checks and MUST stop at the first
failure:

1. **Signature.** Ed25519-verify the entry's signature over `entry_input`,
   recomputed from the entry's own fields, under the entry's `device`.
2. **Delegation.** Obtain a SIP-20 credential naming the entry's `account` as
   `account` and the entry's `device` as `delegate`, and verify it under SIP-20's
   own numbered steps with scope `sqex-chat`.
3. **Chain.** If the verifier holds the previous entry or action by that device
   in that channel, `prev` MUST equal the hash of that signing input and
   `chain_seq` MUST be exactly one greater.

**Step 2 is not optional and a verifier that omits it has done nothing useful.**
Step 1 proves a key signed; only step 2 says whose key it is. SIP-20 states the
same in its own terms — *"a credential naming an account the verifier did not ask
about is not evidence of anything"* — and this is the check most likely to be
skipped, because step 1 alone produces a satisfying `true`.

An entry that fails step 1 or step 2 is **forged**, and a client MUST present it
as such rather than displaying it as an ordinary message. An entry that fails
step 3 leaves the chain **broken**, which is a weaker claim: see below.

**A missing signature is a forged entry, not a tolerated one.** There is no
unsigned member entry in a conforming exchange and an implementation MUST NOT
accept one for compatibility with anything.

### Verifying a membership event

A system entry (SIP-16 `kind` `0x00`) carries the action signature of the actor
who caused it. A verifier checks the signature over `action_input` under
`actor_device`, then the SIP-20 credential binding `actor_device` to `actor`,
then the chain, exactly as above.

The exchange still writes the entry and still assigns its `seq` and `posted` —
what changes is that it can no longer write one nobody authorised. Every event
SIP-16 defines has an actor able to sign for it: an admin for added, removed,
promoted and demoted; the subject themselves for left and for joined; the
publisher for a rotation; and the caller for a retention change.

A `Create` carrying invitations produces several system entries from one
request, and each is signed over its own content rather than one signature
covering the batch. The signatures appear in the order the events are written:
the creator's `joined` first, then one per invitee in list order.

### Redaction

SIP-16's redaction removes an entry's body and keeps the entry as a tombstone.
The signature commits to `SHA-256(body)` rather than to the body, so **a
tombstone's signature still verifies after its bytes are gone**, and the chain
runs through it unbroken. An exchange MUST retain `body_hash` and the signature
when it redacts.

Committing by hash rather than to the body directly is the reason this works,
and it is the reason the commitment is arranged that way. An implementation that
signed the body inline would have to choose between discarding the signature on
redaction — making every deleted message look forged — and refusing to redact.

### A gap is not a fork

Two failures of step 3 look alike and mean very different things.

- A **gap** — a `chain_seq` higher than expected — is produced by pruning, by
  retention, and by a member joining a channel with history they were not given.
  It is ordinary. A client SHOULD show that it cannot verify continuity across
  the gap and MUST NOT present it as misconduct.
- A **fork** — two entries by one device at one `chain_seq`, both validly signed
  — cannot occur without either that device signing twice or somebody replaying.
  It is evidence, and a client MUST surface it.

An implementation therefore reports three states and not two: **valid**,
**forged** (steps 1–2), and **broken** (step 3), with a gap and a fork
distinguished within the last.

### Wire

The additions to SIP-16 and SIP-17:

```
Entry  | seq: u64 | kind: u8 | account[32] | device[32] | posted: u64
       | expires_after: u32 | epoch: u32 | msg_seq: u64
       | chain_seq: u64 | prev[32] | body_hash[32] | sig[64]
       | len: u32 | body |

Post   | type: u8 | channel[32] | epoch: u32 | msg_seq: u64
       | expires_after: u32 | chain_seq: u64 | prev[32] | sig[64] | body |

Action | chain_seq: u64 | prev[32] | sig[64] |

System | event: u8 | subject[32] | actor[32] | actor_device[32]
       | chain_seq: u64 | prev[32] | sig[64] |
```

An `Action` block is appended to SIP-16's `Join`, `Invite`, `Remove`, `Leave`
and `Retain`, and to SIP-17's `Put` when it advances the epoch. `Create` carries
a count and that many `Action` blocks.

`Created` and `Channel` each gain `instance[32]`.

`exchange` and `instance` add nothing per entry: the first is a key the client
already holds, the second is a property of the channel, fetched once. Both are
inputs to a hash rather than fields on the wire.

**A `Post` MUST use a type byte distinct from the one used before this SIP.**
An implementation predating it encodes a body where the chain fields now sit,
and a decoder reading the new layout would take the first 104 bytes of that body
as chain fields and store the remainder as the message — corrupting the log
silently rather than refusing. Every other request in SIP-16 decodes to an exact
length and refuses a short one cleanly, so this is the only place the hazard
arises, and one byte removes it.

### Limits

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| `arg` (`MAX_ARG`) | 8 bytes |
| Added per member entry | 136 bytes |
| Added per system entry | 136 bytes |
| Added per action on a request | 104 bytes |

At SIP-16's `MAX_BATCH` of 64 entries this adds about 8.7 KiB to a fetch bounded
at 512 KiB, and at its 50 000 entries per channel about 6.8 MB against a 128 MiB
cap. Neither limit needs revisiting.

## Rationale

**Why the signature is an entry field rather than a SIP-19 body.** Putting it
inside the body would need no change at the exchange at all, which is genuinely
attractive, and it fails on the requirement that produced this SIP: in a private
channel the body is ciphertext, so only a party holding the channel key could
verify, and a replicating exchange holds none. A signature that can only be
checked by people who could already read the conversation does not help anybody
who has to verify what they carry. The other body-level option, a new SIP-19
part kind, additionally reaches only `Post` and `Edit` — it has no way to sign a
reaction, a redaction or a channel's metadata, since those bodies have no part
list.

**Why the device signs and not the account.** An account key may be held in
hardware — that is SIP-10's entire motivation — and a YubiKey touch per message
is not a chat client. More decisively, a linked device does not hold its
account's key at all, so an account signature is not merely inconvenient there
but impossible. SIP-20 exists to bridge exactly this, and using it here is what
it was built for.

**Why membership events are signed too.** Without them a replica can verify
every message and still not know who was entitled to send one. Membership is the
authority for everything in SIP-16 — its own text calls `Fetch` requiring
membership *"the load-bearing row"* — so leaving it as the exchange's word would
have produced a conversation whose contents were verifiable and whose
participants were not. It also turns out to buy something better than
verification of each event: a replica can answer "was this account a member when
this entry was signed" from the log alone, rather than from an assertion by
whoever handed the log over.

**Why one chain covering entries and actions rather than two.** Two chains would
let an exchange drop every action while presenting an unbroken chain of
messages, which is precisely the history somebody wanting to hide a removal
would like to present. Interleaving them means anything omitted leaves a gap in
the one sequence there is.

**Why the chain is per device rather than per channel.** A per-channel chain is
what a transparency log would use and it is unavailable here: a client cannot
know what the exchange will number next, so it cannot link to the channel's
previous entry at signing time. A per-device chain is computable by the party
that has to compute it. The cost is that it gives a total order only within one
device's contributions, which is stated in Security considerations rather than
implied away.

**Why a signed timestamp was rejected.** Two forms were considered. A
client-asserted `sent`, bounded by a skew check at acceptance, would give a
replica a signed author time where none exists — and would introduce a second
clock beside `posted`, which is what SIP-16's retention, its pruning and both of
its disappearing-message clocks already run on. Two times for one message that
two clients may show differently is a worse failure than one time nobody signs.
The second form, an exchange receipt over `(channel, seq, posted, entry hash)`
signed with its SIP-9 key, is a better idea and was declined only for this
version: it would make the origin's word verifiable and its equivocation
provable rather than merely detectable, at 64 bytes and one signature per entry.
It puts the exchange in the signing business for the first time anywhere in this
stack, and it creates a transferable proof that a named operator carried a named
message. It is the natural content of a later SIP, and nothing in this wire
would have to change to accept it.

**Why deniability was given up rather than preserved.** A group or ring
signature over the member set would authenticate a sender to the members while
leaving a leaked transcript unprovable to anybody else. It is real
cryptography, it is much larger than everything here, and SIP-13 and SIP-17 each
declined to add cryptography of that weight for the benefit on offer. More to
the point, it cannot do the job: replication requires a third party who is *not*
a member to verify authorship, and a construction that hides authorship from
non-members is the opposite of that. Deniability and replication are not both
available, and this SIP takes replication and says so.

## Compatibility

**This breaks the wire and both ends must move together.** `Post`, `Entry`,
`System`, `Created`, `Channel`, and five membership requests all change shape,
and SIP-17's `Put` gains a field. There is no version in which an old client and
a new exchange interoperate, and the `Post` type byte changes so that the
attempt fails loudly instead of storing a corrupted message.

There is no unsigned entry to be compatible with. The reference deployment is
wiped rather than migrated, and this SIP requires a conforming exchange to
refuse an unsigned member entry outright rather than tolerating one for
compatibility. An implementation that carries pre-SIP-31 history has to decide
what to do with it, and the honest options are to show it as unverifiable or to
drop it; it MUST NOT report it as verified.

Nothing in SIP-19 changes. Its bodies are carried exactly as before and its
receiver-side authority rules stand — an edit is still ignored unless it comes
from the account of its target. What changes is that the account is now a fact
rather than an observation, which makes those rules mean what they always
appeared to.

## Security considerations

**Deniability is gone, and this is the largest single consequence.** Before this
SIP a leaked transcript proved nothing about who said what, because any of up to
256 members could have produced any of it. Afterwards every member holds
transferable, non-repudiable proof of every other member's words, for as long as
they keep the bytes. Sealing does not mitigate it: a signature over a plaintext
verifies for anybody shown that plaintext. This is a deliberate trade for the
ability to replicate, and an implementation MUST NOT describe a channel as
deniable.

**The exchange still chooses among signed things.** SIP-19 resolves competing
edits by *"the one with the higher `seq` wins"*, and competing `Metadata` the
same way. `seq` is assigned by the exchange and cannot be signed by anybody, so
the exchange selects which signed edit stands and which signed channel name is
current. It cannot forge either candidate; it picks between them. Every rule
whose outcome depends on `seq` remains the exchange's to decide, however well
the candidates are signed, and a reader should not take signatures to have
removed the exchange from the trust model.

**Ordering across devices is unverifiable and always will be.** Each device's
own contribution is chained and totally ordered. The interleaving of two
devices' entries is a choice made by one party with no way to prove it was made
honestly. SIP-16's *"a single party decided it… no Lamport clock, no vector
clock"* is correct within one exchange and does not survive a second one.

**Equivocation is detectable, not preventable.** An origin can present one log
to one party and a different log to another, with every entry in both validly
signed and both chains internally consistent. Only comparing what two parties
hold reveals it. Making it attributable rather than merely detectable is what
the rejected exchange receipt would have bought.

**Signals and read cursors remain forgeable by the exchange, permanently.** A
typing signal carries an account the exchange asserted, and a `read` cursor is
an unsigned client claim it relays. Neither is signed and neither is
replicated. Nothing durable may be derived from either, and a client that showed
"Alice read this" as a fact would be reporting the exchange's word as Alice's.

**A revoked or stolen device signs valid entries, and dating them is a problem.**
Revocation is prospective everywhere in this design — SIP-20, SIP-22 and SIP-17
each say so — and here the consequence sharpens. Deciding whether a device's
credential was valid *when it signed* needs a time, and the only time available
is the origin's unsigned `posted`. So a verifier evaluating an old entry against
a since-revoked device is resting a security decision on an unsigned field. That
is the concrete cost of leaving timestamps unsigned, and it is stated here
rather than left to be discovered.

**A gap is ordinary; only a fork is evidence.** Pruning, retention and a member
admitted without history all produce gaps. An implementation that presented a
gap as tampering would cry wolf on every channel with a retention window, and
users would learn to dismiss the one case that matters.

**Verifying the signature without the credential proves nothing about who
spoke.** It proves a key signed. Any device may sign anything and claim any
account in the entry it signs; only SIP-20's credential ties the two together.
This is the failure mode most likely to ship, because the incomplete version
returns success on every honest message and is wrong only on the attack.

**The chain resumption rule is load-bearing.** A device that trusts the
exchange's reported position over its own can be made to sign twice at one
`chain_seq`, and the resulting fork is evidence against the device rather than
against the exchange that induced it.

## Reference implementation

Implemented in [sqex](https://github.com/wave-cl/sqex) v0.19.0.

- `sqex-proto::entry_sig` holds both constructions, the `Place` that binds a
  signature to where it was made, and `link`, which takes the next chain step
  from a signing input rather than from an entry — a client cannot hash an entry
  before the exchange has numbered it.
- `sqexd::channel` verifies on post and at each of the nine sites that write a
  system entry, and keeps a `chain` table **independently of the entries** for
  the reason `high_water` already is: pruning would understate the position, and
  a device resuming from an understated mark forks its own chain.
- `sqex-chat` signs at one site on send, signs every membership call, and
  reports the three states on read. It resumes its chain from the greater of
  what it remembers and what `Info` says, never the report alone.

**Two things the reference client does not do, stated because a reader will
otherwise assume it does.**

It takes the device-to-account binding from SIP-22's registry rather than
re-checking a SIP-20 credential itself, which trusts this exchange to have
checked when the device was enrolled. That is adequate for a client talking to
an exchange it already relies on and is *not* adequate for a replica carrying
somebody else's log, which must perform step 2 in full.

And a create is the one place it does not record its own chain step. `created: 0`
covers two outcomes — already a member, which writes nothing, and a squatter
displaced, which writes one event per invitee — and the reply does not
distinguish them. Recording either way risks putting the device a position ahead
of the exchange and forking its own chain on the next thing it signs, so it asks
instead. Under-remembering costs a lookup; over-remembering costs the chain.

`sqexd::channel::welcome` is the one membership change with no actor to sign it:
the exchange putting a newcomer into its own front room, before that account has
issued any request or necessarily registered a device. The member row goes in
without a system entry rather than the exchange writing an event nobody
authorised. A replica therefore sees membership of a welcome channel it cannot
derive from the log.

Integration tests in `sqexd/tests/signed_entry_flow.rs` cover an entry signed by
one device and claimed by another; an entry lifted between two exchanges holding
the same direct message, whose identifiers are byte-identical by construction;
an entry replayed from a previous incarnation of a recreated channel; an
incarnation reused; a chain position reused and a chain position skipped; a
tombstone whose signature still verifies with its body gone; an entry with no
signature at all; a membership event under a signature nobody made; and two
devices of one account signing and chaining separately — the case SIP-22's
degenerate account-is-its-own-device makes untestable until somebody links a
second client, which is exactly how SIP-17's per-device sealing went
unimplemented for a whole release line.

Each was run against the code that preceded it and observed to fail: with the
exchange key out of the signing input the cross-exchange replay is accepted;
with the incarnation out, the cross-incarnation replay is; with verification
removed, five of them are; with the chain check removed the chain test passes
anything; and with the signature cleared on redaction every deleted message
reads as a forgery.
