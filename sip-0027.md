## Abstract

One identity signs a statement about another and lodges it with an exchange.
Others may read the statements about an identity and decide for themselves what
to make of them.

## Motivation

Every identity in this ecosystem is self-asserted. A key proves it is itself
and nothing more: that a base58 string operates a particular service, or was
audited, or has behaved well, is not something the key can establish alone.

Attestation lets identities make claims about each other, so a consumer with an
existing trust relationship can extend it.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian.

```
ATTEST_CONTEXT = "sqex-attestation-v1"   (ASCII, 19 bytes, no terminator)

signing_input = ATTEST_CONTEXT || SHA-256( subject[32] || issuer[32] || claim(1)
                                           || body_len(2) || body
                                           || issued_at(8) || expires_at(8) )

signature     = Ed25519(issuer_secret, signing_input)

Attestation   | subject[32] | issuer[32] | claim: u8 | len: u16 | body
              | issued_at: u64 | expires_at: u64 | sig[64] |
```

Hash-then-sign over a fixed width, as SIP-10, SIP-20 and SIP-31 all do.

### The claim registry

| `claim` | Meaning | `body` |
|---|---|---|
| 0x01 | the subject operates the named service | UTF-8 name |
| 0x02 | the issuer knows the subject by this name | UTF-8 name |
| 0x03 | the issuer examined the subject and says so | UTF-8 description |
| 0x04 | the issuer withdraws an earlier attestation | its 32-byte digest |

Four, and an escape hatch: any other value is a claim this reader does not know.

**A claim of an unregistered type MUST NOT be rendered as text about a person.**
It is reported as present and unreadable, which is what it is. This is the rule
that makes an escape hatch survivable — anybody can encode anything in one, and
an exchange cannot police semantics it does not parse, so the defence has to be
at the reader.

An attestation's **digest** is `SHA-256(signing_input)`. It names what was
signed rather than how it was written down, so it does not change with the
serialisation.

### What the exchange checks, and what it cannot

An exchange MUST refuse an attestation whose signature does not verify, whose
`expires_at` is not after `issued_at`, whose window is longer than a year, which
is already expired, or **whose issuer and subject are the same** — a self-issued
attestation establishes nothing, and without the rule anybody could fill their
own record.

It MUST NOT check anything else, because there is nothing else it can check. It
does not know whether a claim is true, and an exchange that appeared to vouch
for one would be manufacturing an authority the data does not support.

**Anybody may lodge one.** An attestation carries its own proof, so who handed
it over establishes nothing — and requiring the issuer would mean an issuer that
has gone away can never be quoted again. That is what "equally valid handed over
on a USB stick" means in practice.

Lodging the same statement twice changes nothing: it is identified by its
digest, so a replay cannot inflate a record.

### Withdrawal, and why it guarantees nothing

A withdrawal is claim `0x04`, signed by the issuer of the statement it names.
**Only that issuer may withdraw it** — otherwise withdrawing would be a way to
silence somebody else. An exchange MUST refuse a withdrawal naming an
attestation it does not hold or one by a different issuer.

The withdrawal is **kept**, not merely applied, so a reader arriving afterwards
sees that the issuer withdrew rather than that the claim was never made.

**A consumer that never sees the withdrawal keeps trusting the claim**, and
nothing can fix that. Expiry is the only guarantee, which is why the window is
capped at a year and why a short one is the setting that matters.

### Reading

```
Query | type: u8 = 0x02 | subject[32] | has_issuer: u8 | issuer[32] |
Held  | now: u64 | count: u16 | count × Attestation |
```

`issuer` is a filter and **the ordinary way to ask**. Only attestations from
issuers a consumer already trusts carry weight; asking for all of them is the
unusual case, and a consumer that treated the total as a measurement would be
counting how many keys somebody made.

A subject accumulates at most 64 before the oldest are dropped. That is a bound
on storage and **not a quorum**.

## Rationale

**These need signatures, unlike everything else here.** SIP-4, SIP-5 and SIP-25
lean on the transport: the connection proves who is speaking, and the claim
dies with it. An attestation is meant to be repeated to third parties who never
saw the connection, so it must carry its own proof. This is the one exchange
feature where transport authentication is insufficient, and saying so sharpens
what the rest are for.

The exchange is a convenient place to lodge them, not an authority over them. A
signed attestation is equally valid handed over on a USB stick.

## Compatibility

New protocol. Requires SIP-2 for the connection, though **not for the
attestation itself** — the signature is what carries it, and the transport is
incidental. That is the whole distinction this SIP draws against the rest of the
exchange's features.

Lodging and reading are open to any caller, including an unidentified one. An
attestation is meant to travel, and a store that would only disclose to
authenticated callers would be adding a boundary the artifact does not have.

## Security considerations

**No consensus and no scores.** There is no global view of who is trustworthy,
and this SIP must not grow one. Statements are inputs to a consumer's own
policy. Computing a reputation number would manufacture authority the data does
not support.

**Sybil.** Anyone can generate identities and have them vouch for each other. A
count of attestations is meaningless; only attestations from issuers a consumer
already trusts carry weight, and consumers MUST be able to filter by issuer.

**Attestations are public and permanent in practice.** Once read they can be
retained and replayed by anyone. Nothing lodged should be treated as
retractable, whatever the revocation mechanism says.

**Negative claims are dangerous** in a way positive ones are not: an
unaccountable assertion that an identity misbehaved is a defamation vector with
no adjudicator.

**This version registers none, and that is the answer to the open question.**
The registry holds statements an issuer makes in somebody's favour and nothing
against them. The direction is deliberate: adding a claim type later is
possible, and removing one that consumers have started relying on is not.

The escape hatch means somebody can encode a negative claim as an unregistered
type, and an exchange cannot police semantics it does not parse. What stops that
becoming the same problem is the reader's rule above — an unknown claim is not
rendered as text about a person — so the assertion travels as bytes nobody
displays. That is weaker than preventing it and stronger than nothing, and it is
the most a design with no adjudicator can offer.

## Reference implementation

`sqex` 0.33.0. `sqex-proto::attest` for the artifact, `sqexd::attest` for the
store, and `sqex attest say|withdraw|read` in the CLI.

Durable, unlike the beacon and the endpoint store beside it, and the reason
follows from what an attestation is: it outlives the connection by design, so
losing it on a restart would lose something nobody could reproduce. It is also
permanent in practice whatever the store does — once read it can be retained and
replayed by anyone, which the CLI says out loud when lodging one.

Two things the implementation added that the sketch did not have, both recorded
above: the refusal of a self-issued attestation, and the rule that only an
issuer may withdraw its own.
