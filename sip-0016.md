## Abstract

A channel is a durable, totally ordered log of entries held by an exchange. It
has a membership the exchange enforces, a retention window the exchange prunes
against, and a body it never inspects. A private channel is sealed under a key
the exchange does not hold (SIP-17); a public one is listed in a directory and
stored in the clear. This SIP specifies the log, the membership and the pruning.
What an entry *contains* is SIP-19; what an entry carries too much of to be
stored inline is SIP-18.

## Motivation

The exchange has four services and none of them is a conversation.

SIP-5 is a mailbox: 32 KiB, seven days, and deleted on collection. It says of
itself that it is "a rendezvous for messages, not a file host", and it is
equally not a history — a message that both parties have read no longer exists.
SIP-12 carries opaque frames between two identities that have each named the
other, which requires both to be present; nothing is retained, and a peer that
was offline missed it. SIP-13 is a roster with a thirty-second TTL, no owner and
no storage at all, and it says outright that what the members do with the roster
is not its business.

Between them they cover live conversation between people who are online, and
one-shot delivery to somebody who is not. What is missing is the ordinary case:
a conversation that persists, that someone can scroll back through, that a
person joining later can be given part of, and that goes away on a schedule
somebody chose.

That needs three things none of the existing services has. **An order**, so that
"the message before this one" means the same thing to everybody. **Membership
that outlives a connection**, so a conversation has a set of people rather than
a set of sockets. And **retention**, which is the first thing in this stack that
requires the exchange to remember something on purpose.

## Why this one persists

Every other sqex service holds its state in memory, and three of them argue that
this is correct rather than merely easy. The beacon does not persist because
replaying observations the process did not make would be a lie about what it
saw. A relayed session does not persist because a session is live coordination
and a restart honestly ends it. A room does not persist for the same reason.
Only the mailbox calls memory-only storage a limitation, and even there the
worst case is a retry.

A channel cannot hold any of those positions. Its entire value is that it
remembers, and an exchange that forgot a conversation on restart would not be
offering this service at all. So this is the first sqex service that **MUST**
store durably, and the first where a crash between accepting an entry and
storing it is a fault rather than an honest gap.

That is worth stating in the specification rather than discovering in an
implementation, because it changes what an exchange operator is promising. The
other four services can be run by someone who is careless with a disk. This one
cannot.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. Every operation is bound to the caller's
transport identity (SIP-3). A connection carrying no identity has no membership
and MUST be refused.

`CHANNEL_CONTEXT` is the ASCII string `sqex-channel-v1`.

### The channel identifier

A channel is named by 32 bytes. For a channel that anybody creates deliberately
— a public room, a private room — those bytes are generated uniformly at random
by the creator. They are not a name and are not derived from one.

A direct message is the exception, because both parties must arrive at the same
identifier without having spoken first:

```
first, second = the two ACCOUNTS, in lexicographic order of their key bytes
channel_id    = SHA-256(CHANNEL_CONTEXT || 0x01 || first || second)
```

**Accounts, never devices.** Deriving from device keys instead would give each
pair of devices its own channel, so adding a phone would change the identifier
and split one conversation into several that cannot see each other. The
identifier names two people, and it does not move when either of them acquires
a client.

The ordering is SIP-12's, and for the same reason: it settles which of the two
values goes first without either side having to negotiate, and it makes the
derivation identical on both ends. The `0x01` separates this derivation from any
later one over the same inputs.

This discloses nothing the exchange does not already hold. It enforces the
membership list, so it knows both identities either way; the derivation only
saves the two of them from creating two channels and each waiting in the wrong
one.

**A direct message is two people, and the exchange enforces it.** The property is
not recorded at creation; it is **recomputed whenever it matters**, because the
derivation is reproducible by anybody including the exchange:

> A channel is a direct message exactly when its identifier equals the
> derivation above over a set of two identities.

That definition makes the pair the channel's rightful holders as a matter of
arithmetic rather than of who asked first, and every rule below follows from it.

- `Invite`, `Join` and `Remove` MUST be refused on a direct message — checked by
  recomputing over its two members. There is no way to add a third party and no
  way for either to eject the other.
- **Both parties are admins.** A direct message has no asymmetry to express, and
  making both admins avoids inventing a rule for what happens when the creator
  leaves.
- `Leave` removes the caller and nothing else. The other party keeps the channel
  and its history; the ordinary last-member rule destroys it once both have
  gone. Leaving a conversation MUST NOT delete the other person's copy of it.

A `Create` by an identity that is **not** a member of an existing channel
normally changes nothing (see **Messages**). A direct message is the exception,
and it has to be. Where the caller supplies exactly one invitee and the
derivation over the two of them equals `channel`, the exchange compares the
present membership against that pair:

- **Every present member is one of the two** — the caller is returning after
  leaving. Re-add them, keep the history.
- **Any present member is not** — those occupants have no claim to this
  identifier. Discard the channel with its entries and its blobs, and create it
  fresh for the rightful pair.

The second rule exists because without it a single request denies two people the
ability to ever talk. An identifier derived from two public keys can be computed
by anyone, so anybody could create that channel first, sit in it alone, and
every subsequent `Create` by either rightful party would find a channel it is
not a member of and change nothing — permanently, for a pair who had never done
anything. Discarding is safe because the claim is provable and exclusive: to
discard a channel you must show it is the derivation over yourself and one other
identity, which nobody but those two can do, and what is discarded is only
whatever the squatter put there.

Without that check a direct message is an ordinary private channel whose creator
is an admin, and an admin may invite — so either party could silently add a
third to a conversation the other believes is between two people. The
identifier would then no longer match the membership, which makes the addition
*detectable* by anyone who recomputes it, and detectable is not the same as
prevented. Enforcing costs one hash at creation and converts a convention into
arithmetic both parties can reproduce.

A pair who want a third person start a room. That is a different channel with a
different identifier, and it is visible as one.

### Accounts and devices

A **person** is an account and a **client** is a device, bound to it by a SIP-20
credential and resolved by the exchange through SIP-22's registry. Every
operation here is made by a connection carrying a device identity (SIP-3), and
the exchange resolves it to an account before doing anything else.

Everything in this SIP then works in accounts. Membership, roles, mentions,
receipts and display are all per account: a person appears once in a member list
however many devices they own. The one place a device appears is the entry
header, because SIP-17 derives its sealing subkey from it.

An account with no registered devices is its own device (SIP-22), so the
ordinary single-client case requires nobody to have read any of this.

### Visibility, membership and roles

```
visibility  0x00 private     0x01 public
role        0x00 member      0x01 admin
```

The creator of a channel is its first member and an admin. An admin may invite,
remove, promote, set the retention policy, and set a public channel's name and
topic. A member may post, fetch, signal and leave. There are two roles and no
more; a finer permission model is a policy question that belongs to whatever is
built on this, not to the wire.

A **public** channel may be joined by any identity, without invitation, and is
listed in the exchange's directory. Its `name` and `topic` are plaintext, they
are what the directory searches, and its entries are stored in the clear.

A **private** channel MUST refuse a join. Membership comes only from an invite
by an admin. Its `name` and `topic` at the exchange MUST be empty — a private
room's name is carried as a sealed metadata entry (SIP-19, type `0x05`), because
the name is very often the part that identifies the members.

A channel MUST always have at least one admin: a `Remove` or a demotion that
would take the last one away is refused, and an admin promotes somebody first.
In a **private** channel a `Leave` by the last admin while other members remain
is refused too, since leaving there gives up the role and would strand the
others with a channel nobody can administer. In a public one it is not, for the
reason below: leaving does not give up the role. Neither ever binds on a direct
message, where both parties are admins.

A promotion names a member. A public channel whose membership has fallen to zero
therefore has nobody to promote, and its remaining admin may only `Close` it or
wait for somebody to join.

**How a channel ends differs by visibility, and the difference is the point.**

A **private** channel or a direct message is a conversation between particular
people, so its existence is its membership: the last member may always leave, and
doing so destroys the channel along with its entries and its attachments. A
blob attached to another channel as well survives, per SIP-18's rule that a blob
is deleted when its last attachment goes.

A **public** channel is a place rather than a conversation. It is listed in a
directory precisely so that people find it later, and it **MUST NOT** be
destroyed by anybody leaving — its membership may fall to zero and it persists,
still listed, still holding whatever its retention has not yet pruned. It ends
only when an admin calls `Close`.

For that to work, **a public channel's admins are admins whether or not they are
currently members**. Joining and leaving govern reading and posting; being an
admin is a durable role attached to the channel. An admin of a public channel may
therefore `Leave` freely, and may still `Close`, `Retain`, `Invite` or promote
afterwards. In a private channel admin remains what it is elsewhere in this SIP,
a property of a member, because there is no channel left to administer once
everybody has gone.

### Entries and ordering

The exchange assigns each accepted entry a sequence number: `u64`, monotonically
increasing per channel, starting at 1, never reused even after pruning.

That is the total order, and it is the only one. There is no Lamport clock, no
vector clock and no client-supplied ordering. Two members who fetch the same
range see the same entries in the same order because a single party decided it.

```
Entry | seq: u64 | kind: u8 | account[32] | device[32] | posted: u64
      | expires_after: u32 | epoch: u32 | msg_seq: u64 | len: u32 | body |
```

`kind` is `0x01` for an entry a member posted and `0x00` for one the exchange
wrote itself; see **System entries** below.

`posted` is the exchange's clock in Unix seconds. `account` and `device` are
both the exchange's observation of the connection that posted — the device from
SIP-3, the account from the registry — and neither is a cryptographic fact, the
same caveat SIP-5 states about its own `sender`. `account` is who a reader
displays; `device` is what SIP-17 derives the sealing subkey from, and carrying
it means a reader can derive that subkey without asking anyone which device is
whose.

`expires_after` is a disappearing-message timer in seconds, 0 for none; see
**Retention**.

`epoch` selects the key the body is sealed under, per SIP-17. **Epoch 0 means
the body is not sealed**: it is a SIP-19 message in the clear, which is the case
for every entry in a public channel. In a private channel epoch 0 MUST NOT
appear on a member entry — but it is exactly what a system entry carries, since
the exchange holds no key and could not seal one if it wanted to. A system entry
also has `account`, `device`, `msg_seq` and `expires_after` all zero.

A private channel is created with no epoch at all, reported as 0, and a `Post`
to it MUST be refused until its creator has published the envelopes for epoch 1
(SIP-17). There is no window in which an entry could be stored unsealed.

`msg_seq` is the sending **device's** own counter within the epoch, and SIP-17
uses it as the AEAD nonce. Per device, not per account — that is what keeps two
of one person's clients from colliding. It is in the clear because the exchange
already knows who posted and in what order, so it discloses nothing further. An
exchange MUST NOT interpret it; a receiver MUST, because a repeat is a nonce
reuse. In a public
channel nothing is sealed and there is no nonce, so `msg_seq` MUST be 0 and
carries no meaning.

### Who may call what

Every rule below is normative and every one is enforced by the exchange, which
can enforce all of them because each depends only on the membership and roles it
holds itself. A caller lacking the stated authority MUST be refused.

| Operation | Who may call it |
|---|---|
| `Create` | Any identity; on an existing channel it changes nothing, except that a party of a direct message may use it to return |
| `Join` | Any identity, and only on a public channel |
| `Invite` | An admin; never on a direct message |
| `Remove` | An admin; never on a direct message |
| `Leave` | A member |
| `Post` | A member |
| `Fetch` | A member |
| `Info` | A member, or an admin |
| `Retain` | An admin, which in a direct message is either party |
| `Signal` | A member |
| `List` | Any identity; it returns public channels only |
| `Cursor` | A member, setting its own mark and no one else's |
| `Cursors` | A member |
| `Redact` | The account that posted `target`, or an admin; never a system entry |
| `Close` | An admin, who need not be a member of a public channel |

**`Fetch` requiring membership is the load-bearing row**, and it is what stops a
private channel being read by anybody who learns its identifier. Note its
consequence for public channels, which is a design decision rather than an
accident: the directory shows names and topics, and reading the contents means
joining first. That gives an exchange somewhere to apply a rate limit and gives
a public room a membership to moderate, and it costs a stranger one request.

Membership is checked at the moment of the call, so a removed member's next
`Fetch` is refused — including one already parked in a long poll, which an
exchange MUST answer or drop rather than continue to hold open. Nothing is
served on the strength of having been a member earlier.

These are the rows an exchange enforces once a caller has reached it. Whether it
serves that caller at all is a separate question an operator may answer with a
whitelist — every route here is the kind of protected endpoint one would cover,
and SIP-24 specifies how a peer asks to be admitted. This SIP requires no
whitelist and works without one; SIP-24 notes why a closed-set mechanism suits
a private deployment and not a public one.

None of this protects content; it is access control at a service, and a
dishonest exchange can disregard every row. What protects content is that the
exchange holds no key (SIP-17). These rules bound what a *stranger* can obtain
and what a *removed member* can continue to obtain, and that is all they claim.

`Create` is **idempotent on `channel`**. A create naming a channel that already
exists returns `created: 0` and the channel's current epoch, changing nothing.

The `invites` list adds members in the same request, which matters more than
convenience: it is what makes the direct-message race safe, and it is what lets
the exchange recognise a direct message at all — the derivation is over the
creator and exactly one invitee, so a create that supplies neither cannot be
one. Both ends derive the
same identifier and both create it naming the other, so whichever request is
processed first produces a channel that already contains **both** identities.
The loser changes nothing and is already a member. Without it, the loser would
not be a member of the channel it had just tried to create, and would have to
wait to be invited by a peer with no reason to know it was waiting.

Except for the direct-message case in **The channel identifier**, a create
naming a channel that exists and does not have the caller as a member MUST be
answered the same way as one naming a channel that exists and does:
`created: 0`, `epoch: 0`, and no further
detail — reporting the real epoch would disclose how often a channel the caller
cannot see has rotated, and therefore roughly how often somebody was removed
from it. The alternative probes private channels by guessing.

`Join` succeeds on a public channel and MUST be refused on a private one, direct
messages included. `Leave` removes the caller. `Invite` and `Remove` require an
admin, and a `Remove` MUST be followed by a rotation per SIP-17 — the exchange
cannot enforce that and MUST NOT pretend to, so it is a requirement on the
client. Authority for every operation is tabulated in **Who may call what**.

`Post` requires membership, and `expires_after` MUST NOT exceed the channel's
`retention_secs`. `epoch` MUST be 0 in a public channel and MUST be
the channel's current epoch in a private one; the exchange rejects anything else,
which stops a member posting under an epoch some current member cannot read.

`Close` destroys a channel with its entries, its envelopes and its attachments;
a blob attached elsewhere survives, per SIP-18. It is
how a public channel ends, and it is available for a private one too, where it
saves the members leaving one at a time. It is not reversible and there is no
tombstone: the identifier becomes free again, and a `Create` naming it afterwards
makes a new and unrelated channel.

`Info` returns the full member list, and is answerable to a **member or an
admin**. An admin need not be a member of a public channel, where the role is
durable, and one deciding whether to `Close` a room should be able to see who is
in it first — the two operations having different audiences was an oversight
rather than a policy.

`my_msg_seq` is the highest `msg_seq` the exchange has ever accepted from the
**calling device** at the channel's current epoch, or 0 if there is none. It is
how a client that has lost its counter resumes without guessing (SIP-17), and
the exchange keeps it independently of the entries it summarises, so pruning
does not erase it. Clients **MUST** show it.
SIP-13 gives the argument and it applies here with more force: an admin can add
an identity at any time, and the member list is the only place that becomes
visible.

`List` searches the public directory by substring of name or topic, `offset` for
paging, an empty `query` for everything. Private channels MUST NOT appear in it
under any query.

### Fetching, and waiting

`Fetch` returns entries with `seq` greater than `since`, oldest first, up to
`MAX_BATCH` entries or `MAX_BATCH_BYTES`, whichever binds first. `since` of 0
means from the oldest entry retained.

When there is nothing to return, the exchange holds the request open for up to
`wait_secs`, bounded by `MAX_WAIT`, and answers as soon as an entry is accepted
or a signal arrives for the caller. A `wait_secs` of 0 answers immediately.

Long-polling is a departure from SIP-13, where joining every two seconds serves
as both heartbeat and poll, and the difference is deliberate. A roster tolerates
two seconds of staleness; a conversation does not, and a two-second poll across
every channel a person belongs to is a great deal of traffic to buy latency that
is still poor. An exchange MAY answer early at any time, and a client MUST treat
an empty reply as ordinary.

`first` and `last` are the oldest and newest sequence numbers the exchange still
holds. A client whose `since` is below `first` has been away longer than the
retention window and **has a gap it cannot fill**; it MUST show that rather than
present the remaining entries as though they were the whole conversation.

### Signals

`Signal` is relayed to the channel's other members and **MUST NOT be stored,
sequenced, retained or included in any fetch by sequence number**. It is
delivered in the `signals` section of a `Fetch` reply, at most once, and
discarded after `SIGNAL_TTL` or once `MAX_SIGNALS` are outstanding for a
recipient, oldest first.

Signal kinds are defined in SIP-19 — typing, and nothing else today, because
reading turned out to be durable state and became a cursor instead. An exchange
MUST NOT interpret the body, and an exchange that dropped every signal it was
ever given would still conform to this SIP. That is the intended standard of
service: signals are a courtesy, and nothing may depend on one arriving.

### Receipts

Two cursors per account per channel, held by the exchange as state rather than
as entries — a 256-member channel must not produce 256 log rows every time
somebody reads a message.

`POST /channel/cursor` sets the caller's `read` mark; `POST /channel/cursors`
returns everyone's. Both cursors MUST be monotonic, and `read` MUST NOT exceed
`delivered`.

**`delivered` is observed, not asserted.** The exchange already learns each
member's high-water mark from the `since` on every `Fetch`, and until now threw
it away; recording it costs one integer and no new disclosure. `read` is the one
thing only a client knows, so it is the one thing a client states.

`receipts` of 0 opts the caller out. The exchange then **withholds every other
account's `read` from that caller**, reporting 0 for it, while continuing to
report `delivered` for everybody. Reciprocity is enforced at the exchange rather
than left to each client's good manners, which is what stops somebody taking the
signal without giving it. Delivery is never withheld, because the exchange
observes it whether or not anyone asks and pretending otherwise would be theatre.

Cursors are per account. Delivered means any of that person's devices fetched
it; read means any of them read it.

### System entries

The exchange writes its own entries into the log, unsealed, with `kind` `0x00`:

```
System | event: u8 | subject[32] | actor[32] |
```

| `event` | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0x01 | `subject` was added by `actor` |
| 0x02 | `subject` was removed by `actor` |
| 0x03 | `subject` left |
| 0x04 | `subject` joined a public channel |
| 0x05 | `subject` was promoted to admin by `actor` |
| 0x06 | `subject` was demoted by `actor` |
| 0x07 | `actor` rotated the channel to a new epoch |
| 0x08 | `actor` changed the retention policy |

A system entry's `account` and `device` are zero: the exchange wrote it, and no
member did. An unknown `event` MUST be ignored by a reader, the same rule SIP-19
applies to its own body types.

These are the exchange's record and **MUST NOT** be posted by a client. Two
reasons, and both matter. The exchange cannot seal, so it could not write into a
private channel's sealed stream even if it wanted to — and a client-posted
membership event would be a claim by the very admin whose action it describes,
which is no record at all. The exchange is already the sole authority on
membership and already holds every one of these facts, so writing them down
discloses nothing it did not have.

Sharing the channel's sequence space is what makes this cheap: a client fetching
a range gets messages and events already interleaved, in the order they happened,
with nothing to merge.

The division is honest about who can attest to what. System entries carry what
the exchange knows; sealed SIP-19 bodies carry what it does not. A private
channel's rename stays a `Metadata` body for exactly that reason — the exchange
cannot see the name, so it cannot record the change.

### Redaction

`POST /channel/redact` removes the body of `target` and keeps the entry, with
`len` 0, as a tombstone. The caller MUST be the account that posted `target`, or
an admin of the channel; both are facts the exchange holds, which is the whole
reason this is an operation here rather than only a SIP-19 message.

**A system entry MUST NOT be redacted.** An entry of `kind` `0x00` was written
by the exchange and has no posting account, so only the admin clause could reach
it — which would let an admin remove somebody and then delete the record saying
that they had. An audit trail its subject can erase is not an audit trail, and
these entries exist for no other purpose.

A client deleting a message issues **both** this and the SIP-19 `Redact` body:
this one removes the bytes, that one tells other clients to show the message as
deleted. A client that issued only the SIP-19 body would leave the message at
the exchange for anyone who later joined with history access.

The tombstone is deliberate, and it is the opposite of what pruning does. Pruning
leaves nothing, because a shadow index of who spoke and when, long after the
words are gone, is a worse disclosure than the gap. Here the gap **is** the
record: a reader should be able to see that something was deleted rather than
find a conversation that silently does not follow.

Redacting an entry with an attachment does not remove the blob — the exchange
cannot read the reference. The redacting client MUST also detach it (SIP-18).

### Retention

`retention_secs` and `max_entries` are set at creation and changed by `Retain`.
Both are enforced by the exchange: an entry is pruned once it is older than
`retention_secs`, or once more than `max_entries` newer entries exist, whichever
comes first. `max_entries` of 0 means no count limit. `retention_secs` MUST lie
between `MIN_RETENTION` and `MAX_RETENTION`.

Pruning removes the entry entirely. Nothing survives as a tombstone. This is the
opposite of SIP-5, which deliberately keeps a collection record after dropping a
payload, and the reason is that the products differ: a mailbox's record answers
"was it collected", which is useful to the sender and harmless; a channel's
history *is* the thing, and a shadow index of who said something and when, long
after the something is gone, is a worse disclosure than the gap it fills.

Shortening a retention window applies to entries already stored, and the
shortening MUST take effect at the next prune rather than only for new entries.
An exchange MUST prune on a schedule and MUST NOT rely on a member touching the
channel: a window measured in days against a channel nobody has opened in weeks
is exactly the case that matters.

**Disappearing messages** are the entry's own `expires_after`, and they run on a
different clock from the channel's retention — which is the entire reason they
are not a duplicate of it. Channel retention counts from `posted`. A
disappearing message counts, for the person reading it, from when they read it.

Both clocks are normative and they measure different things:

- **The exchange** prunes the entry at `posted + expires_after`, as a backstop.
  It cannot do better, because it does not know when anybody read.
- **A client** deletes its own copy at its own read time plus `expires_after`,
  and that is the behaviour a person experiences.

A recipient who never reads still loses the message at the exchange, which is
correct: an unread message with a five-minute timer should not sit there for a
month. `expires_after` MUST NOT exceed the channel's `retention_secs` — a
per-message timer may only shorten.

The timer is in the entry header and therefore visible to the exchange. It has
to be, or the exchange cannot prune on it; see Security considerations.

### Limits

Recommended values:

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| Members per channel | 256 |
| Entry body | 32 KiB |
| Entries per channel | 50 000 |
| Stored entry bytes per channel | 128 MiB |
| `MIN_RETENTION` / default / `MAX_RETENTION` | 1 hour / 30 days / 1 year |
| Channels created per identity | 256 |
| Channels an identity may be invited to and not yet posted in | 64 |
| `MAX_BATCH` / `MAX_BATCH_BYTES` | 64 entries / 512 KiB |
| Directory results per `List` | 64 |
| `MAX_WAIT` | 25 s |
| `SIGNAL_TTL` / `MAX_SIGNALS` | 30 s / 32 |
| `name` / `topic` | 64 / 256 bytes |

An overrun MUST be refused, and refused distinguishably from a malformed
request. It MUST NOT be dropped silently.

The 32 KiB entry body is not a free choice, and an implementer should know why
before raising it: there is no streaming path in either direction, so a `Post`
must fit inside one request body whole, and the reference stack caps that at
64 KiB. Raising the entry above roughly half that requires changing the transport
layer's request handling as well, which is a larger change than it appears.

The invitation quota is the anti-spam measure. Anyone may invite anyone, so
without it an identity can be added to unbounded numbers of channels by
strangers. Note that an invitation makes somebody a member immediately — there
is no pending state at the exchange to count, because membership is what the
exchange enforces and a half-membership would be a second thing to enforce. So
the quota counts memberships the identity **has never posted in**, and a further
invite is refused until they post in one or leave it. An identity named in a
`Create`'s `invites` list counts the same way — otherwise the quota would be one
request away from meaningless. A client SHOULD present
channels a person has not spoken in separately from the ones they have.

## Rationale

**Why the exchange assigns the order.** The alternative is a client-supplied
message identifier with a causal order — a Lamport clock, or a hash chain —
which removes the exchange from a decision it currently makes. It was rejected
because the exchange is already the sole authority on availability and on
membership, and it is already the only party that sees every entry. Adding a
distributed ordering would let a dishonest exchange be *detected* reordering
entries, but it cannot be stopped from withholding them, which is the same
outcome by a longer route. Meanwhile the cost is real and lands on every client:
partial orders that have to be presented to a person as a list, and a reply that
can name a message the reader has not got. SIP-9 draws this line explicitly —
the exchange is trusted for availability, not authenticity — and a sequence
number sits on the availability side of it.

**Why a total order and not per-sender streams.** Because a conversation is read
as one column. Per-sender streams would let each member number its own entries
and leave interleaving to the reader, which is cheaper for the exchange and
worse for every client, and it makes "reply to the message above" unsayable.

**Why membership is an ACL and not a bearer secret.** SIP-13 chose the opposite,
and gave the argument: an owner implies revocation, revocation implies an
authorization list, and an authorization list needs the exchange to be trusted
with that decision. That is right, and it is the correct trade for a thirty-
second voice roster where the remedy for an unwanted member is to hang up. It is
the wrong trade for a conversation with months of history, where the remedy has
to be "remove them" and cannot be "abandon the room and rebuild it elsewhere".
The cost is stated plainly in Security considerations: the exchange now holds a
durable membership graph. Both models are defensible; this one buys revocation
and pays for it in metadata.

**Why public channels are stored in the clear.** Anyone may join a public
channel, so anyone may obtain any key it is encrypted under, including whoever
runs the exchange. Encrypting anyway would produce a system that looks
end-to-end and is not, which is worse than one that is honest — and it would
cost the directory, substring search, and any prospect of an operator moderating
what their own service carries. Private channels and direct messages are sealed
under SIP-17 and the exchange holds no key for them. The asymmetry is the point.

**Why a private channel's name is not stored at the exchange.** A membership
graph plus a name is a great deal more than a membership graph. "These eleven
identities" is a fact about a set of people; "these eleven identities, in a
channel called *redundancy planning*" is a fact about what they are doing. The
name costs nothing to move inside the sealed body, so it moves.

**Why long-polling rather than a push.** The exchange already forwards QUIC
datagrams for SIP-12, and pushing entries down that path would be lower latency
still. It was rejected for the first version because entries must be reliable
and ordered, which is the one thing the datagram path explicitly is not, so a
push would need its own acknowledgement and retransmission scheme alongside a
fetch path that would still have to exist for history. A held request reuses
what is there. If the latency proves insufficient, a datagram *notification* —
"something arrived, come and fetch" — is a small addition that does not change
this wire.

**Why signals are not entries.** A typing indicator that persisted for thirty
days is not a feature. Keeping them off the log entirely, rather than storing
them with a short TTL, also means an exchange never has to decide whether a
signal counts against retention or a quota.

**Why the exchange's own entries cannot be redacted.** The redaction rule is
otherwise uniform — the author or an admin — and system entries have no author,
so an admin would be the only party who could reach them. That is precisely the
wrong party: the entries record what admins did, and the one whose action is
recorded is the one with a motive to remove it. Exempting them costs a
comparison on `kind` and is the difference between a record and a courtesy.

**Why a public channel outlives its membership.** Tying existence to membership
is right for a conversation and wrong for a place. A directory exists so that
somebody finds a room a month from now; a room that evaporated because the last
reader closed a tab would make the directory a list of things that mostly no
longer exist. It also produced a strange obligation — the owner of a public room
could never leave it without either destroying it or handing it to somebody else
— which is an odd thing to require of whoever went to the trouble of creating
one. Separating the durable role from the transient membership costs an extra
field and removes both problems.

**Why the direct-message property is recomputed rather than stored.** A flag set
at creation would be simpler to implement and would have to be *right* at
creation, which it is not always able to be — a create whose invitation is
dropped because the recipient blocked the caller (SIP-21) produces a one-member
channel at a direct-message identifier, and a flag would either be wrongly set
or wrongly absent. Recomputing has no such state to get wrong: the question
"is this a direct message" is answered from the membership in front of you and a
hash, every time it is asked. It also makes the identifier self-verifying, which
is what lets the squatting rule above be stated at all.

**Why a direct message is enforced rather than merely derived.** The derivation
alone already gives the two parties a name they both compute and neither
negotiates, which was its purpose. Leaving it there would make "this is between
us" a convention that either party can break, and break silently from the other
side's view — the addition is detectable only by somebody who thinks to
recompute a hash, which no interface will prompt anybody to do. Checking at the
exchange costs one SHA-256 at creation and nothing afterwards, because the
channel is flagged once. It is the cheapest structural guarantee in this SIP.

**Why receipts are cursors and not entries.** A receipt per reader per message
is the obvious model and it does not survive a group: 256 members reading 100
messages is 25 600 rows describing 100. Two integers per member say the same
thing, because reading is monotonic — you do not read message 40 and then unread
it — so a high-water mark loses no information anybody wanted. It also means
receipts cost nothing against retention and cannot be used to fill a channel.

**Why reciprocity is enforced here rather than by clients.** Both comparators
make the read-receipt setting symmetric and both implement it client-side, which
means a modified client takes the signal without giving it. Withholding at the
exchange makes that impossible rather than impolite. It costs one byte on a
request the caller was making anyway.

**Why the entry carries both an account and a device.** Either alone would do
and each would cost something. Account alone would force every reader to ask the
exchange which device sealed an entry before it could derive the subkey, on
every entry. Device alone would force the same lookup to display a name.
Thirty-two bytes against a 32 KiB entry is a tenth of a percent, and it makes an
entry self-contained for both purposes.

**Why blobs are a separate service.** See SIP-18. In short: an attachment does
not fit in an entry, must not be duplicated when a message is forwarded, and has
a lifetime that has to be managed without the exchange being able to read the
reference that points at it.

## Compatibility

Purely additive. Eleven new routes, no change to any existing wire format, and
no change to SIP-3, SIP-5, SIP-12 or SIP-13. An exchange without this SIP
answers 404 and a client without it never asks, so old and new interoperate in
both directions on everything else. No flag day.

It is additive on the wire and not on the operator. An exchange offering this
service acquires a durable store, a pruning schedule and a great deal more disk
than the other four services together, and an operator who deploys it by
upgrading a binary will find that out the hard way.

## Security considerations

**The exchange holds the membership graph in the clear, and holds it durably.**
This is strictly more disclosure than anything else in the stack. SIP-13 already
concedes that a room reveals a group rather than a pair, but a room's roster is
soft state that evaporates thirty seconds after everyone leaves; a channel's
membership is a list of who talks to whom that survives restarts and sits on a
disk until somebody deletes it. Adding the timestamps the exchange keeps anyway,
it also knows when each person joined, when they left, and who removed them.
This is the price of revocation and it is not a small one. An implementation
MUST NOT describe a private channel as private communication without saying what
the exchange sees.

**The exchange sees traffic, not content.** For a private channel it holds
sender, time, entry size and epoch. Sizes and cadence alone carry a good deal:
who is active, when a conversation is heated, who replies to whom within seconds.
SIP-12 says the same about frames and it is more true here, because a channel
persists and can be analysed at leisure rather than observed live.

**Public channels are not confidential and cannot be made so.** Anyone may join,
including whoever runs the exchange and anyone they permit. Everything in one is
plaintext to the operator, to every member, and to anyone who reads the disk.

**Removal is immediate for delivery and prospective for reading.** Once removed,
an identity cannot fetch, post or signal. What it can still read is everything it
was ever given a key for, which is every epoch up to the rotation — held on its
own disk, beyond anyone's reach. Rotation (SIP-17) is what stops it reading
what is said *next*. An interface that presents removal as erasure is lying.

**Receipts disclose presence and attention, to the exchange and to the room.**
A read cursor says when you were there and how far you had got, and unlike a
typing signal it is durable and can be read back weeks later. Opting out
withholds what you see and does not withhold `delivered`, which the exchange
observes regardless — so an observer always learns when your client collected a
message even if it never learns whether you looked at it. The exchange sees both
for everybody, whatever any member's setting says.

**A disappearing-message timer is visible to the exchange and is not erasure.**
The field is in the entry header by necessity. What it buys is that the exchange
really does drop the entry; what it does not buy is anything about the copies
already delivered. A client that keeps them, or a person who photographs the
screen, is outside its reach entirely — the same honest limit redaction has, and
an interface presenting either as erasure is lying.

**Retention is a promise, not a property.** The exchange prunes because it says
it will. A dishonest one keeps everything and no member can tell. Every member
can independently keep everything regardless, and most clients will cache. A
retention window is a statement about one server's storage and about nothing
else, and a user reading "history: 7 days" will assume much more than that
unless told.

**An admin is trusted, and the trust is not enforceable.** An admin can invite an
identity nobody else wants present, and the other members' only defence is
noticing it in the member list. This is SIP-13's roster argument with an owner
attached, and it is weaker: in SIP-13 nobody can add a member who cannot prove
the secret, whereas here an admin can add anyone at all.

**Anyone can invite anyone, which is an unsolicited-contact vector.** The quota
bounds the volume and does nothing about the first one — it is a rate limit, not
a boundary. Refusing a particular account is SIP-21's block list, which this SIP
deliberately does not require: an exchange may carry channels with no notion of
blocking, and one that carries both applies SIP-21's rules. A client SHOULD keep
channels the person has never spoken in out of the main view.

**Denial of service against a channel** is available to any member: posting
until `max_entries` is reached prunes everyone else's history out from under
them. There is no cryptographic defence — the members are members. An exchange
SHOULD rate-limit posts per identity, and an admin can remove the offender,
which is the remedy this model has and SIP-13's does not.

**A guessed channel identifier discloses nothing beyond existence, and only
that.** `Create` answers identically for a channel that exists whether or not
the caller belongs to it, and every operation that would reveal anything
requires membership — see **Who may call what**, where that is stated
normatively rather than only here. At 32 bytes, guessing is not a threat model.

**A direct message's identifier is derived from its two members, so it is
guessable by anyone who knows both.** That is not a way in — membership is still
required for every read — but it does mean the exchange, and anybody who can
enumerate identities, can test whether two particular people have ever opened a
direct message. The pair are not hidden from the exchange in any case, since it
enforces the membership list, but a third party with a list of identities can
probe for relationships between them at the cost of one request each. A random
identifier would avoid it and would cost the property that both ends compute the
same name without speaking first.
