## Abstract

An exchange records which device keys belong to which account, by verifying the
SIP-20 credentials the devices present, and answers the question every service
above it needs answered: this connection is a client — whose? It also does the
one thing a portable credential structurally cannot, which is revoke. A person
who loses a phone recovers here, not in SIP-20.

## Motivation

SIP-20 says an account may sign a credential naming a device, and that anybody
holding the account key can verify it with no prior record. That is exactly
right for a peer checking a message it has just received, and it leaves two
things undone.

**Somebody has to be asked.** A connection arrives carrying a device identity
(SIP-3) and a service needs an account. It could require the credential on every
request, which is 154 bytes on each one and a signature check with it; or it
could be told once. Registration is being told once.

**A credential cannot be withdrawn.** SIP-20 is explicit that `not_after` is the
whole mechanism and that a credential is honoured to its expiry by any verifier
holding no other record — which is the property that makes it portable and is
also useless to somebody whose laptop was stolen this morning. A revocation list
has to live where it can be reached, and it binds only the party that holds it.
This is that party.

It is a small service, and it is deliberately its own document rather than a
section of SIP-16. A registry of who runs which clients is not a fact about
channels; SIP-21 will want it, anything built later will want it, and burying it
inside the chat log's specification would mean a second consumer either
depending on SIP-16 wholesale or reimplementing it.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. Every operation is bound to the caller's
transport identity (SIP-3). A connection carrying no identity has no account and
MUST be refused.

A **person** is an account: an Ed25519 identity. A **client** is a device: a
different Ed25519 identity, bound to an account by a SIP-20 credential. Both are
ordinary keys and nothing distinguishes them but the credential.

### Messages

```
Register | type: u8 = 0x01 | credential |
Revoke   | type: u8 = 0x02 | device[32] |
List     | type: u8 = 0x03 | account[32] |

Ack      | now: u64 |
Devices  | now: u64 | count: u16
         | count × (device[32] || added: u64 || not_after: u64) |
```

`POST /device/{register,revoke,list}`.

`Register` verifies `credential` per SIP-20 — against the account it names, for
the scope the exchange serves — and thereafter maps that device key to that
account. It is idempotent for a device that is already mapped: re-registering
refreshes the credential and changes nothing else, which is how a device renews
before its `not_after` passes. It is **not** available to a device that has been
revoked; see below.

**A device may register itself, or be registered by another device of the same
account.** The caller's SIP-3 identity MUST equal either the credential's
`delegate` or an already-registered device of the account the credential names.

Self-registration is not a convenience: an account key may be held in hardware,
and a hardware key cannot be a transport key at all — SIP-11 exists because of
exactly that. Requiring the account to connect would make the first device of
every hardware-held account impossible to register, which is the deployment this
whole chain was built for. The signature is the authorisation; that is what a
portable credential is.

**A device belongs to exactly one account.** A `Register` presenting a
credential from a different account for a device that is already mapped MUST be
refused while that mapping stands; revoking the device first is what changes it.
Otherwise a connection carrying that device key would have no defined answer to
the question every service above asks, which is whose client this is.

Registration by an existing device matters where a whitelist is enforced
(SIP-24): a new device can be enrolled before it has ever connected, by a client
the exchange already serves. It is the same trust boundary — an existing device
of an account may already seal envelopes to that account's other devices under
SIP-17 — so it grants nothing new.

`Revoke` removes a device. Any registered device of the account may call it,
**except that a device MUST NOT revoke one registered before it**. `Revoke` MAY
name the caller itself, which is how a client signs itself out.

**The account itself may revoke any of its devices, and is exempt from the
seniority rule.** It need not be registered as a device to do so. This is not a
special case so much as the general one: the account key is what signed every
credential, and a design in which it can withdraw none of them is not one
anybody intended. Security considerations below already assumes this — it names
the account key as the recovery when somebody has lost their oldest device —
and without this paragraph no rule here grants it.

Note that the exemption is necessary rather than convenient. An account that
registered itself as a device *after* linking another would be the junior of the
two, so seniority alone would leave it unable to remove a device it authorised.
Seniority exists to stop a compromised recent device evicting its seniors; it
was never meant to bind the authority they all derive from.

**A revocation MUST outlive the credential that was revoked.** The exchange
records the revocation and the time of it, and MUST refuse any `Register` for
that device presenting a credential `issued` at or before that time — until the
revoked credential's own `not_after` has passed, after which it is dead by
SIP-20's rules and the record may be dropped.

Without that rule revocation does nothing against the attacker it exists for.
The credential is **on the device**: somebody holding a stolen phone reads it
out of storage, calls `Register` with it, and the signature still verifies and
the expiry has not passed, so the mapping comes straight back. Every other rule
here would be intact and the mechanism would be decorative.

The rule is stated in terms of `issued` rather than as a permanent ban because a
device may legitimately return — a phone that was mislaid and found. Recovering
it requires the **account** to sign a fresh credential, dated after the
revocation, which is precisely the authority that ought to be needed and
precisely what a thief does not have.

`List` returns an account's devices, oldest first. It is answerable to anybody:
the mapping is public by construction, since every credential carries both keys
in the clear to whoever verifies it.

### Under a whitelist

Where an exchange enforces a whitelist, registration and admission are separate
questions and neither answers the other. Registration says whose device this is;
admission says whether the exchange will serve it at all. A device may be
registered and not admitted, in which case it resolves to an account and is
refused at every protected route; or admitted and not registered, in which case
it connects and is a member of nothing.

A device that is not yet admitted asks with SIP-24, and an administrator
decides. **A credential is evidence and not authority**: it tells an
administrator which account vouches for a key and does not entitle that key to
anything. An exchange MUST NOT admit a device to a whitelist merely because its
credential names an admitted account.

Note the asymmetry that follows, since it is easy to assume otherwise:
revocation here does not remove a whitelist entry. See SIP-24.

### Expiry

A registration expires when its credential does. An exchange MUST NOT resolve a
device whose credential's `not_after` has passed, and SHOULD drop the
registration rather than keep answering with it.

There is no separate TTL. The credential carries its own lifetime and a second,
disagreeing one held by the exchange would be a way for two parties to reach
different conclusions about the same device — which is the failure this whole
arrangement exists to avoid.

### What a consumer does with it

A service resolves the calling device to an account before doing anything else,
and then works in accounts. A device that is not registered acts only as itself:
it is a valid identity and it is nobody's client.

**An account with no registered devices is its own device.** A connection whose
identity is an account the exchange has never been told about resolves to that
account. This is the ordinary single-client case and it must not require anybody
to have understood any of this — one key, one client, no credential, no
registration, exactly as though this SIP did not exist.

**That fallback is a cliff and not a slope, and a client linking its first
sibling MUST register itself in the same act.** The moment any device is
registered the fallback stops applying to that account, and everything sealed
per device (SIP-17) goes to the registered set alone. A client that signs a
credential for a second client without registering itself has therefore just
removed itself from every epoch minted afterwards — it will hold the old keys,
read the history, and silently receive nothing new. Nothing else in this
document hints at that, and it is the first thing a second implementer will get
wrong.

**A device learns which account it acts for from the credential, and MUST
remember it.** `List` maps an account to its devices and there is no inverse:
an exchange resolves the other direction internally and answers no question
about it. But the client needs the same answer — a direct message's identifier
derives from accounts (SIP-16), as does whether it is an admin and whose name
belongs beside a message — and the only place it appears is the credential it
presented at registration. A client that does not persist it will act under its
own device key and compute a different channel for the same conversation.

**Sealing to a newly registered sibling is somebody's job, and this SIP does
not give it to anyone.** SIP-17 permits a device to seal to devices of its own
account without an admin; permission is not obligation, and a device that has
just registered holds no key for any channel its account belongs to. Something
has to run on an existing client and hand them over. The reference
implementation does it whenever the client starts and exposes it as a command;
this document notes the requirement rather than specifying the trigger, because
when to do it is a client's decision and *that* it must happen is not.

### Limits

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| Registered devices per account | 8 |
| Registrations per account per hour | 16 |
| Revocation records retained | until the credential's `not_after` |

An overrun MUST be refused distinguishably from a malformed request.

## Rationale

**Why a registry rather than presenting the credential every time.** Carrying it
on each request costs 154 bytes and a signature verification per operation, and
a signature check on the hot path of a chat service is a poor trade for state
that changes a few times a year. It would also leave nowhere to revoke.

**Why the seniority rule on revocation.** Without it, a device can evict every
other device of its account, so somebody who steals a freshly linked laptop uses
it to remove the phone that would have removed it. Ordering by registration time
costs one comparison and makes the attack run the wrong way: the older device
always wins, and the older device is overwhelmingly the one its owner still has.
It is not a complete answer — a thief with the *oldest* device can still evict
the rest — and no rule available here is.

**Why a device serves one account and not several.** A client acting for two
people is a coherent thing to want — one laptop, a work identity and a personal
one — and it is a different design from this one. It would require every request
to say which account it was made as, and every rule in SIP-16 and SIP-17 that
resolves a device to an account to resolve it to an account *and a claim*. The
cheaper answer, and the one taken here, is that two identities means two device
keys, which costs a keypair and no protocol.

**Why registration is public.** Withholding the mapping was considered and is
close to pointless: a credential is shown to every peer that verifies a message,
and it carries both keys. Pretending the registry is private would protect
something already published while making a member list impossible to render.

**Why not a lifetime of its own.** An exchange could hold registrations for
longer or shorter than the credential says. Every version of that produces two
answers to "is this device current", and the interesting failures are the ones
where a peer verifying offline and an exchange resolving online disagree.

**Why eight devices.** It is a limit on a person rather than on a protocol, and
it exists to bound the envelope arithmetic in SIP-17, where recipients are
devices rather than accounts. A 256-account channel at eight devices each is
2 048 envelopes on a rotation. Raising it is an operator's choice and it is not
free.

## Compatibility

Purely additive. Three new routes, no change to any existing wire format, and no
change to SIP-20, which anticipated a service-side revocation list and describes
one without requiring it.

The single-device case is unaffected in both directions: a client that never
registers anything, talking to an exchange that does not implement this SIP,
behaves exactly as it did — its identity is its account, and every service above
resolves it to itself.

## Security considerations

**The registry is a durable, public record of how many clients a person runs and
when they added each one.** That is more than the exchange needed before, it is
readable by anybody, and it is a reasonable proxy for how many machines somebody
owns and roughly when they got them. Revoked devices SHOULD be forgotten rather
than kept as history; nothing here requires an exchange to forget them and an
operator should decide deliberately.

**Revocation binds this exchange and nothing else.** A peer verifying a SIP-20
credential offline honours it to its `not_after` regardless of what has been
revoked here, so a stolen device keeps working against anyone not asking this
registry. This is the cost of a portable credential and the reason lifetimes
should be short. An implementation MUST NOT present revocation as though it were
global.

**A revoked device keeps every key it held.** Revocation is prospective
everywhere in this design: it stops the exchange resolving the device and stops
future envelopes reaching it (SIP-17), and does nothing about the epochs already
collected. A revocation SHOULD therefore be followed by a rotation of every
channel the account belonged to, exactly as a removal is — and SIP-17 lets an
ordinary member perform that rotation for their own revocation rather than
waiting on an admin, because otherwise this advice could not be acted on in any
channel the person does not run.

**A device can sign itself out and that is the only self-service act.** The
seniority rule means a compromised recent device cannot evict its seniors, and
it equally means somebody who has lost their *oldest* device cannot use a newer
one to remove it. Recovering from that requires the account key, which for a
hardware-held identity is the point of holding it in hardware and for a
software-held one is another key to have lost.

**A stolen device holds its own credential, which is why revocation is keyed on
`issued`.** Everything needed to register is on the device itself, so a
revocation that merely deleted a mapping would be undone by one request from
whoever has the hardware. Refusing credentials dated at or before the revocation
means undoing it requires the account key, which is not on the phone. An
implementation that treats `Register` as unconditionally idempotent has removed
the only recovery this design offers.

**Registration is an unauthenticated write from the account's perspective.**
Anybody holding a valid credential registers the device it names, and the
exchange cannot tell a device its owner is holding from one an attacker has
extracted. That is the same statement SIP-20 makes about bearer authorisation,
restated here because this is where it becomes durable.

## Reference implementation

Implemented in [sqex](https://github.com/wave-cl/sqex) v0.9.0, and usable from a
client since v0.10.3:

- `sqex-proto::device` holds `Register`, `Revoke` and `List`.
- `sqexd::device` is the registry, and `route()` in `sqexd::server` resolves
  device to account **once**, at the top, so that every handler below it works in
  whichever of the two it should: membership, roles and display are per account;
  sealing subkeys, message counters and prekeys are per device.
- Routes: `POST /device/{register,revoke,list}`.
- `sqex-chat device {link,claim,list,revoke,reseal}` is the client surface.
  `link` signs a credential and registers the signing client in the same act;
  `claim` presents it, records the account, publishes prekeys and collects what
  its siblings have already sealed.

**The sentence about sealing subkeys being per device was aspirational until
v0.10.3.** Counters genuinely were, but the two key-distribution routes worked
in accounts at both ends, so nothing could be sealed to a second device — see
SIP-17's reference implementation, which describes how that went unnoticed for a
whole release line.

`/device/register` has no exemption from the identity requirement, and an
earlier draft of this SIP gave it one. The exemption was removed: a route that
answers a caller the exchange would not otherwise serve is a hole in the
principle SIP-2 and SIP-3 exist to establish, whatever the bootstrap
convenience. A new device is registered by an existing device of the same
account, or it asks through SIP-24, and there is no third way.

An account with no registered devices resolves to itself, which is the ordinary
single-client case and means nothing here is required before the rest of the
stack works.

Integration tests cover one person's two devices sharing a membership; a revoked
device unable to re-register with the credential it still holds — the case where
deleting a mapping alone would be decorative, since the credential is on the
stolen phone; a junior device unable to evict its senior; a device belonging to
exactly one account; and the registry surviving a restart. Since v0.10.3 they
also cover the account revoking a device it never registered beside, and being
exempt from seniority while a stranger is still refused.
