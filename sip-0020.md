## Abstract

A signed, self-contained artifact by which one Ed25519 identity — an **account**
— authorises another — a **delegate** — to act on its behalf within a named
scope, until a stated time. It is verified by anyone holding the account's public
key, with no prior record of the grant and no involvement from whoever issued it.
SIP-11 specified this SIP's existence and its shape, and deferred it until a
second consumer needed one. Chat is that consumer: it is what lets one person
run several devices.

## Motivation

SIP-11 documents how a hardware identity that cannot itself be a transport key
puts a software key onto a service's whitelist — by signing a SIP-10 grant that
the service records. It closes with the case that pattern does not serve, and
names what would:

> A **portable delegation credential** — a signed, self-contained
> `{ identity, delegate, scope, not_after }` artifact that a service verifies
> without having seen the grant, with expiry and explicit scope — would serve
> the case this pattern does not: delegation checked by a party that holds no
> server-side record. It is deliberately not specified here; it should be its
> own Standards Track SIP, taken up when a second consumer needs it.

SIP-16 is that consumer. A channel's membership is a list of people, but a
connection is made by a **client**, and a person has more than one. Without a
way to say "this key is a device of that account", the two are the same thing,
which forces one client per person — and in SIP-17 it does worse than that. The
per-sender subkey is derived from the sender's Ed25519 key and each sender
counts its messages from zero, so two clients sharing an identity derive the
same subkey and reuse the same nonce. ChaCha20-Poly1305 answers that by leaking
the XOR of the two plaintexts and losing its authentication guarantee entirely.

So this is not only a missing feature. Separating the account from the device is
what makes the nonce space safe, and the credential is what performs the
separation.

A second-party check is what rules out the alternative of a server-side registry
alone. An exchange could simply be told which devices belong to whom, and it
does hold such a registry — but the parties that most need to know are the
*other members*, who are the ones that verify proofs, check edit authority and
decide what to display. They hold no record of any grant and must not have to
ask the exchange to vouch for one.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. `DELEGATION_CONTEXT` is the ASCII string
`sqnr-delegate-v1`.

### The credential

```
Credential | account[32] | delegate[32] | scope_len: u8 | scope
           | issued: u64 | not_after: u64 | signature[64] |
```

`account` and `delegate` are Ed25519 public keys. `scope` is an ASCII service
label of at most `MAX_SCOPE` bytes. `issued` and `not_after` are Unix seconds;
`not_after` MUST be greater than `issued`.

```
signing_input = DELEGATION_CONTEXT || SHA-256(credential without signature)
signature     = Ed25519(account_secret, signing_input)
```

Hash-then-sign over a fixed-width input, exactly as SIP-10 does and for the same
reason: the signing input is 48 bytes whatever the credential's length — the
16-byte context and a 32-byte digest — so a hardware key that cannot stream a
large message can still produce one. A
YubiKey holding the account identity signs its own devices into existence.

### Verification

A verifier, given the credential bytes, the `account` it expects, and the scope
it is authorising, MUST perform these checks and MUST stop at the first failure:

1. **Decode.** The credential is exactly the length its `scope_len` implies,
   with no trailing bytes, and `scope_len` is at most `MAX_SCOPE`.
2. **Account.** `credential.account` equals the account key the verifier already
   holds. A credential naming an account the verifier did not ask about is not
   evidence of anything.
3. **Validity.** `issued <= now <= not_after` by the verifier's own clock. A
   verifier MAY allow a small skew on `issued` and MUST NOT allow any on
   `not_after`.
4. **Scope.** `credential.scope` equals the scope being authorised, compared as
   bytes. A credential for one scope MUST NOT authorise another, and a verifier
   MUST NOT treat an unrecognised scope as permissive.
5. **Signature.** Ed25519-verify `signature` over `DELEGATION_CONTEXT ||
   SHA-256(credential without signature)` under `account`.

The delegate key is not checked for anything. It is a name the account has bound
itself to, and whether the holder of it is present is a question the *transport*
answers — SIP-3 proves possession at the handshake, and this credential says
what that possession means.

### Scope

Scope is a flat ASCII label, not a hierarchy and not a permission set. `sqex-chat`
is the label this document defines; others belong to whoever defines them.

A flat label is deliberate. A permission grammar would have to be parsed
identically by every verifier before it could be trusted, and disagreement about
what a scope permits is worse than having no scope, because the failure is
silent and lands in the permissive direction. One string, compared for equality,
cannot be misread.

### Chaining is not permitted

A delegate MUST NOT issue credentials of its own. A credential names the account
that signed it, and a verifier checks it against the account it already holds;
there is no path by which a chain would be verified, and none is offered.

The reason is worth stating rather than leaving to inference. A chain needs
every link checked, a depth limit, and a rule for what a shortened scope means
at each step — and the first implementation to get any of that wrong grants
authority nobody issued. A device that needs to authorise something asks the
account to sign it.

### Revocation

There is none in the credential. `not_after` is the whole mechanism, and a
credential is valid until it expires whatever anybody later wishes.

A service MAY keep a revocation list and refuse a delegate on it — SIP-16's
device registry does exactly that, and it is what makes a lost phone actionable
in practice. That is a service's own record and binds only that service. A
verifier holding no such record honours the credential to its `not_after`, which
is the property that makes it portable and is also the reason short lifetimes
matter. See Security considerations.

### Limits

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| `MAX_SCOPE` | 32 bytes |
| Credential | 145 + `scope_len` bytes |
| RECOMMENDED `not_after - issued` | 90 days |

## Rationale

**Why not reuse SIP-10.** A SIP-10 transaction is bound to one `server` key and
consumes a single-use nonce issued by that server. Both are deliberate: they
stop a transaction captured against one service being replayed at another. Both
are also exactly wrong here. A device credential must verify at any exchange the
account ever uses, and to every other member of every channel, none of whom
issued a nonce or appear in the artifact. Same signing discipline —
domain-separated, hash-then-sign, hardware-signable — different artifact,
because the binding requirements are opposite.

**Why an expiry rather than a revocation list.** A revocation list has to be
reachable to be checked, which makes an offline verifier either unable to verify
or willing to accept anything, and the second is what implementations choose. An
expiry is carried by the thing itself and needs nobody. The cost is that a
compromised delegate stays valid until it expires, which is why the recommended
lifetime is ninety days rather than years, and why services that *can* hold a
list are encouraged to.

**Why `issued` is present when `not_after` would do.** It bounds how long a
credential can have been sitting unused before it is first presented, it lets a
verifier apply policy about credentials older than it expects, and it makes the
lifetime legible to a person reading one. It costs eight bytes.

**Why the account signs the device rather than the device asserting the
account.** The obvious alternative — a device stating which account it belongs
to and the account confirming when asked — needs the account online at
verification time, which is the thing hardware-held identities are least able
to be. Signing once, in advance, is what the artifact is for.

**Why scope is required rather than optional.** An optional scope is an absent
scope in practice, and a credential with no scope authorises everything the
account can do, forever until expiry, at every service. Requiring the field
makes the blast radius of a stolen device a decision somebody made rather than
one nobody noticed.

## Compatibility

Purely additive and entirely new. No existing wire format changes and no
existing SIP requires amendment: SIP-11 anticipated this document and needs no
correction, since the pattern it describes remains correct for the case it
describes.

It is not a replacement for SIP-11. A server-side whitelist grant and a portable
credential answer different questions — "may this key connect to this service"
and "whose device is this" — and a deployment may well want both.

## Security considerations

**A credential is a bearer authorisation for its scope until it expires.**
Anyone holding the delegate's private key is the account for that scope. This is
the same shape as SIP-13's room secret and has the same absence at its centre:
no revocation that a verifier holding no record can honour. `not_after` bounds
it in time and nothing bounds it in the meantime.

**A lost device is only actionable where a record exists.** An exchange can
refuse a revoked device, and SIP-16's registry is where a person actually
recovers from losing a phone. Another member verifying a credential offline
cannot, and will accept messages from that device until the credential expires.
An implementation MUST NOT describe revocation as though it were global.

**The credential is public and identifies its account.** It carries both keys in
the clear and is shown to anyone who verifies it, so a delegate key is
permanently linked to its account for everyone who sees one. There is no
unlinkable form here and none is attempted.

**Expiry is checked against the verifier's clock, which may be wrong.** A
verifier with a clock set far forward rejects valid credentials, and one set far
back accepts expired ones. The `now` echoed by exchange services (SIP-4, SIP-16)
gives a client something to notice a bad clock against; there is no defence
against a verifier that is confidently wrong, and this is the failure mode to
test for.

**Scope confusion is the misuse to guard against.** A verifier that accepts any
credential naming the right account, without comparing scope, converts a chat
device into an administrator. The check is one byte comparison and it is step 4
for a reason.

**Compromise of the account key is total and unrecoverable here.** An attacker
signs themselves a device and is indistinguishable from the person. Nothing in
this SIP detects that, and nothing in it recovers from it. That is the cost of
an artifact with no issuer to consult.

## Reference implementation

Implemented in [sqex](https://github.com/wave-cl/sqex) v0.9.0:

- `sqex-proto::credential` holds the 145-byte-plus-scope layout, `issue` and
  `verify`. The signing input covers the delegate, the scope and both bounds, so
  a credential cannot be lifted into another service or another device.
- `sqexd` verifies rather than issues. Nothing in the exchange mints a
  credential and nothing needs to: an account signs for its own devices, which
  is the whole of the mechanism.

The scope is checked against the route's own scope and not against a list, so
adding a service does not mean revisiting this. A credential for another service
is not a chat device, and there is a test that says so by name.

Integration tests cover a device enrolled with a credential its account signed;
a stranger unable to enrol a device onto an account they do not hold; a
credential for another service refused; and an expired credential ceasing to
resolve, which is the property that makes the bound worth carrying.
