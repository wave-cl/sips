## Abstract

Alice, whose home is `squic.org`, wants to write to Bob, whose home is
`trunk.exchange`. Today she can call him (SIP-39) and nothing else: a
direct message or an invitation she makes at her exchange lives there,
and Bob learns of it only by connecting to an exchange that is not his.
This document closes the gap with four small pieces, each on a mechanism
that already exists. Alice's exchange **locates** Bob -- finds his
exchange by the domain, resolves the name, learns his home and his
devices -- and remembers where he lives. It **proxies** the one-time
things she needs from his home, a device list and a prekey, so she
seals to him as she seals to anyone. When she puts him in a channel it
**tells** his home, and his home, holding his own statement that it is
his home (SIP-59), carries that statement to hers and pulls the channel
for him; from then on he reads and writes it where he is, by SIP-43.
And a **direct message lives at the home of the lower key**, so the two
of them never make two.

Two more pieces came from running it. The member's own word: a device
tells its home which origin to pull from (`/account/hint`), and a home
that carried a create to an origin records that origin for the member
itself, without waiting to be told what it already knows. And the Move a
client presents unasked is bounded to the exchange its store is filed
under, or none yet; anywhere else the client is a **visitor** -- it
presents nothing, says so, and moves only when told -- so a wrong
address is not a Move.

That rule is applied to the direct messages that already exist twice: two
exchanges can each hold a channel of the pair's identifier, each a complete
conversation with its own incarnation, key and log, and neither aware of the
other. The copy at the lower key's home is the conversation and the other is
a **stray**; the home, on learning of a stray, tells the exchange holding
it, and that exchange **folds** it -- ends its log, keeps it and its files
for both parties to read, points the identifier at the conversation, and
refuses to open another. A client keeps what it read of the folded copy
beneath the conversation that continues, so nothing either of them wrote is
lost.

An exchange that holds *some* devices for an account that lives elsewhere --
its former home, above all -- answers `/device/list` with what the home
lists, holding its own registry only for the case where the home cannot be
asked, and says which of the three it gave, so a client sealing a key to a
stale list waits and says so while a reader takes any of the three.

## Motivation

SIP-39 put the shape of federation in one sentence: a client holds one
connection, to its own exchange, and its exchange talks to the others.
Calls have had that since; conversations have not. SIP-43 let a member
post into a channel from a copy, SIP-59 let a home hold copies of
everything its accounts are in and carry their posts back -- but a home
pulls only the channels it has been told about, by the account, and the
account can only tell it about channels it already knows. A channel
somebody *else* makes for you, at an exchange you have never connected
to, is exactly the one nobody can tell your home about. That is every
first message from a stranger, and every invitation.

The other half of a first message is the key. A private channel's epoch
key reaches a member sealed to a prekey of each of their devices (SIP-17,
SIP-23), and those prekeys are published at the member's home. Alice
cannot take one from a pool at an exchange she is not a client of;
SIP-35 says why the pool must not be copied. What she can do is ask her
own exchange, and her exchange can ask Bob's -- one request, one prekey,
served once, as it would have been served to her directly.

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-76.* Three live runs on an exchange nobody could reach inbound -- a laptop
behind its own address, taking part as a home -- ended the same way. A
member at the laptop opened a direct message that lived at
`trunk.exchange` (the lower key's home), and trunk, which could not open
a connection to the laptop, could not tell it; the member's client
waited ten seconds for a copy that was never coming and gave up. The
laptop had itself carried the create to trunk moments before. It knew
the origin. It had the member's own signed act naming it. It waited to
be told anyway.

The same happens without a laptop: an origin that is briefly unable to
reach a home, a home behind a firewall that admits only what it dials,
an origin from before §Telling the home that tells nobody. §Telling the home wrote *the
member finds the channel the way they always could* -- by connecting to
the origin -- and SIP-8's whitelist is exactly what stops that.

What is missing is the member's own statement. A home acts for an
account on the account's signature (SIP-59); the account's device is
connected to the home already, authenticated by SIP-3; and what the home
needs to know -- *pull from there* -- is a fact the device holds.

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-82.* The reference client's store is filed under one exchange -- its home
-- and re-filed when the account moves (SIP-59). The client therefore
knows, before it asks anything, whether the exchange it just connected
to is the one it lives at. The rule as first written (§A client says
where it lives) did not use that: it asked the *exchange* whether it had
a record, and an exchange that had never met
the account said no, which is also what the account's home would say
after losing its state. The two cases are different to the client and
were the same to the rule.

It happened while testing §Devices of an account that moved: a scratchpad tool connected to a
laptop exchange as an account homed at trunk.exchange, to ask one
question about somebody else's devices, and moved the account to the
laptop on the way in. The laptop was listed nowhere and could reach
trunk.exchange, but the Move named no origins, so trunk.exchange went
on recording itself as home; the account had two homes and one of them
was a machine that would be switched off within the hour. The Move
that undid it was the same one request.

The user's own client has the same rule. `sqex-chat --server-host` at
an exchange one has never used is a Move, silently, with nothing to
say so afterwards but `sqex-chat home` at the right exchange.

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-71.* SIP-44 stated the problem and did not solve it, and SIP-59 stated it
again: an account moving home may find its new home already ordering a
direct message of the same name, and the home skips the pull, so *the
account reads that one where it was* -- which is two conversations with
one name, found by the same client under the same key, one of them
reachable only by connecting somewhere else.

§Where a direct message lives fixed where a direct message is *opened*. It did not fix the
ones opened before it, the ones opened by a client that could not locate
the other party, or the ones two people opened at their own exchanges
before either exchange federated with the other. On 2026-09-17, during
that section's own live run, two clients that had each derived the same
identifier at two exchanges found two channels, and the client-side
alias that made the conversation findable again was a repair to a
store, not a rule about which channel was the conversation. Every
account that has ever written to the same person from two exchanges has
one of these.

Nothing in the log can settle it. Both channels are well-formed; both
constitutions are signed; both pairs of members are the rightful pair
by SIP-16's arithmetic. The two logs cannot be merged: their positions,
receipts and sealing keys belong to different incarnations, and SIP-44
already declined to rewrite an identifier for less reason. What can be
settled is which one continues, and the answer §Where a direct message lives gives for a new
direct message is the right one for an old one too.

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-75, via SIP-71.* The first draft of the fold said, of attachments: *a blob is
pruned with its channel, and the folded log carries the entries that
referred to them.* That was stated for the sake of stating it, and it is wrong in the way that
matters: the log carries a reference the reader can decode -- the
blob's name, its key, its shape, a preview -- and nothing to fetch. A
photograph sent in a conversation that turned out to be a stray is
shown as a photograph that was sent and cannot be seen. The fold's whole
claim was that nothing either of them wrote is lost; a file is a thing
one of them wrote.

SIP-18 already has the shape for keeping a blob past a channel: a blob
belongs to its **attachments**, not to a message, and lives while any
attachment does. What a fold needs is an attachment that outlives the
channel it was made in, for the window the log does, held by the pair
the log is held for.

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-81.* The former-home case is the ordinary one. An account lives at X for a
year, registers a phone and a laptop there, and moves to H. Its
conversations with X's members go on being ordered at X (SIP-59: "the
exchange you left goes on serving the conversations that live there");
the members at X go on sealing every epoch key to the account's devices
as X lists them. The account loses the phone, revokes it at H, links a
new one at H. X never hears: revocation is the home's (SIP-22), and
the new phone registered at H. Every key at X from then on is sealed
to a device in a drawer and not to the one in the account's hand -- and
the account's new phone reads nothing at X until the account itself
notices and rotates, which SIP-17 lets it do, once it has worked out
why.

§Proxying the key's services saw this for an exchange that never knew the account,
and asked the home. It did not ask for an exchange that once knew it, and that
is the exchange whose list is most confidently wrong: not empty, just
old.

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-83, via SIP-81.* The first two kinds are current, by the party that knows. The third is
a snapshot from before the account left: a device revoked since is on
it, one linked since is not, and after a handover (SIP-44) the new
key's devices are nowhere in it. §Whose list chose to answer it rather than
refuse, because a refusal would break the read path -- a channel would
be unreadable through a home's outage for want of a binding that only
needs *a* credential, not the current set -- and accepted that a key
sealed from it might reach a device in a drawer, for the outage's
duration, as before.

The two uses want different things, and the answer could not say which
it was. A rotation sealed to a stale list is not an outage-sized cost:
the revoked device holds the new epoch, and the account's real device
does not, until somebody rotates again for a reason they cannot see.
Better to not rotate. A client that knows the list is stale can wait a
cycle, or tell the person; a client that does not know rotates and
hopes.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. **Home** is SIP-59's: the exchange
an account signed as its own. **Origin** is SIP-43's. The **home task** is SIP-59's; a
**device** and the account it registers under are SIP-22's; a store
**filed under** an exchange is SIP-40's and SIP-59's.

### Locating

```
Locate  | len: u16 | target |
Located | account[32] | home[32] | dom_len: u8 | domain | Devices |
```

`POST /account/locate` is answered to any identified caller. `target`
is `label@domain`, UTF-8, where `label` is a SIP-38 name or a base58
account key and `domain` is where an exchange is found by SIP-33. The
exchange finds the domain's exchange -- discovering it or using the
link it holds, checking the key it finds against the one it has pinned
-- resolves the label there where it is a name (as SIP-39 resolves a
callee), asks it `/account/home` (SIP-59) and follows the answer once
where it names a third exchange, and asks the home `/device/list`
(SIP-22). `Devices` is the home's answer as SIP-22 lays it out,
credentials included (SIP-32), so the caller verifies each device
against the account itself and takes nothing on the exchange's word. A
domain this exchange does not federate with is refused
`not_authorised`; a domain that cannot be reached, or a label that
resolves to nothing, `not_found`.

The exchange records the answer as the account's **learned home**: the
home's key, its domain, and when it was learned. A learned home is the
domain's exchange's word about who lives there -- the same trust a
SIP-38 resolution places in it, and no more. It is kept apart from a
signed Move (SIP-59) and is never mistaken for one: a learned home makes
this exchange **proxy** and **tell**, below; it never opens the acts-for
gate, which stays on the account's own signature. Where the exchange
holds both for one account, the signed one is what it goes by.

### Proxying the key's services

For a device whose account's home -- signed or learned -- is another
exchange, this exchange answers two routes by asking the home as a
client and handing back what it was given:

- `/prekey/take` (SIP-23): one prekey, taken at the home, served here
  once. The pool is not copied and nothing is kept; the take is the
  home's and the answer is its answer. A home from before this
  document, or one that will not admit this exchange, is `no_prekey`.
- `/device/list` (SIP-22), for an account with no devices registered
  here: the home's list, credentials included.

The home admits the asking exchange's key as SIP-39 already requires it
to for name resolution; an operator who federates with an exchange
lists its key.

### Devices of an account that moved

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-81.* SIP-22 keeps an account's devices in the registry of
the exchange they registered at, and SIP-17 seals a channel's key to every
device of every member as `/device/list` reports them. §Proxying the key's services has
an exchange that holds **no** devices for an account ask its home; an
exchange that holds *some* -- the account's former home, where its devices
registered before the Move -- would answer those, which are the devices
the account had when it left. The sections to §At a former home have it
answer what the home lists instead. **Home**, **away** and **Move** are
SIP-59's; `/device/list`, `Devices` and the **registry** are SIP-22's.

### Whose list

On `/device/list` for an account, an exchange answers:

1. **its own registry**, where the account's home is this exchange or
   nowhere on record -- SIP-22 as it stands;
2. **the home's list**, where the account is recorded as living at
   another exchange, by its signed Move or a learned home -- whether or
   not this exchange holds devices of its own for it. The home is asked
   `/device/list` as §Proxying the key's services asks it, and its answer is served
   unchanged, credentials included: it is the home's word, as that
   section already made it, and this exchange verifies nothing of it;
3. **its own registry**, where the account lives elsewhere and the home
   could not be asked -- unreachable, refusing, or from before §Proxying the key's services --
   as that section answers its own (empty) list in that case. The
   answer is what this exchange held when the account left, and a
   caller asking the old way cannot tell it from a current one (§Saying whose list it is).

### Caching

An exchange MAY keep the home's answer for an account for up to
`DEVICES_TTL = 60` seconds and serve it again within that time. The
`now` in a served `Devices` is the home's own from the answer kept,
not this exchange's clock; a caller checking a credential's validity
against `now` checks it as of the home's answer, which is at most a
minute old. A failed ask is not kept: the next `/device/list` asks
again.

### Saying whose list it is

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-83, via SIP-81.*

```
ListDevicesFrom | type: u8 = 0x05 | account[32] |
DevicesFrom     | from: u8 | Devices |
```

`POST /device/list` accepts `ListDevicesFrom` (type `0x05`) beside
`ListDevices` (`0x03`), and dispatches on the type byte. The answer to
`ListDevicesFrom` is `DevicesFrom`: `from`, then SIP-22's `Devices`
exactly as `/device/list` would answer `ListDevices` for the same
account at the same moment. `from` is:

| `from` | the list is |
|---|---|
| `0x00` **here** | this exchange's own registry: the account's home is this exchange, or nowhere on record |
| `0x01` **the home's** | the home's answer, fresh or kept within §Caching's `DEVICES_TTL` |
| `0x02` **stale** | this exchange's own registry for an account recorded as living elsewhere, because the home could not be asked |

A decoder refuses any other value, and refuses trailing bytes as
`Devices` does. `ListDevices` goes on being answered with `Devices`,
unchanged, for every client from before sqex 0.100.0.

### Sealing to a list

A client about to **seal a channel key** to an account's devices --
minting an epoch, rotating, removing a member -- asks with
`ListDevicesFrom`. On `0x02` it MUST NOT seal to that list: it leaves
the epoch where it is, reports that the member's home could not be
asked, and tries again later. On `0x00` or `0x01` it seals as SIP-17
says. A client that asks an exchange from before sqex 0.100.0 is
refused `malformed` for the unknown type byte, and falls back to
`ListDevices`, which is what it did before.

A client **reading** -- binding a signature to an account, listing a
member's devices for display -- MAY ask either way and use any answer:
a credential that verifies is evidence whichever list carried it.

### At a former home

`/device/register` and `/device/revoke` from one of the account's own
devices join the routes a former home refuses with `moved` (SIP-59):
the former home answers the home's list, so a registration made here
would be seen by nobody, and the device is told where to go instead.
(An earlier draft left these routes served and said why; the reason
did not survive the rule above it.) Nothing is carried to the home: a
device registers there itself.

### Telling the home

Whenever this exchange writes a present membership for an account whose
home is another exchange -- a `Create` naming it an invitee, an `Invite`,
a direct message opened with it, a forwarded create (below) -- it tells
the home:

```
PeerInvited | type: u8 = 0x10 | account[32] | channel[32] | dom_len: u8 | domain |
```

`POST /peer/invited` is a peering route (SIP-35), gated by the peer list
and answered with SIP-16's `Ack`; `domain` is the origin's own, the hint
the home finds it by. Sent once per membership written, best effort: an
origin that cannot reach the home has told nobody, and the member finds
the channel the way they always could -- or tells its home itself, and a
home that carried the create hints itself (§The home hints itself). Refused uniformly like every
peering route.

The home, on `/peer/invited` for an account that holds a Move naming
this exchange (SIP-59), records the origin among the account's hints,
carries the Move to it (`/peer/moved`), and pulls at once. It does no
more than that, and for an account with no Move here it does nothing:
a home acts for an account on the account's signature, and the
invitation was the origin's word on the strength of a learned home. The
loop closes on three statements, each made by the party entitled to make
it -- the domain's exchange says where the account lives, the account
says which exchange is its home, and the origin serves that home for
that account.

### A device hints its home

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-76.*

```
Hint    | origin[32] | dom_len: u8 | domain |          POST /account/hint
Hinted  | now: u64 | pulling: u8 |
```

From any identified caller. The account is the caller's own: the device
itself where it is registered as nobody's, else the account its
registration here names (SIP-22) -- never one the caller asserts. Where
that account's Move names this exchange (SIP-59), the exchange records
`origin` among the account's hints, with `domain` where given, and wakes
its home task, which pulls from the origin at once; `pulling = 1`. Where
the account is not homed here, nothing is recorded and `pulling = 0`: a
home acts for an account on the account's signature, and this device
has not got one. A `domain` may be empty for an origin the exchange
already knows how to reach -- one on its lists, or one it holds a link
or a forwarder to.

A hint is idempotent and adds to the account's hints; it does not
replace them as a Move's `origins` do (SIP-59). An exchange MUST bound
the hints it keeps per account at `MAX_HINTS`, dropping the oldest.

### The home hints itself

An exchange that carries a `create_at` to an origin for an account whose
Move names it (§Where a direct message lives) MUST, on the
origin's `200`, record that origin among the account's hints as if the
account had hinted it, and wake its home task. It knew the origin before
the origin could tell it; §Telling the home remains, and is what informs
a home that carried nothing.

### A client hints

A client SHOULD hint its home:

- after a `create_at` it made, naming the origin it named there --
  belt beside the braces above, for an exchange from before
  §The home hints itself (0.94.0);
- when it learns, from `/channel/home` (SIP-43), that a channel it is a
  member of lives at an exchange other than its own, once per origin;
- when a person tells it where a channel lives, by hand.

A client that opened a direct message elsewhere (§Where a direct message
lives) and is waiting
for the copy SHOULD hint before it waits.

### A client says where it lives

A client holding the account key, on connecting to its exchange, asks
`/account/home` for itself; where the answer is `not_found` or says this
exchange with `since = 0` -- no Move on record -- it signs a Move naming
this exchange (SIP-59) and presents it, once. A linked device does not:
it cannot sign for the account, and the account either did or will. A
Move naming the exchange the client is already at moves nothing and is
what lets the home act when an origin tells it.

*Amended 2026-09-20 (was SIP-82): only under §When a client presents a
Move unasked.*

### When a client presents a Move unasked

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-82.*

A client holding the account key, on connecting to an exchange `E`,
MAY sign and present a Move naming `E` without being told to, only
where **both** hold:

1. `E` has no home on record for the account (`/account/home` answers
   nothing, or a record with `since = 0`), as §A client says where it lives requires; and
2. the client's store is filed under `E`, or under no exchange yet.

Where the store is filed under another exchange `H`, the client is a
**visitor** at `E`: it presents no Move, reads and writes at `E` as
whatever `E` lets a member of channels ordered there do, and tells the
person that the account lives at `H` and that this is `E`. A visitor
moves only by an explicit act (SIP-59's Move as the client's `move`
command performs it), which names `H` and every origin the client
knows, as it does today.

A store filed under no exchange is a first run, and the Move it
presents is the account's first "this is my home". A client that can
be told a home some other way -- a restore (SIP-48), a domain the
person gave it -- SHOULD prefer that to the exchange it happens to be
pointed at.

### What an unasked Move names

A Move a client presents on its own (rule 2's first case: the store's
own home has lost the record) names as origins what `move` would name:
the exchange the store was filed under, and every origin of every
channel the client holds. That exchange is `E` itself here, and the
list is what lets `E` gather what §Telling the home promised.

### The residue of a Move by accident

A visitor at an exchange `E` whose record names **`E` itself** as the
account's home finds one of two parties behind: `E`, holding a Move a
client from before §When a client presents a Move unasked presented there and never displaced
because nothing told it of the later one; or the store, because
another of the account's devices moved the account to `E` since the
store was last filed. The two look the same from the store alone, and
acting in the second case would move the account back over the other
device's Move. So the client keeps, with the store, the `issued` of
the Move that filed it -- every Move it makes or presents -- and:

- where `E`'s record is **earlier** than that, `E` is behind: the
  client signs a Move naming `H`, the store's home, and presents it at
  `E`, which names nowhere the account is not. `E` then records the
  account as away at `H` and serves it as a former home does (SIP-59,
  §Devices of an account that moved). The client says it did this;
- otherwise the client does nothing and says the store may be behind
  -- the account may have been moved here from another device -- and
  leaves the person to `move` or to follow. A store from before this
  document kept no `issued` and reads as behind.

*(The first release told `E` in both cases; the live run below found
the second.)*

A home told of a later Move naming another exchange -- by the client,
or by that exchange over `/peer/moved` -- records it and answers
`moved` for the account's home-only routes (SIP-59), and answers the
home's device list (§Devices of an account that moved), as before; what these rules add is that a
client never causes the case where it is *not* told.

### Where a direct message lives

A direct message's identifier is derived from its two accounts (SIP-16),
so two people who each open it at their own exchange make two channels
with one name, and SIP-16's arithmetic cannot say which is the
conversation. This document does: **a direct message lives at the home
of the lower key** -- the `first` of SIP-16's derivation -- as its home
is known when the message is opened. The party at that home creates it
there as always. The other party creates it *there* too, from where they
are:

```
CreateAt | origin[32] | Create |
```

`POST /channel/create_at` names the origin the `Create` is for; the
request is signed under `origin` as SIP-43 signs every act made through
a copy, and the exchange forwards it as a `ForwardAction` with path
`/channel/create`, which SIP-43's list of honoured paths gains -- along
with `/channel/key/put`, a rotation, since the first message of a
sealed channel opened elsewhere is one. The
origin honours it under the acts-for gate for the creating account (the
forwarding exchange carries that account's Move first, as SIP-59 has it
carry every act), creates the channel as it would for a directly
connected creator -- SIP-16's rules for a returning party and for a
squatter apply unchanged -- and, the creator's home being another
exchange, tells it as above; the forwarding exchange pulls the channel
and the creator reads and writes it there. A client that finds the other
party's home is its own, or cannot locate them, creates at its own
exchange as before.

Groups live where they are made. A group's members are invited by an
admin who is where the group is, and the telling above is what reaches
them.

### A direct message opened twice

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-71.* A direct message's identifier is derived from its two
accounts, so two exchanges can each hold a channel of that name for the same
pair, each a complete conversation with its own incarnation, key and log.
The sections to §The client keeps what it read apply the rule above to the
ones that already exist twice. **Attachment**, **blob** and the fetch rules
are SIP-18's; a **generation** and the trade are SIP-42's (§Generations).

### Which copy is the conversation

A **direct message** is a channel whose identifier equals SIP-16's
derivation over its two present members; `first` is the lesser of the
two keys. The channel is **the conversation** exactly when it is
ordered at, or held as a copy by, the **home of `first`** as SIP-59
records it -- that is, the exchange `first`'s own signed Move names.
A channel of the same identifier ordered anywhere else is a **stray**.

Where the home of `first` orders none and holds none, there is no
conversation to prefer and nothing here applies. Where it holds one
already, that one is the conversation, whatever another exchange
holds: a stray that is elder, longer, or busier is still a stray. This
rule needs no clock and no comparison between exchanges, and it is the
rule a client already applies under §Where a direct message lives when it opens
a direct message.

An account that has not said where it lives has no home to decide, and
its direct messages are left as they are.

### The home learns of a stray

The home of `first` learns that an exchange holds a channel of the
identifier the way SIP-59 has a home learn of anything: by asking an
origin what channels an account of its is in (`/peer/mine`), where the
origin is one the account's Move hinted at, or one that told the home
under §Telling the home (`/peer/invited`). An origin that answers with a direct
message identifier the home already holds -- ordered, or as a copy
from another origin -- is holding a stray, and the home SHOULD tell it
at once and on each later cycle it still answers so:

```
PeerFolded | type: u8 = 0x14 | channel[32] | first[32] | instance[32] | dom_len: u8 | domain |
```

`POST /peer/folded`, over the peering link, gated as `/peer/moved` is
(SIP-59; open peering admits any identified caller, SIP-35). `instance`
is the conversation's SIP-31 incarnation as the home holds it, and
`domain` is where the home is reached by SIP-33, a hint as everywhere.
The home carries `first`'s Move (`/peer/moved`) before it, as SIP-59
has it carry the Move before every act on the account's behalf.

### The stray's exchange verifies, then folds

An exchange receiving `PeerFolded` verifies, in order, and refuses
uniformly with SIP-35's `peering_refused` where any step fails:

1. it **orders** `channel` itself -- a copy it holds is its origin's to
   fold, not the caller's to ask about;
2. `channel`'s present members are exactly two, `first` is the lesser
   of them, and the derivation over the two equals `channel` -- so it is
   a direct message and `first` is its `first`, by arithmetic and not
   by the caller's word;
3. it holds a signed Move for `first` naming the caller: the caller is
   `first`'s home by `first`'s own statement, which is the only
   authority a fold rests on;
4. `instance` is not its own -- a home naming the exchange's own
   incarnation is confused, and there is nothing to fold.

It then **folds** the channel, in one transaction:

- the channel's entries are kept as the **folded log** under
  `(channel, instance)` with the stray's own incarnation, for
  `FOLDED_SECS`, together with the two members' keys;
- the channel is destroyed as SIP-16 destroys a channel whose last
  member left -- its row, members, chain positions and envelopes go,
  its attachments are kept (§Folded attachments), and its incarnation
  is retired (SIP-31) so nothing
  signed under it is ever admitted again;
- a **fold record** `(channel, into = caller, domain, at)` is kept for
  `FOLDED_SECS`.

The stray's exchange answers `200`; `409` when it holds nothing under the
identifier at all (folded already, destroyed, never here); the uniform
refusal otherwise, which says no more than the channel itself would.

### What a fold record does

While a fold record stands, the identifier **points at the
conversation**:

- `Create` naming `channel` is refused with SIP-35's `replicated`,
  naming `into` -- the same refusal a copy gives a write, and for the
  same reason: the channel lives elsewhere. SIP-16's rule for a
  returning party does not apply, because there is no channel here to
  return to; a client that wants to reopen the conversation opens it
  where it lives, which is where §Where a direct message lives would have sent it.
- `/channel/home` for `channel` answers `into` with the domain hint --
  **to the two members**, and to anybody else as it answered before the
  fold. A direct message's identifier can be computed by anyone, and a
  fold record disclosed to a stranger would say that two people talk.
- The fold record is an **origin hint** for the other member, beside the
  hints SIP-59 has an account give itself: where that member is homed
  here, the home task pulls the conversation from `into` as it pulls any
  channel of theirs elsewhere, and wakes at once. The record stands
  apart from the account's own hints, because a later Move replaces
  those and must not lose this. A member who is not homed here learns
  from the refusal and the home answer, as they would for any channel
  that lives elsewhere.

Once the record has expired, the identifier is an ordinary one again.
A stray opened after that is folded again when the home of `first`
next sees it; nothing depends on the record outliving its window.

### Reading the folded log

```
Folded  | type: u8 = 0x23 | channel[32] |            POST /channel/folded
```

A `ByChannel` of type `0x23`, answered to **either member** of the
folded direct message -- both could read it while it was a channel,
and both can now -- with SIP-16's `Entries` as a fetch answers them:
every entry the exchange still holds of the most recent folded log for
that identifier, `first` and `last` its bounds, no signals, no tip. A
caller that is neither member, or an identifier with no folded log, is
answered `not_found`, which is what the channel itself would have
said. Attachments are kept with the log, for the same window and the
same two members (§Folded attachments).

### Folded attachments

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-75, via SIP-71.* When an exchange folds a stray (§The stray's exchange verifies, then
folds), the channel's attachments are **kept as folded
attachments** -- each blob's name, with the fold's time -- rather than
released with the channel. A folded attachment holds its blob alive as
any attachment does (SIP-18 §Lifetime): a blob is deleted when it has
no attachment and no folded attachment left.

A folded attachment expires with the fold record, at `FOLDED_SECS`
after the fold, and MUST be swept on the same schedule as attachments
are; the blob goes then if nothing else holds it. A second fold of the
same identifier replaces the first's folded attachments as it replaces
its log.

### Fetching a folded attachment

SIP-18's `Head` and `Get` are answered for a blob a folded attachment
names **to the two members of the folded direct message**, as they are
answered to a member of a channel the blob is attached to. To anybody
else a folded attachment grants nothing: it is served on the same terms
as the folded log, to the same two people, and the fold record is
where those two are written.

A folded attachment never widens an audience. A blob a folded
attachment names may still be attached to other channels, whose members
fetch it on those channels' terms; SIP-18's rule that a private
attachment overrides a public one stands, and a folded attachment
counts as private for it.

`Attach` and `Detach` do not apply to a folded attachment: the channel
it belonged to is gone, and there is nobody to answer to.

### A copy of a stray

An exchange holding a copy of a stray (a replica under a `0x0b`, or a
home acting for a member) finds, on its next pull, that the origin no
longer serves the channel -- as it finds any channel its origin
destroyed -- and holds what it has until told otherwise. It is told the
way SIP-59 and §Telling the home tell a home anything: an origin it pulls for a
member of the pair lists the conversation, and it pulls that. A copy
MUST NOT carry held positions into the conversation: the conversation is
another incarnation, and a position carried across is SIP-32's fault
exactly. **A copy whose origin serves another incarnation of a channel
than the one it holds drops what it holds and takes the channel afresh**,
which is the rule for a recreated direct message at a replica as much as
for this; and **a channel an exchange orders is never taken as a copy**,
whatever an origin lists -- the stray is folded first, and only then is
the identifier a copy's to hold.

### The client keeps what it read

A client whose channel changed incarnation under it -- the copy it
read replaced by the conversation, at the same identifier, with a
different `instance` (SIP-31) -- MUST keep the messages it had read
and decrypted, shown before the conversation that continues and
marked as an earlier copy, and MUST NOT delete them. SIP-17 forbids
decrypting a counter twice and the exchange serves an epoch key once,
so a message a client drops is one it can never read again.

A client notices the change by the exchange's word -- `Channel`'s
`instance` against the one it holds -- and not only by SIP-16's cursor
rule, which a conversation longer than the stray never trips, nor by
anything it signs, which a reader never does. Before it resets the
sequence space (SIP-16), it SHOULD ask `/channel/folded` at the exchange
it reads from, and where a log is answered, read what it had not yet
read of it under the keys it still holds, so the kept history is whole.
The incarnation it records after the reset MUST be the one the exchange
serves now, whichever rule noticed: a client that recorded a stale one
reset again on its next poll, and took with it an epoch key it had
collected in between, which the exchange serves once. A client from
before §The client keeps what it read (sqex 0.89.0) deletes, as it did, and the entries stay at the
exchange for the window.

A client whose channel is no longer served at all where it read it --
the exchange folded it and holds no copy of the conversation -- and
that is answered a folded log there MUST keep what it read the same way
(found on the 2026-09-19 live run below: the folding exchange could not
reach the lower key's home, so nothing ever replaced the stray).

A client whose `Create` of a direct message is refused `replicated`
opens it at the exchange named, as §Where a direct message lives has it open one at the
lower key's home, and reads the copy its own exchange pulls.

A client showing an earlier copy shows its files as it shows
a live message's -- described from the reference, fetched on request --
and MAY fetch them from the exchange it reads at, which is the one that
folded, or from its own store where SIP-42 put them.

## Rationale

**Why the exchange locates and the client does not.** The client could
discover the domain itself and ask Bob's exchange; SIP-39's motivation
is the answer, and the whitelist is its enforcement: Bob's exchange
admits the exchanges it federates with, not every stranger's client.
One connection, to your own exchange, is the shape, and locating is the
first thing on this path that the exchange does for the client rather
than the client for itself.

**Why a learned home is not a signed one.** Because it is not: the
domain's exchange said it, and a domain's exchange can say what it
likes. What a learned home may cause is a proxied take and a message to
a home -- one prekey served once to a member who was going to be sealed
to anyway, and a notice the home acts on only under its account's own
signature. Nothing served to anybody rests on it.

**Why the home carries the Move rather than the origin asking for it.**
The origin does not know whether the account ever signed one, and asking
would make `/peer/invited` an oracle for it. The home knows, and acting
is its business: an account that never said "this is my home" has not
asked its exchange to gather anything for it.

**Why a client presents a Move naming the exchange it is at.** Without
one, the home can be told and cannot act; with one, everything an
origin tells it about becomes a copy it holds. Signing it on first
connection costs a signature and changes nothing that day, which is why
it is the client's default and not a step to remember.

**Why the lower key.** Any deterministic rule would do; this one is the
derivation's own ordering, already computed to make the identifier, so
neither client learns anything new to apply it. The alternative -- let
both create, then settle the fork as SIP-53 settles one -- strands
somebody's first message every time two people write at once, which is
exactly when a direct message starts.

**Why a forwarded create and not a create at the other exchange.** Alice
is not admitted at Bob's exchange and should not have to be; her
exchange is, on her behalf, by the peer list. The create is her own
signed act, carried as SIP-43 carries her posts, and Bob's exchange
verifies it under her key exactly as if she were connected.

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-76:*

**Why the device's word is enough.** The hint causes one thing: the
home asks an origin what channels the account is in there (`/peer/mine`)
and pulls what the origin serves for that account. The origin serves
only what the account may read; the home holds only what it was already
entitled to hold under the account's Move. A device that lies about an
origin makes its home ask an exchange that will answer with nothing.

**Why the home hints itself.** §Telling the home put the telling at the origin
because the origin is the party that writes the membership. But a
`create_at` is the home writing the membership *through* the origin,
and it holds the origin's key in the request. Waiting for the origin to
say what the home just did was the round trip the laptop could never
complete.

**Why not a Move.** A Move carries hints (SIP-59) and replaces them; it
is signed by the account key, which a linked device does not hold; and
signing a new Move to say *also pull from there* is a heavy instrument
for a light fact.

**What was rejected.** Having the client pull the channel itself from
the origin: it cannot connect there, which is the case the hint
exists for. Having the origin retry the telling: it should, and §Telling the
home says nothing against it, but an origin that can never reach the home
retries forever.

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-82:*

**Why the store's word and not the exchange's.** The exchange can only
say whether *it* has a record. The store says where the account lives,
because SIP-59 re-files it on every Move the account makes from this
client, and SIP-42 keeps a second device's store in step. An
exchange with no record is either a stranger or a home that lost its
state, and only the store can tell which.

**Why a visitor is allowed at all.** A person pointing a client at
another exchange may mean to: to read a public channel there, to ask
where somebody lives (§Locating), to admit themselves (SIP-24). None of
those is a Move, and refusing to connect would make the client unable
to do the thing §Locating exists for. Saying so is enough.

**Why the first run still moves.** A fresh store at an exchange the
person typed is the person choosing a home, and there is no other
source to prefer where none was given. It is stated as the case these
rules do not close: a fresh store pointed at the wrong exchange
moves there, as it did before, and the person sees the home named on
the first screen.

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-71:*

**Why the lower key's home decides, and not the elder copy.** An elder
copy is a comparison between two exchanges' clocks, which SIP-69 found
are not close enough to order two events by, and it would need a
tie-break on top. The home of the lower key is one exchange, deciding
about one account of its own, under the rule every client already
applies when it opens a direct message (§Where a direct message lives) -- so the conversation ends up
where the next `open` would have put it anyway. And the decision costs
nothing: the stray is not lost, only ended, and both parties read all
of it.

**Why one exchange tells and the other acts.** Two exchanges each
deciding which copy is the conversation is SIP-53's *two rehomes* with
a rule needed to make them agree. One decider needs no agreement. The
stray's exchange verifies the decider is who it claims -- the home, by
the account's own signed Move -- and verifies the arithmetic itself;
the one thing it takes on the home's word is that the home holds the
identifier, and a home that lied would only have folded a log into a
conversation that does not exist, where the other member's client
would find nothing and open one, which the home would then hold.

**Why the stray is ended and not carried.** SIP-53 carries a log to a
new origin that continues it; the positions, receipts and head run on.
A stray cannot continue the conversation's log or be continued by it:
two logs, two incarnations, two sets of sealing keys. Rewriting either
into the other's numbering is what SIP-44 refused for identifiers and
would refuse here. What can be done is what SIP-53 does with entries
past a fork -- keep them for the people who wrote them -- and a folded
log is a strand of a whole channel, served to both members because
both were members.

**Why the identifier points on for a window and not for good.** A
pointer kept for good would outlive the conversation it points at:
`first` moves home again, or the pair destroy the conversation and
start over somewhere the pointer does not know. A window covers the
case the pointer is for -- a client that has not yet noticed -- and
after it the rule that made the fold makes the next one.

**Why the client keeps history on every incarnation change and not only
on a fold.** The client cannot always tell the two apart, and it does
not need to: a message this person read is theirs, whichever channel
it was read in, and SIP-16's reset was about sequence spaces, not about
forgetting. Deleting was an implementation's shortcut, and it was
lossy in the one way SIP-17 makes permanent.

**What was rejected.** Letting the *higher* key's home fold its own
stray on learning the lower's home: it can, safely, but two actors need
a rule to agree and one actor needs none. Merging by re-posting the
stray's messages into the conversation: the messages would carry the
wrong author's signature (the re-poster's) and the wrong time. A field
in the `Create` naming the intended home, so a stray could be detected
at creation: an assertion, and §Where a direct message lives already sends the create to
the right place when the home is known.

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-75, via SIP-71:*

**Why an attachment and not a copy of the file.** SIP-18 keeps a file
alive by attachment and lets a message expire with it; a fold that
copied the bytes into some folded store would be a second lifetime
rule for the same bytes. Keeping the attachment, with a new holder and
a fixed window, is the mechanism already there.

**Why the pair and not the channel's members.** The channel has no
members: it was destroyed. Who may read the folded log is written in
the fold record, and who may fetch the files it names should be the
same two people, for the same reason -- both could while it was a
channel.

**Why the fetch is from the folding exchange.** That is where the blob
is, and where the log is served from. A client that reads the folded
log there reads the files there too; the other member's
client, at the other exchange, reads through its own connection to the
folding exchange as §Reading the folded log has it read the log.

**What was rejected.** Carrying the blobs to the conversation's home:
the conversation's channel is another log, and attaching a stray's
files to it would show files nobody posted there. Keeping the
attachments for good: the window above is the answer -- a client that
has not read its earlier copy in a week has had its chance, and a
sibling that did holds the file.

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-81:*

**Why the home's list over the union.** A union of the former home's
registry and the home's answer would keep a revoked device in the
list, which is the failure §Whose list exists for. The home is where
the account revokes (SIP-22) and links, and after a handover (SIP-44) the only
place its current key's devices are; every other list is a snapshot of
it.

**Why the own registry when the home cannot be asked, and not a
refusal.** A refusal here breaks the two things a client does with the
list at once: sealing a key, which could wait, and binding a signature
to an account for display, which is on the read path of every poll and
would leave a channel unreadable while a home was down. A stale list
lets a key be sealed to a device that may be gone -- the state before
§Whose list -- for the duration of an outage, and keeps reading
working. It is stated as the weaker answer rather than dressed as the
current one, and §Sealing to a list lets a client that asks see it.

**Why a cache, and why a minute.** §Proxying the key's services's ask was for an
account the
exchange had never seen, a rare case. This one fires for every away
member of every channel a client binds on every poll, and a client
already asks for sixty at once. A minute is long enough to collapse a
poll's asks into one round trip per account and short enough that a
revocation at the home is seen at the former home before the account
has finished telling the person who took the phone. The home's `now`
is served rather than this exchange's so that nothing is asserted
twice.

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-83, via SIP-81:*

**Why a new type byte and not a field.** SIP-34: a decoder that admits
slack is malleable, so every answer refuses trailing bytes, and growth
is by a new type byte with the old one reserved forever. The route
already dispatches on type bytes (SIP-24's admission request rides on
`/device/register`'s vocabulary the same way), so the request side
costs one byte and the answer side is a new shape that names the
request it answers, which is what SIP-34 asks.

**Why the seal path waits and the read path does not.** Stated above.
The cost of a stale seal is silent and lasting; the cost of a stale
read is nothing, since a credential verifies or does not on its own.

**Why the client says so.** A rotation that does not happen looks,
from inside the client, like a rotation that did; SIP-17 has no
"pending". The person removing a member wants to know the removal has
not yet taken.

## Compatibility

Additive: three client routes, one peering message, one honoured forward
path, and one proxied behaviour on two existing routes. It amends one
line of SIP-59: `/prekey/take` for a device of an account that moved
away is no longer refused `moved` but answered from the home, which is
what a sender wanted from it. An exchange from
before this document refuses `/account/locate` and `/channel/create_at`
with `not_found`, answers `/prekey/take` for a device it does not hold
with `no_prekey` as it always did, and refuses `/peer/invited` as an
unknown peering message -- so a home from before it is never told, and
its members find channels at other exchanges by connecting there, as
they did. A client from before it opens a direct message at its own
exchange; a client from after it, finding the other party's home is
older, does the same thing.

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-76.* Additive: one client route and one rule on an existing one. An exchange
from before §A device hints its home (0.94.0) refuses `/account/hint` with `not_found`; a
client that sent it falls back to waiting, as it did. A home from before
that does not hint itself, and is told by the origin as before.

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-82.* The visitor rule is client only: no wire change and nothing at the
exchange. A client from before it (0.99.1) goes on presenting Moves wherever it is pointed;
an account moved that way is recovered as today, by a Move back. The

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-71.* Additive: one peering message, one channel route, one refusal on an

## Security considerations

**A learned home is a hint, and everything that acts on it says so.**
Stated above and repeated because it is the load-bearing distinction:
the acts-for gate opens on a Move and on nothing else.

**What locating discloses.** To the home, that somebody at the locating
exchange asked after the account -- what a name resolution disclosed
before this document -- and to the locating exchange, the account's
devices and credentials, which SIP-22 publishes to anybody. The
locating exchange keeps the learned home, which is one more row in a
graph it already holds.

**A proxied prekey is one prekey.** The home serves it once and the
locating exchange keeps nothing; the envelope sealed to it is the
member's and lands in a channel the home pulls. An exchange that took
prekeys it did not need would drain a pool, which SIP-23's rate limits
bound and its recipient notices as a pool that empties.

**`/peer/invited` from a peer that lies.** The home records a hint and
carries a Move to an exchange that will refuse it if the account is not
there. The cost is a request; a peer on the list could make it anyway.

**The forwarded create is the creator's signed act.** The origin checks
it under the creator's key and admits it under the acts-for gate for
that account, which the creator's own Move opened. A forwarding exchange
cannot create in anyone's name but that of an account it has been
signed for.

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-76.* **Authority.** A hint names an origin and nothing else, for the caller's
own account, as SIP-22 attributes the caller. It grants the home no
entitlement it did not have.

**Disclosure.** The home learns that its account may have channels at
`origin`, which the account's own client is telling it. The origin
learns, from the pull, that the home acts for the account, which the
Move the home carries already says (SIP-59).

**Denial of service.** Bounded per account by `MAX_HINTS`; each hint
costs the home one peering request per home-task cycle, which SIP-56's
peering limits already bound at the origin.

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-82.* **A Move is the account's word.** Nothing here changes who may move an
account: the key holder, by signature. It changes when the reference
client uses the key on the person's behalf, which is the only party
the visitor rule binds.

**A stranger exchange learns less.** Under the rule as first written an exchange
learned, from any connection by an unfamiliar account, a signed Move
it could present anywhere as the account's current home until a later
one displaced it. A visitor gives it a connection and nothing signed.

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-71.* **Authority.** A fold rests on one signed statement, the account's own
Move, and one computation, SIP-16's derivation, both checked by the
exchange that acts. The peering link's admission and rate (SIP-35 §Open peering,
`Kind::Peering`) bound how often anyone can ask.

**What a lying home can do.** An exchange that is `first`'s home by
`first`'s own signature can end the stray whenever it likes, whether or
not it holds a conversation to point at. That is no more than a home
could already do: SIP-59 lets it pull, forward and read everything
`first` is in. The stray is not destroyed -- it is kept and served to
both members for the window -- so the worst outcome is a conversation
that has to be reopened.

**Disclosure.** `/peer/folded` tells the stray's exchange that `first`
is homed at the caller, which `/account/home` answers to any identified
caller already, and that the caller holds a channel of that identifier,
which follows from `first` being a member of it. The folded log is
served to the two members and nobody else, as the channel was.

**Denial of service.** A fold record and a folded log are bounded by
the window and by the one-per-identifier rule, and a fold can only be
caused for a channel the exchange orders whose lesser member has a
Move on record naming the caller -- so the most an attacker can fold
is their own direct messages, from their own home.

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-75, via SIP-71.* **Audience.** A folded attachment is fetchable by exactly the two
people who could fetch it while the channel stood, for a bounded time.
It grants nothing to a third party, and is never the attachment that
lets a blob be fetched publicly.

**Storage.** A fold that keeps files costs what the channel cost, for a
week at most, and the channel's quota already bounded it. A stray is
made by a member of the pair, and the attachments were theirs.

**A blob shared across channels.** A blob attached to a stray and to a
group stays fetchable by the group's members on the group's terms and
by the pair on the fold's; the fold's expiry releases only the fold's
hold.

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-81.* **What the home learns.** That some exchange asked for the account's
devices, which §Proxying the key's services discloses already for the empty case and SIP-39
for name resolution. The home already admits the asking exchange's key
or does not; nothing new is admitted.

**What the asking exchange serves.** The home's signed credentials,
unchanged; a client verifies each against the account as SIP-32 says,
so a home that listed a device the account never linked would be
caught by every reader, and this exchange adds nothing a reader would
have to trust.

**A learned home that lies.** §Locating's learned home makes this
exchange proxy and tell, never serve; here it makes it proxy a list. A domain
exchange that named a false home could have this exchange serve that
home's device list for the account -- credentials the reader verifies
against the account key and refuses where they fail, so the false home
can withhold devices and cannot add one. Withholding is denial, which
the domain exchange could do more simply.

**Staleness.** Bounded by the cache's minute where the home answers,
and by the outage where it does not; §Rationale says why the second is
accepted.

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-83, via SIP-81.* **Nothing new is disclosed.** `from` says which of three answers this
exchange gave, all of which it gave before; a caller could already
learn where the account lives from SIP-59's `/account/home`.

**A lying `from`.** An exchange that marked a stale list as the home's
could have served the same list under §Whose list with no mark; the
credentials in it verify against the account key either way, and a
revoked device's credential verifies too -- revocation is the home's
record, not the credential's. The mark is the exchange's word about
its own state, and is trusted exactly as its list was.

Building it found four things that were already wrong, each of which an
independent implementation should check for.

**The home never knew what it ordered.** SIP-59's *a channel the home
already orders is skipped* was implemented against a column the table
does not have; the query failed quietly, the check answered *no* for
every channel, and a home pulled its own channels from any origin that
listed them -- where the replica's `adopt` kept the row and marked it a
copy of the origin. Nothing noticed for twelve documents because
nothing had made an origin list a channel the home ordered until a
direct message did. A copy is now never adopted over a channel the
exchange orders, and a copy held under another incarnation is dropped
and taken afresh, which SIP-32's recreated direct message needed too.

**`replicated` named nobody.** SIP-35 says the refusal names the origin
so a client can go there; the detail was empty. It carries the key now,
which is what a client refused a `Create` under a fold record follows.

**A cached answer outlives the fold.** The client caches a channel's
`info` and its home per connection. Read after a fold, the stale `info`
hid the changed incarnation while the first entries of the conversation
were folded under the stray's keys and verified under the stray's
exchange; refreshed on arrival and re-asked on a change, with the
exchange that ordered the copy that ended kept among the keys the log
may verify under (SIP-53's former origins), the same log reads clean.

**Two resets spent the key.** The cursor rule noticed the restart first,
with a stale incarnation recorded; the fresh one noticed it again on the
next poll, reset again, and deleted the epoch key collected in between
-- and the exchange had served that envelope once. Hence the rule above
that the incarnation recorded is the one served now.
