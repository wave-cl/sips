## Abstract

A member of an exchange asks it to carry a transport connection to another
exchange: the home opens a UDP socket towards the far exchange and copies
packets between that socket and a stream the member holds, reading none of
them. The member then dials the far exchange *through* the copied path with
the same sQUIC handshake it would use directly -- the far exchange's key is
pinned (SIP-9), the member's identity is carried (SIP-3), the far exchange
answers cookies (SIP-7) and refuses strangers (SIP-8) exactly as it would to
a packet from anywhere. What changes is one thing: the far exchange sees the
home's address, never the member's. Nothing in sQUIC changes, nothing at the
far exchange changes, and a home that does not offer this refuses it at the
handshake.

## Motivation

An account lives at the exchange it trusts, and reads and writes there. Some
things are only served elsewhere: a public room found by a federated search
(SIP-16 §What a client does with a search row) is joined at the exchange
that holds it, and a second exchange added to a client is connected to
directly. Each of those connections shows the far operator the address the
client connects from -- and a public exchange with open peering and open
calls is, by construction, run by somebody the client has no reason to
trust with that.

Everything the stack already carries across exchanges is carried at the
application layer: a home fetches prekeys and device lists on a client's
behalf (SIP-60), carries a member's acts to an origin (SIP-43), relays a
call between two homes (SIP-39). Each of those is a message the home
*understands*, forwarded under a rule the home enforces. None of them gives
a client a connection of its own to a far exchange -- one on which it is
the party the far exchange authenticates, holds a SIP-21 session with, and
serves every route to -- without the client dialling the far exchange
itself.

The home is the right place to carry it. It already authenticates the
member (SIP-2, SIP-3), already knows which accounts it is home to (SIP-59)
and which keys it admits (SIP-8, SIP-47), already finds exchanges by name
(SIP-33) and pins what it finds, and already has a public address the
member is content to stand behind. A separate proxy would need every one of
those from scratch.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. Three parties: the **member**, a
client identity connected to its **home** exchange; the **target**, another
exchange the member wants a connection to.

### The tunnel connection

A member that wants a connection carried opens a second sQUIC connection to
its home under the ALPN `sqex-tunnel`, as SIP-39's relay link uses
`sqex-relay` beside the exchange's `h3`. It is an ordinary connection in
every other respect: the home's key is pinned (SIP-9), the member's identity
is carried (SIP-3) and MUST be present, and SIP-8's whitelist applies to it
as to any connection. A home that does not offer carriage does not offer the
ALPN, and the handshake fails as any ALPN mismatch does; a client reports
that this exchange does not carry connections.

One tunnel connection MAY carry several tunnels. Each is one bidirectional
QUIC stream, opened by the member.

### Opening a tunnel

The member writes `Open` as the first bytes of the stream:

```
Open    | type: u8 = 0x01 | key[32] | dom_len: u8 | domain |
```

`key` is the SIP-9 key the member intends to pin at the target; `domain` is
the name the member found it by. The home answers with exactly one `Opened`:

```
Opened  | status: u8 | family: u8 |
```

| `status` | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0 | open -- packets may follow in both directions |
| 1 | refused -- the member may not have a tunnel from this home |
| 2 | no_address -- `domain` did not resolve to an exchange (SIP-33) |
| 3 | wrong_key -- `domain` resolved to an exchange whose key is not `key` |
| 4 | not_an_exchange -- reserved; a home MAY use it where discovery found a name that is not an exchange's |
| 5 | over_limit -- the member holds as many tunnels, or has opened as many, as §Limits allows |

`family` is 4 or 6, the address family the home's socket was bound in
(§Address families), and 0 on any status but 0. On any status but 0 the home
closes the stream after `Opened`.

The home decides `status` in this order, so that a non-member learns nothing
from the answer beyond `refused`:

1. **Membership.** The member's transport key is one the home admits by
   SIP-8's whitelist or SIP-47's registered-device rule, or the identity's
   account has a SIP-59 Move on record naming this home. Otherwise
   `refused`. A home with no whitelist and open admission carries for its
   homed accounts and for nobody else. A home MUST answer `refused` to a
   non-member before resolving `domain`, so that the answer cannot be made
   to depend on the target.
2. **Rate.** §Limits, otherwise `over_limit`.
3. **Resolution.** `domain` by SIP-33, following a SIP-40 handover as the
   home's finder does everywhere else -- the same lookup the home makes to
   peer with an exchange, and with the same result: an address and the key
   the exchange signs under. No address: `no_address`.
4. **The key.** The resolved key MUST equal `key`, else `wrong_key`. The
   home does not carry a connection to a key the name does not vouch for,
   and the member does not pin a key the home supplied: both hold the key
   before the tunnel exists, from different sources, and they must agree.
5. **Not itself.** A `key` equal to the home's own SIP-9 key is `refused`.
   A tunnel to oneself carries nothing a direct connection would not, and a
   chain of them is a loop.

On `open` the home has bound a fresh UDP socket in the target's address
family, connected to the target's address, and holds it for the life of
the stream.

### Carriage

After `Opened` with status 0 the stream carries packets in both directions:

```
Packet  | len: u16 | bytes |
```

Each `Packet` the member writes, the home sends on its socket as one UDP
datagram, `bytes` verbatim. Each datagram the socket receives, the home
writes to the stream as one `Packet`, verbatim. The home MUST NOT parse,
alter, reorder within a direction, or hold back a packet except as §Limits
allows; it MUST NOT send a datagram of its own on the socket. `len` MUST NOT
exceed `MAX_PACKET` (§Limits); a `Packet` larger than that ends the tunnel.

What travels in `bytes` is the member's sQUIC connection to the target,
handshake and all. The member runs its ordinary dialler against a local UDP
socket whose packets it copies into `Packet`s, and copies `Packet`s back out
of, so that the dialler needs no knowledge of the tunnel: it pins `key`,
carries the member's identity, answers the target's SIP-7 cookie with the
target's own `MAC2`, and negotiates whatever ALPN and session it would
negotiate directly. The target sees an sQUIC client at the home's address.

**A tunnel is a stream, not a datagram flow.** Packets arrive at the target
in the order the member sent them and none is lost between member and home.
The inner connection therefore runs over a lossless, delayed link, and its
own loss recovery is idle unless the path beyond the home loses something.
§Rationale records why this is the carriage for this revision and what a
later one may change.

### Closing

Either end closes a tunnel by finishing its side of the stream. The home
closes the socket when the stream is finished or reset by the member, when
the tunnel connection ends, when the tunnel is idle for `IDLE_SECS`
(§Limits) in both directions, or when the member is no longer admitted. A
member whose tunnel closed under a connection it still holds MAY open
another; the target sees a new source port, and the member's inner
connection is gone with the old one -- the tunnel carries a connection, it
does not preserve one.

### What the member MUST NOT do through a tunnel

A member MUST NOT ask the target for a SIP-25 introduction over a tunnelled
connection. The address the target observed (SIP-25 §The address is
observed, never asserted) is the home's socket, which forwards to nobody but
the member's inner connection; an introduction would hand the peer an
address that answers nothing, and would disclose to the peer the home the
member stands behind. A member that wants to be introduced connects
directly, by choice.

### Address families

The home binds its socket in the family of the address it resolved, and
reports it in `Opened.family`. The member's local socket may be of either
family: the inner connection never sees the address on the wire, and a
member on an IPv4-only network reaches an IPv6-only target through a
dual-stack home. This is the one place in the set where a party's family
and the family its packets travel over differ, and it is by design: SIP-25's
rule that the family of a request is the family it arrived over holds at
the target, where what arrived was the home's.

### Limits

| Limit | Reference value |
|---|---|
| Tunnels open per member | 4 |
| `Open` rate | a SIP-56 bucket, `tunnels`, counted per member: burst 4, refilled 10 per hour |
| Bytes per tunnel per second, each direction | 2 MiB, a token bucket; a packet over budget is dropped, as UDP drops |
| `MAX_PACKET` | 1500 |
| `IDLE_SECS` | 60 |
| Tunnel connections | `max_connections` and the idle timeout apply as to any connection |

A home states its own values in configuration and reports the number of
tunnels it currently carries where it reports its other counts.

## Rationale

**Why carry packets and not requests.** A home could serve the target's
routes by proxy, as SIP-60 serves prekeys: understand each request, forward
it, forward the answer. That is the right shape when the home has a rule to
enforce on the way through, and every route it forwards is one it must know.
Here the member wants the *whole* target -- every route it serves today and
every one it will -- with the target authenticating the member and not the
home. Carrying the packets gives that for free and forever, and gives the
home nothing to understand: it copies bytes it cannot read, and the member's
sQUIC session with the target is as end-to-end as a direct one.

**Why the member dials, and not the home.** If the home dialled the target
and handed the member a session, the home would hold the member's identity
key or a key the target took for the member's, and would be a party to
every cookie and every pin. Running the member's own dialler against a local
socket keeps the home out of the handshake entirely and keeps the transport
unchanged: no flag, no option, no new code path in sQUIC, at the home's
outbound end or the target's inbound one. The only thing the design asks of
the transport is what any UDP-carried protocol already tolerates -- a
different source address than the sender's.

**Why a stream and not datagrams.** QUIC DATAGRAM frames would be the
natural carriage for UDP and would keep the inner connection's loss signal
honest. They will not fit: an sQUIC Initial is 1200 bytes of QUIC plus the
sQUIC trailer, and a DATAGRAM frame carrying it must itself fit the outer
connection's packet, which on a path below roughly 1350 bytes it does not.
Fragmenting datagrams across frames is a reassembly protocol, and one worth
having only once the tunnel carries traffic that minds latency. What it
carries in this revision is control traffic: joining a room, posting,
reading -- and calls, the one flow that does mind, already cross between
exchanges by SIP-39 without the member connecting to the far exchange at
all. A reliable stream under a connection that runs its own loss recovery
is the textbook case of two congestion controllers on one path; here the
inner one sees a lossless link and stays at its floor, and the outer one is
the one that reacts. That is acceptable for a control connection and would
not be for media, which is why media is not what this carries. A later
revision MAY add datagram carriage negotiated at `Open`; the stream form
stays as the fallback every path supports.

**Why members only.** A home that carried for anyone would be an open UDP
relay with an sQUIC front door. Every tunnel costs the home a socket and
bandwidth; every tunnelled packet arrives at the target from the home's
address, and a target that throttles an abuser by source (SIP-7, SIP-56)
throttles the home. The operator who admitted the member, or is home to the
account, is the one who can answer for that traffic and revoke the standing
that permits it.

**Why resolution is the home's.** The member could send an address and let
the home connect to it. Then the home would be a relay to any UDP endpoint
a member named, and a member could aim a flood through it at anything. Requiring
a name that resolves by SIP-33 to a key the member already holds bounds the
targets to exchanges that published a signed record and consented, by
publishing, to be connected to; and it lets the home apply the same
handover-following (SIP-40) it applies to its own peers.

**Why refusal comes before resolution.** Answering `no_address` or
`wrong_key` to a non-member would let a stranger use the home as a resolver
and a key oracle for any name. `refused` first, then the rest, is SIP-35
§Peering's uniform-refusal rule at a new door.

**A tunnel through a tunnel** is possible by construction: the member dials
a second home's `sqex-tunnel` through the first, and opens a tunnel there.
Each home sees only the next hop. This document neither specifies nor
forbids it; it notes that a member doing so pays each hop's limits, and that
the second home admits the member on the same grounds as the first.

**What was rejected.** A `CONNECT`-style route on the `h3` connection was
considered and set aside: a tunnel is bytes both ways for as long as the
inner connection lives, which is the SIP-39 stream model, not
request/response, and the separate ALPN keeps every tunnel off the
connection the member's sessions run on. A per-exchange list of
*targets* an operator permits was considered and set aside for this
revision: the member is the party the home answers for, and the target is
already bounded to exchanges that publish; an operator that wants to name
targets can add it as configuration without a wire change.

## Compatibility

Additive. A home from before this document does not offer the ALPN, and a
client that asks for a tunnel there fails at the handshake with the same
error any unsupported ALPN produces; a client MUST treat that as "this home
does not carry connections" and MUST NOT fall back to dialling the target
directly without saying so, since silently connecting directly is the one
outcome the member asked not to have. A target needs no change and cannot
tell a tunnelled connection from a direct one except by its source address.
Nothing on any existing wire changes; `sqex-tunnel` is a new ALPN and every
frame on it is new.

A home enables carriage in configuration, off by default, and prints its
state and limits at startup, so that an operator can read what the exchange
will do for its members without reading a stream.

## Security considerations

**What the home learns.** Which member connects to which exchange, when,
and how much each way. It learns nothing of what is said: the inner
connection is sQUIC between the member and the target, and the home holds
neither party's key. This is exactly what the home learns today when the
member reads a federated channel or is introduced by SIP-25, and less than
what it learns carrying a member's act (SIP-43), which it reads.

**What the target learns.** The member's identity (SIP-3), which the member
chose to carry and the target requires; and the home's address, which
discloses which exchange the member is a member of. A member that wants to
disclose neither is asking for a different identity, not a different
transport; it is out of scope and not made harder by this document.

**Reflection and amplification.** A member can send packets to a target
through the home's address, so the home's address is what the target sees
misbehave. Three things bound it. The target must be an exchange with a
signed SIP-33 record -- not an arbitrary address -- so the set of parties
that can be reached is the set that published. What the home sends is
bounded by what the member sent it, byte for byte, so there is no
amplification through the home, only relocation. And the member is one the
operator admitted or is home to, revocable by the same act that admitted
it; a target that throttles the home by source (SIP-7) is telling the
home's operator which member to revoke, and the home's open/close log line
per tunnel says which.

**Cookie state at the target is shared.** SIP-7 buckets by source address,
so every member tunnelling through one home shares the target's view of
one client. One member's flood makes the target demand cookies of them
all. That is a cost, borne by the members and never by the target, and it
is the intended one: a home that carries for an abuser feels it first.

**The uniform refusal** (§Rationale) keeps a stranger from using the home
as a resolver. A member, admitted, can resolve any name through the home's
finder -- which it could do itself, with the same DNSSEC record, and learns
nothing the record does not say.

**Loops.** A home refuses itself as target. A tunnel through a tunnel
(§Rationale) is bounded by each hop's per-member limits and by the plain
fact that every hop needs an admitted member at the front.

**Idle and lifetime.** A tunnel is a socket the home holds; the idle timer
and the per-member cap keep an abandoned client from holding sockets
forever, and the tunnel connection is subject to `max_connections` and the
idle timeout like any other connection to the exchange.

**What this does not protect against.** Traffic analysis by anyone who can
see both the member's link to the home and the home's link to the target;
a home that logs more than this document asks it to; and a target that
correlates a tunnelled identity with a direct connection the same identity
made earlier from a disclosed address. The last is why a member that wants
its address kept from a target keeps it from the first connection, not the
second.
