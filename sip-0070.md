## Abstract

SIP-5 addresses mail to a key and lets whoever connects as that key
collect it. Since SIP-20 a person has several keys -- an account and the
devices that act for it -- and since SIP-62 the account's key may be one
nobody connects as at all: a seed sealed in a device's store. Mail sent
to such an account waits for nobody. This document has an exchange serve
a device **its account's mailbox with its own**: a device registered to
an account (SIP-22) lists, fetches and deletes what is addressed to the
account as well as what is addressed to itself. Sealing is untouched --
an item sealed to the account opens only where the account's key is,
which SIP-67 lets be more than one device -- and a device that collects
what it cannot open MUST NOT delete it.

## Motivation

The SIP-68 live run sent two messages to a throwaway account at
trunk.exchange, and its device could not list them: the account had
handed over the day before (SIP-62), so its key is a seed in that
device's store and connects as nothing. The messages are still there,
and will expire unread. That gap is older than SIP-62: a phone linked to
an account by a credential (SIP-20) could never see mail sent to the
account, because SIP-5 knows only the key that connected. SIP-38 made
the account the thing a name resolves to, so a sender who looks
somebody up by name addresses their account, and a person reading on
their phone never sees it.

The registry already holds the answer to "whose device is this"
(SIP-22), and every other service consults it -- a device posts as its
account, reads its account's channels, is rung for its account. The
mailbox is the last service that does not.

## Specification

**Account** and **device** are SIP-22's; **item**, **queue**, **List**,
**Fetch**, **Delete**, **Status** and **sealed to** are SIP-5's.

### A device collects for its account

Where the caller's transport identity is registered as a device of an
account (SIP-22), and is not that account itself:

- `List` answers the items waiting for the **account** and the items
  waiting for the **device**, together, oldest first by arrival, each
  with its own id;
- `Fetch` and `Delete` by id act on an item in either queue;
- `Status` is unchanged: a sender asks about what it sent, and the
  caller's account has no bearing.

A caller registered to nobody sees its own queue, as today; an account
that is its own device likewise. Quotas (SIP-5) are per queue and
unchanged: an account's queue is one queue however many devices read
it, and a device's is its own.

Nothing on the wire changes. `Listing`, `Fetched` and the rest are
SIP-5's, and an item does not say which key it was sealed to: a client
that holds several keys opens with each, and SIP-5's AEAD says which
was right.

### What a device MUST NOT do

An item a device fetched and could not open with any key it holds is
addressed to a key another device of the account holds. The device MUST
NOT delete it: deleting completes collection (SIP-5) and would take
from the device that can read it what it never read. A client SHOULD
say that such an item waits for the account's key, and where it holds
no such key, that a device which does can read it (SIP-67).

### SIP-68

A queue the account's home collected from a former home (SIP-68) is the
account's queue at the home, and its devices read it as above.

### What the exchange does not do

It does not fold a device's queue into its account's, or an account's
into a device's: an item stays addressed to the key it was sent to, and
is listed to that key's holder and, where that key is an account, to
its devices. It does not re-seal, cannot, and does not say which key an
item is for; SIP-5 §Sealing binds the sender's choice of key into the
ciphertext, and that is where it stays.

## Rationale

**Why the registry and not a new grant.** A device is its account's by
the account's own signature (SIP-20), recorded at the exchange (SIP-22),
and consulted for everything else the device does in the account's
name. A separate grant for the mailbox would be a second thing to
sign, kept in a second place, for a service that is not more sensitive
than the channels the device already reads under the same binding.

**Why not seal to the devices.** A sender could look the account's
devices up (`/device/list`) and seal one copy to each; that is a
sender's choice today and remains one. Requiring it would make mail to
an account depend on the sender knowing the account's devices at the
moment of sending, and a device linked afterwards would never see it.
The account key is the stable address; SIP-67 is how the person decides
which devices can open what is sealed to it.

**Why no delete of the unopenable.** Collection is one act per item
(SIP-5 delivery is at-least-once, completed by delete) and the exchange
does not know which device can open what. If any device could delete
any item, a phone without the account key would clear mail the laptop
was going to read. The rule costs nothing a correct client would do
anyway -- a client deletes what it has read -- and it is the one rule
that makes shared collection safe.

**Why `Status` is unchanged.** A sender asked the exchange it sent to
about the item it sent; which of the recipient's devices collected it
is the recipient's affair, and the reply has no field for it (SIP-68
says the same of a home's collection).

## Compatibility

Additive. An exchange from before this document lists a device its own
queue only; a client from before it opens with the one key it has and,
fetching an item sealed to the account, fails to open it and -- if it
follows SIP-5 -- does not delete it, which is the rule above. A client
of this document at an old exchange sees no account items and loses
nothing.

## Security considerations

**What a device learns.** The senders, times and sizes of its account's
waiting mail -- the metadata SIP-5 already shows the recipient, shown
to the recipient's devices. A device the account revoked (SIP-22) sees
nothing from the moment of revocation, since the registry no longer
binds it.

**What a device can do.** Delete what it cannot read, if it breaks the
rule above -- a hostile device of one's own account is a hostile
device, and it could already leave the channels it is in, post as the
account, or ring its correspondents; SIP-22's revocation is the remedy.
It cannot open what is sealed to the account without the account's
key, which is the point of sealing and unchanged.

**Nothing is re-sealed.** The exchange holds ciphertext it cannot read
and hands it to more callers than before, all of them the account's by
the account's signature. The set of keys that can open an item is
exactly the set that could before.

## Reference implementation

`sqex` 0.86.0. `sqexd`: `/mailbox/list`, `/mailbox/fetch` and
`/mailbox/delete` consult the registry and serve the caller's account's
queue beside its own (`Mailbox::list_for`, `fetch_for`, `delete_for`).
`sqex-chat mail list|read|delete`: reads through the chat store, which
is where an account key a device made or was entrusted with lives
(SIP-62, SIP-67); `read` opens with the device's key and then the
account's, and says when neither fits; `delete` refuses an item it has
not opened. `sqex mail fetch` (the plain client, one key) says when an
item is sealed to another key rather than reporting it as damaged.
Tests: a linked device lists and reads mail sent to its account beside
mail sent to itself, and a stranger sees neither; an entrusted device
opens what a device without the key can only list, and the latter
refuses to delete it.

Run live on 2026-09-18 at trunk.exchange with the throwaway account of
the SIP-67 run, whose key one device holds: mail sent to the account and
mail sent to that device were listed together by it and read with the
right key each; the account's other device listed the account's item,
was refused the read and the delete, and the plain one-key client said
the item was sealed to another key; the holder collected both and the
sender's `Status` said collected. One finding: the client refused the
holder's delete too, because "opened" was kept in the process and a
command-line client is one command per process -- delete opens the item
first now (0.86.1). And the exchange's mailbox is in memory, so the
items of the SIP-68 run did not survive the restart that deployed this;
that is SIP-5's choice and older than this document.
