## Abstract

[SIP-25](sip-0025.md) introduces two identities by telling each the address
the exchange observed the other arrive from. It pairs on identity alone, so
two parties that reached the exchange over different address families are
each handed an address they have no route to. This document makes the family
part of the match: a request is offered on the family it arrived over, a pair
is introduced only on a family both offered, a party reachable on more than
one offers each by asking over it, and a pair that shares none is told so
rather than being given an address that cannot be dialled.

## Motivation

A live run on 2026-09-18, phone to desktop. The phone was on a mobile
network with an IPv4 carrier-NAT address and no IPv6 at all; the desktop
reached the same exchange over IPv6. Both asked for an introduction, within
a second of each other, and the exchange paired them and answered both. The
phone was handed the desktop's IPv6 address and its transport refused it out
of hand: it had no IPv6 route and never sent a packet. The desktop was
handed the phone's IPv4 address, waited, and reported that the punch had
failed.

Three things were wrong, and only the first is about a call not connecting.

- **The attempt was never possible.** No packet either side sent could have
  reached the other. The eight seconds spent were spent on nothing.
- **The diagnosis was wrong, and stated confidently.** The listening side
  reported that this needs endpoint-independent mapping and that symmetric
  NAT defeats it -- SIP-25's language, accurate in general and not what
  happened here. An operator reading that would look at the NAT.
- **Nothing could tell the difference.** A peer who never asked and a peer
  who asked from another family look identical to the caller: silence, then
  a relay. The failure that can be fixed and the failure that cannot are
  indistinguishable.

The relay carried the call, as SIP-25 intends. This is not about a call being
lost; it is about an introduction that could not work being attempted anyway,
and about the exchange knowing that and not saying it.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. The wire is SIP-25's, at SIP-25's
lengths.

### The family of a request is the family it arrived over

A request offers exactly one family: the family of the address the exchange
observed it arrive from. It is **observed, never asserted**, which is
SIP-25's rule for the address itself and holds here for the same reason -- a
party that could name a family it is not on could aim a coordinated pair of
connection attempts at an address it does not hold.

A pending request is held under the asker, the peer, and that family, where
SIP-25 held it under the asker and the peer alone.

### A pair is introduced on a family both offered

Both parties are introduced when each has a live request for the other **on
one family**, and are told that family's addresses. Everything else in
SIP-25 is unchanged: the same start time computed once and read back by the
other, the same long poll, the same silence for a peer who has not asked.

Where more than one family matches, the exchange SHOULD choose IPv6. A pair
that both reached the exchange over IPv6 is more often globally addressed
and not translated at all, and an introduction between two such parties has
the best chance of any of them.

### Offering more than one family

A party reachable on several families offers each by **asking over each**:
one request per family, from a connection of that family. There is no field
to set and nothing to declare.

This is deliberate. A declared capability would be an assertion, which
SIP-25 excludes for addresses, and would also be a claim a host is in a poor
position to make: an address is not a route, and a host may hold an IPv6
address that reaches nothing. Asking over a family proves the exchange can
be reached over it, which is the closest available evidence that a peer
might be too.

### Both asked, and share no family

When both parties have a live request for each other and no family is common
to them, each is answered with `ready = 2`, a third value beside SIP-25's
waiting and ready, with `kind = 0` and the address bytes zeroed. The reply's
length is unchanged, so it is byte-for-byte the shape of the other two.

This answer is served **only when both have asked**. That is what makes it
safe to send: it says the peer asked, which SIP-25 withholds from a party
who has not consented, to a party who has consented by asking. Nothing is
disclosed to anyone SIP-25 would not already have answered.

A client receiving it MUST fall back to [SIP-12](sip-0012.md) and SHOULD say
what happened in the terms it was told -- that the two are on networks with
no family in common -- and not in the terms of NAT traversal.

### Asking for the third answer

`Introduce` carries a type byte. A caller that understands `ready = 2` sends
type `0x02`; the fields and the length are otherwise SIP-25's. A caller
sending SIP-25's `0x01` is never answered `2`: where the families do not
meet, it is answered with the same waiting it would have received had the
peer not asked at all.

An exchange that does not implement this document refuses `0x02` as
malformed, which is how a caller discovers it and falls back to `0x01`.

## Rationale

**Why the exchange decides and not the client.** The client cannot. It
learns the peer's address only after the wait it is trying to avoid
spending, and a client told an address it cannot use still does not know
whether the peer is unreachable or simply never asked.

**Why not make the exchange translate.** It could hand each party an address
of its own family by relaying the punch, but that is the relay, and SIP-12
already is it. An introduction whose traffic goes through the exchange is
not an introduction.

**Why this does not promise more calls will connect.** It will not, for the
pair in the motivation: a carrier-NAT address behaves as symmetric often
enough that SIP-25 says to assume it does, so even matched on IPv4 that
punch would likely fail. What changes is that a pair that cannot meet learns
so at once instead of after the wait, and learns the true reason. Removing a
wasted attempt and a wrong answer is the whole of the claim.

**Why one family per request rather than a set.** A request carries the
address it came from and one family is what that is. A set would need the
exchange to hold addresses it observed at other times on other connections,
and to answer with one it did not see on the connection asking -- which is
the assertion this document is avoiding.

## Compatibility

**A SIP-25 client against a SIP-69 exchange** is strictly better off. It
sends `0x01`, is matched on family, and where there is no match receives the
waiting it understands. It is no longer handed an address it cannot dial.

**A SIP-69 client against a SIP-25 exchange** has `0x02` refused as
malformed, falls back to `0x01`, and is on the old behaviour.

**Whatever the exchange said**, a client MUST check that a disclosed address
is one it can dial before dialling it, and relay with a plain reason if it
is not. An exchange is not the authority on what a client's network can
reach, and this is the check that would have produced a true diagnosis on
the day.

## Security considerations

**No new disclosure.** `ready = 2` is served only to a party that has itself
asked, which is the gate SIP-25 already sets, and it carries no address.

**Probing is not made easier.** Learning whether an identity asked still
requires asking, and an asker is disclosing itself to do so. A party that
wants to know which families another is on learns nothing it could not learn
by being introduced.

**The family is not a new secret.** It is implied by the address SIP-25 would
have disclosed to the same party under the same consent.

**Denial of service is unchanged.** The pending table gains a family in its
key, so an identity may hold one pending request per peer per family rather
than per peer. Two is the practical bound, and an exchange SHOULD apply its
existing per-identity limits across families rather than per family.

## Reference implementation

`sqex` 0.88.0, corrected in 0.88.1 and 0.88.2. Both corrections are recorded
here because each is a trap an independent implementation would fall into.

`sqex-proto::rendezvous`: `TYPE_INTRODUCE_FAMILIES = 0x02` and
`Introduce::family_aware`; `Answer::NoSharedFamily = 2` beside waiting and
ready, with `Introduced::no_shared_family` zeroing the address so all three
answers encode to one length. `sqexd::rendezvous`: `Family::{V4, V6}`, and a
pending table keyed on `(asker, peer, family)` where SIP-25 keyed on the pair
alone; `request` answers `no_shared_family` only when the peer holds a live
request on another family, and `waiting` when it holds none, which is what
keeps the two apart; `wait` returns a settled answer without polling, because
a pair that shares no family is not pending. `sqexd::server` downgrades that
answer to `waiting` for a caller that sent `0x01`. `sqex-proto::direct`:
`Meeting::NoSharedFamily`, and the caller's own check that a disclosed
address is one it can dial.

**Canonicalise an observed address before anything else touches it.** An
exchange listening on `[::]` is dual-stack and observes an IPv4 peer as
`::ffff:a.b.c.d`, which a standard library's "is this IPv6" will agree is
IPv6. Keyed, compared and disclosed in that form, two parties on IPv4 land in
different families and each is handed an address its socket cannot send to --
so this document's answer is served to pairs it was never written for, and
reads to an operator as a fault in the network. That defeated the
implementation entirely until 0.88.2, on a pair where one end held no IPv6
address and no IPv6 route at all. The distinction the document does draw has
to survive the fix: a mapped IPv4 party and a real IPv6 party still share no
family.

**Say which side reached the conclusion.** An exchange refusing a pair and a
caller refusing an address are different findings, and while both produced
one sentence no log could show the exchange's half had run at all: two
devices' clocks are not close enough to order one answer against the other
by time. 0.88.2 separates the wording, and only the exchange's mentions that
both asked -- the one thing a caller cannot know.

A third correction belongs to SIP-25 and was surfaced by this document.
Binding the reused port in the exchange's family moved the listen onto IPv6,
where the just-dropped exchange connection had not yet released the socket.
The party that completes the pair computes the start time; the other reads it
back and so has no lead left to spend, and that party is the listener.
0.88.1 waits for the port rather than assuming a fixed pause.

Tests: a pair asking on one family is introduced and told that family's
addresses; a pair asking across families gets `2`, and every answer encodes
to one length; a peer who has not asked is still silence; a party asking over
both meets on the one they share; a SIP-25 caller gets waiting where a SIP-69
caller gets `2`; two mapped IPv4 parties are one family and are handed
dialable addresses, while a mapped IPv4 party and a real IPv6 one are not.
Each mapped-address test was checked by removing the canonicalisation and
watching it fail.

Deployed on two exchanges on 2026-09-18, both listening `[::]:443`. The first
direct call between a phone on a carrier-NAT IPv4 address and a desktop
followed: two public IPv4 addresses behind NAT, punched, Opus both ways,
packet counts mirroring exactly, no loss.
