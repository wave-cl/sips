## Abstract

Two people compare a short code, derived from both of their keys, by a
channel the exchange is not in -- in person, over a call -- and each marks the
other *verified* on their own machine. The code is six words from a fixed list,
and a QR that carries the same thing for a camera. Verification is a fact the
person established, kept where they established it; nothing about it comes
from the exchange, and a client that wants to say it out loud lodges a SIP-27
claim that others may read but MUST NOT act on.

## Motivation

A key proves it is itself. Everything that stands in for one is an assertion
somebody else makes: a profile name is the subject's word (SIP-21), a name at
a domain is the exchange's (SIP-38), and an attestation is the issuer's
(SIP-27). A person writing to `alice@squic.org` is trusting the exchange that
the key it resolves to is Alice's, every time, and has no way to have checked
once and be done.

The check that needs no third party is the oldest one: the two people look at
the same thing at the same time and agree it matches. Forty-four characters of
base58 read aloud is that check made unbearable, which is why nobody does it.
Six words is the same check made ordinary.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. `A` and `B` are two account keys, Ed25519
public keys of 32 bytes (SIP-3).

### The safety number

```
SAFETY_CONTEXT = "sip41-v1"   (ASCII, 8 bytes, no terminator)

number = SHA-256( SAFETY_CONTEXT || min(A, B) || max(A, B) )
```

`min` and `max` are by the bytes of the keys, so the number is the same
whichever side computes it. It is a property of the pair, not of either key:
the words two people read to each other are theirs, and a third person has
different words with each of them.

### The words

The first 66 bits of `number`, as six 11-bit indices into the wordlist in the
appendix, most significant first:

```
word[i] = LIST[ (number >> (256 - 11 * (i + 1))) & 0x7FF ]      i = 0..5
```

The list is the 2048 English words of BIP-39, in its order, chosen because it
was made to be read aloud -- no two words share their first four letters, and
none is a common misspelling of another -- and because it is already in more
pockets than anything this ecosystem could publish. The list is fixed by this
document; a client MUST ship it verbatim, and the appendix gives its digest.

Sixty-six bits is enough. An attacker who wants their key to produce Alice's
words with Bob has to find a key that collides on 66 bits, which is 2^66 work
per target pair and yields a key they do not have the private half of unless
they generated it -- and generating 2^66 keys is the same 2^66. The threat is
not that the words are guessable; it is that somebody skips comparing them.

### The code

For a camera, the same thing as text:

```
sip41:<base58 A>:<base58 B>:<hex number>
```

with `A` the lower key. A client showing the words SHOULD show this as a QR
beside them. A client scanning one MUST recompute `number` from the two keys
and compare it to the hex, and MUST check that one of the keys is its own
account and the other is the account it is verifying; a code that names other
keys is somebody else's conversation.

### Verifying

A person compares the words with the other person -- reads them, hears them,
scans the code -- and, if they match, marks the other person's **key**
verified. The mark is:

- **Local.** Kept on the machine that made it, by the account key it was made
  for, with when. It is not sent anywhere by being made.
- **On the key, not the name.** An account is its key, and a direct message is
  derived from two keys (SIP-16), so nothing can change the key under a
  conversation. What can change is what a *name* resolves to. A client that
  holds a verified key for a name (SIP-38) and later resolves that name to a
  different key MUST say so before letting the person write to it, showing
  both keys in full, and MUST NOT carry the mark to the new key.
- **Undoable.** A person may withdraw their own mark at any time; nothing else
  may.

A client MUST NOT mark a key verified on any evidence but the person's own
comparison. In particular, a name resolving, a profile matching, another
person's attestation (below), or the exchange's word are none of them grounds.

### Saying so

A client MAY, at the person's choice and never by default, lodge a SIP-27
attestation with the exchange:

| `claim` | Meaning | `body` |
|---|---|---|
| 0x05 | the issuer compared the subject's safety number with them | empty |

The body is empty because the number is recomputable by anybody holding both
keys, and carrying it would say nothing the keys do not. The claim is
registered here and in SIP-27's table.

A reader MAY show that people it has itself verified have verified a key --
"verified by two people you know" -- as information about the key. A reader
MUST NOT treat any attestation, from anyone, as a verification: the mark is
the person's own comparison or nothing.

## Rationale

**Why words and not a number.** Signal's safety number is sixty decimal
digits; comparing two of them aloud takes a minute of "three seven, no, *two*
seven". Words from a list built for the purpose are read at speaking speed and
mishear far less, and six of them are fewer syllables than twenty digits.

**Why the pair and not each key alone.** A fingerprint per key would let a
person verify Alice once and have it hold for every conversation, which sounds
better and is not: it makes the fingerprint something to publish, and a
published fingerprint is one more thing a name can be made to resemble. A
pairwise number has nothing to publish. The cost is that Alice and Bob's words
are useless to Carol, which is correct: Carol's verification of Alice is
Carol's to do.

**Why local, and why the exchange is not in it.** The exchange is the party a
verification exists to be independent of. It resolved the name; it served the
profile; it will serve the next one. A mark that lived at the exchange would be
the exchange's word about the exchange's word.

**Why the attestation is optional and inert.** SIP-24 and SIP-27 both hold
that a credential is evidence, not authority. Publishing "we met" is useful to
people deciding whom to compare words with, and it is a disclosure -- that the
two are acquainted -- so the person makes it or does not. Letting it stand in
for the comparison would rebuild the web-of-trust failure mode: everybody
trusts somebody who trusted somebody, and nobody compared anything.

**Why BIP-39's list.** Writing a new list is easy and testing one is not: the
properties that matter -- audibility, distinctness under a bad line, no
offensive words, four-letter unique prefixes -- were established for that list
by use, at scale, by people reading it aloud to each other under worse
conditions than a phone call. Borrowing it costs nothing and buys that.

**Why not the whole digest.** Twenty-four words would be a recovery phrase,
not a comparison. Six is what two people will actually read.

## Compatibility

Nothing on the wire changes but a claim type, and SIP-27 already ignores
unknown ones: a client from before this document shows the 0x05 claim as
present and unreadable, which is what SIP-27 requires of it.

## Security considerations

**The comparison is the mechanism.** A client that shows the words and lets
the person press *verified* without leaving the screen has done nothing wrong
and nothing useful; the security is in the two people reading, and the client
can only make that easy. It MUST NOT make it easier to skip than to do.

**A man in the middle** holding the exchange sees two keys it does not own and
one conversation it cannot read. To make Alice's words with Bob come out right
for a key it holds, it would need that key to collide with Alice's on the
number, which is 2^66 per pair. To make the two of them read the *wrong* words
and think they match, it would need to control what one of their screens
shows, which is a compromised client and out of scope.

**The attestation discloses acquaintance.** Lodging the 0x05 claim tells the
exchange, and anyone who reads it, that the two people met or spoke. That is
why it is opt-in per verification and never the default.

**A verified mark is a claim about a key.** A device stolen with its client
unlocked shows every mark its owner made; it does not let the thief make marks
that reach anyone else. A client that syncs marks between a person's own
devices (SIP-42, later) carries the person's own marks and nobody else's.

**The wordlist is part of the protocol.** Two clients with different lists
show two people different words for the same number, and the comparison fails
safe: they do not match, and nobody marks anything. A client MUST NOT localise
the list.

## Reference implementation

Not yet. Planned: `sqex-proto::safety` (the number, the words, the code, the
list) and SIP-27 claim `0x05` in `sqex-proto::attest`; `sqex-chat`'s store
keeps the marks; `sqex verify <key>` prints the words; sigil shows them with
the QR and carries the mark on the conversation, the bar, and the member.

## Appendix: the wordlist

The 2048 words of BIP-39's English list, in order, one per line, joined by
`\n` with no trailing newline:

```
SHA-256 = 2f5eed53a4727b4bf8880d8f3f199efc90e58503646d9ff8eff3a2ed3b24dbda
```

The list begins `abandon ability able about above absent absorb abstract
absurd abuse` and ends `young youth zebra zero zone zoo`. An implementation
ships it as a file whose digest is the one above, and tests that it is.
