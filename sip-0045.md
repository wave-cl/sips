## Abstract

A phone cannot hold SIP-30's stream open: its operating system puts the
application to sleep and closes what it held. This SIP lets a device leave
an **endpoint** with the exchange -- an HTTPS address of the device's own
choosing, served by a push distributor of the device's own choosing -- and
has the exchange send a **content-free wake** to it when an event would
have gone on a stream the device is not holding. The device wakes, connects,
and fetches as it would have. The exchange learns nothing it did not already
know; the distributor learns only *when*.

## Motivation

SIP-30 was written for a client that is running, and says so: one held-open
response per client, an event written the moment it happens, the client
fetches. That client is a desktop. A handset's operating system ends a
background connection within seconds of the screen going dark, and lets an
application run again only when the platform's own push service says so.
The README has carried this as the named open problem since the stream was
built.

The obvious answer -- send the message through the platform's push service
-- is the one this stack exists to refuse: it would put the exchange's
plaintext, or a member's sealed body and the metadata around it, into a
service that reads everything it carries. What a push service is *for*,
and all it needs to be for, is to say "something happened, wake up". That
is one bit, and the device already knows how to do the rest.

The remaining question is who talks to the platform's service. Not the
exchange: an exchange with Apple's and Google's credentials is an exchange
that knows which of its accounts run on which phones, and that has agreed
to those services' terms on its members' behalf. The device chooses its
own distributor and hands the exchange an address; the exchange posts to
the address. This is the arrangement UnifiedPush standardised, and this
document adopts its shape rather than inventing one: an application server
that knows only an endpoint, a distributor the person chose, and a payload
the distributor cannot make sense of because there is nothing in it.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. Every request is bound to the
transport identity (SIP-3), which is the **device** (SIP-22): a wake is a
device's, not an account's, since an account's other devices may be wide
awake.

### Registering

```
Register | type: u8 = 0x01 | ttl: u32 | endpoint_len: u16 | endpoint |
Forget   | type: u8 = 0x02 |
Ack      | now: u64 |
```

`POST /wake/register` keeps `endpoint` for the calling device for `ttl`
seconds (at most 30 days; a device re-registers when it connects, which is
often enough). `endpoint` is an absolute `https://` URL of at most 512
bytes, UTF-8; an exchange MAY additionally accept `http://` to loopback for
its own tests and MUST accept nothing else. One endpoint per device; a
second registration replaces the first. `POST /wake/forget` drops it.

An exchange MUST NOT make anything of the endpoint but a place to post to.
It is not shown to other members, not replicated (SIP-35), and not served
back on any route.

### Waking

Whenever the exchange would publish a SIP-30 event to an account, for each
device of that account (SIP-22's list, and the account itself where it has
none) that holds a registered endpoint and **no open stream of its own**, it
sends a wake: `POST endpoint` with body `wake` (4 bytes, ASCII) and no
other content. The body names nothing -- not the account, the device, the
channel, the sender, nor the kind of event -- and MUST NOT: the distributor
is a third party the exchange did not choose, and the wake is the whole of
what it may learn.

A device that holds a stream is not woken: it will be told on the stream.

Not every event is worth a wake. A signal (SIP-36's typing and call states)
or a cursor move is momentary, and a device woken for one arrives to find
nothing; a heartbeat or a resync is about the stream itself, which an
absent device does not hold. An exchange MUST NOT wake for these. A ring is
raised as its own event beside the signal that carried it, and is woken for.

**Coalescing.** An exchange MUST NOT send more than one wake per device per
`WAKE_MIN_SECS` (30) while the device stays away, whatever arrives in
between: the first wake is all the device needs, and each further one is a
timestamp handed to the distributor for nothing. A ring (SIP-36's
`Ringing`, SIP-39's `CrossCall`) is the exception and MAY be sent at once
regardless: a call is answered in seconds or not at all.

**Failure.** A response of `404` or `410` from the endpoint means the
distributor no longer knows it, and the exchange MUST forget the
registration. Any other failure is dropped; the next event tries again
after the coalescing interval. An exchange SHOULD bound the time it spends
on a wake (a few seconds) and MUST NOT let a slow endpoint delay the stream
it is also writing to.

### What the device does

Woken, it connects, subscribes (SIP-30) and reconciles as SIP-30 says a
fresh subscription must: the wake carried nothing, and everything it needs
is where it always was. A device that wants to show a notification before
the person opens the application fetches what is new and composes the
notification itself, from the plaintext it alone can open; nothing in this
document says what a notification contains, because nothing in it can.

### Limits

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| Endpoint length | 512 bytes |
| `ttl` | 30 days |
| `WAKE_MIN_SECS` | 30 |
| Wake body | `wake` |
| Time allowed per wake | 5 s |

## Rationale

**Why the device chooses the distributor.** Because the alternative is the
exchange choosing it, and then holding a relationship with Apple and
Google on every member's behalf. A device that runs a UnifiedPush
distributor of its own -- or a self-hosted one, or a friend's -- hands the
exchange an address and nothing about who is behind it. The exchange's
code is the same for every distributor there is, which is what keeps it
from having opinions about any.

**Why the body is a constant.** Any variation in it is information, and
the party receiving it is one the exchange did not vet. A single fixed word
carries exactly the one bit the mechanism exists to carry. A distributor
that wanted more would have to be told more, and there is nothing to tell
it.

**Why per device and not per account.** A person's desktop holding a
stream says nothing about their phone; a phone that has a stream open is
awake and needs no push. Only the device knows whether it is listening,
and the exchange knows that per connection, which is per device.

**Why coalesce.** A busy channel would otherwise turn into a wake per
message, each one a timestamp the distributor keeps; and a phone woken
thirty times a minute is a phone with a flat battery. One wake, then
silence until the device has come and gone, is what the device wants and
the least the distributor can be given.

**Why the ring bypasses it.** Coalescing trades a little latency for a lot
of quiet, and a ring is the one event where thirty seconds is the whole
call. One extra wake for a ring is the cost, and it is paid only when
somebody is calling.

## Compatibility

Additive: two routes. An exchange from before this document refuses them
with SIP-21's `not_found`, and a client reads that as "no wake here" and
keeps its stream open as long as it can, which is what it did. Nothing on
the stream changes.

## Security considerations

**What the exchange learns.** The endpoint: which distributor a device
uses, and an opaque token within it. It already knew the device, the
account and every event; the endpoint is the one new fact, and a device
that would rather not disclose it registers nothing and is not woken.

**What the distributor learns.** *When* a device had something to be told,
to within the coalescing interval, and how often. Not what, not from
whom, not which channel, not even that it is this stack: the request is a
fixed four bytes from an address. Timing is metadata, and a person who
considers their distributor untrustworthy has chosen the wrong distributor;
the design bounds what a wrong choice costs to timing, and coalescing
bounds the timing.

**A wake is not authenticated, and need not be.** Anybody who learns an
endpoint can post to it and wake the device for nothing; the device then
connects and finds nothing new. That is a nuisance the distributor's own
rate limits address, and an endpoint is a secret the device chose and the
exchange keeps; nothing here publishes it.

**The exchange as a source of requests.** An exchange posts to addresses
its members supplied. It MUST NOT follow redirects, MUST bound each request
in time, and SHOULD refuse endpoints that resolve to itself or to private
address space except where it is configured to accept loopback for tests
-- or it is a way to make an exchange knock on doors on its members'
behalf.

## Reference implementation

Planned: `sqex-proto::wake`; `sqexd` keeps endpoints beside its device
registry, counts open streams per device, and posts wakes from a task the
stream never waits on; `sqex wake register <url>` / `sqex wake forget`. No
phone client exists yet; this is the half of the problem the exchange can
solve ahead of one, and a desktop test client stands in for the phone.
