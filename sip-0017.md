## Abstract

A private channel is sealed under a symmetric key that every member holds and
the exchange does not. The key belongs to an **epoch**; an admin mints a new
epoch to rotate, which is what makes removing a member mean something. Each
member seals with a subkey derived from the channel key and its own identity, so
several senders can share one key without sharing a nonce space. The key reaches
a member in an envelope sealed to their identity with SIP-5's construction,
which works while they are offline. The inviter chooses how many past epochs to
include, and that choice is the answer to "can the person I just added read what
we said last week".

## Motivation

SIP-16 gives a channel a membership the exchange enforces and a body it never
inspects. It does not say what seals the body, and for a private channel that is
the whole question.

The two constructions already in the stack both fail here, in instructive ways.

SIP-12's session key is derived from three Diffie-Hellman terms between exactly
two identities, two of which need a *static* private key — which is precisely
why the exchange cannot complete it. There is no three-party form without new
cryptography, and SIP-13 says so. A channel of 256 members would need 32 640
pairwise sessions and every message encrypted 255 times; and worse, it would
have no answer at all for history, because a member who joins on Tuesday has no
session that existed on Monday and the exchange would have to store one copy per
recipient of every message ever sent.

SIP-5's seal does work to an offline recipient — that is its whole point — but
it seals to exactly one recipient with a fresh ephemeral, so it too is one
ciphertext per member per message.

What a stored, shared, re-readable log needs is the thing SIP-13 explicitly
declined to build at its size: **one ciphertext, one key, many readers**. SIP-13
rejected it because at eight people the saving is in a client's uplink only and
the cost is a distribution and rotation policy. That arithmetic inverts once
there is history: the exchange stores one copy instead of N, a member who joins
later can be handed a key rather than replayed a conversation, and the rotation
policy — the cost SIP-13 declined to pay — is not overhead but the mechanism
that makes removal work.

So this SIP is the group key SIP-13 said would need its own proposal. This is
that proposal.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. Every operation is bound to the caller's
transport identity (SIP-3). A connection carrying no identity has no membership
and MUST be refused.

`KEY_CONTEXT` is the ASCII string `sqex-channel-key-v1` and `ENVELOPE_CONTEXT`
is the ASCII string `sqex-channel-envelope-v1`.

### Epochs

An epoch is a `u32` and a **channel key**: 32 bytes, generated uniformly at
random. Epoch 0 is reserved and means the body is not sealed at all, which is
the case for every entry in a public channel and MUST NOT appear in a private
one.

A private channel is therefore created with **no epoch**, reported as 0, and
nothing can be posted to it until its creator publishes the envelopes for epoch
1. Epochs count up by one from there. The gap between creating a channel and
keying it is real but not observable: SIP-16 refuses a post under an epoch that
is not current, so there is no window in which an entry could be stored
unsealed.

An admin mints a new epoch by generating a key and publishing an envelope of it
to every current member. A member that has not received an envelope for the
current epoch cannot read new entries and MUST NOT post; posting under a
superseded epoch is refused by the exchange (SIP-16), which is what keeps a
channel from silently splitting into two conversations neither of which can read
the other.

An admin **MUST** rotate after removing a member. Nothing at the exchange can
enforce that — it holds no key and cannot tell whether one changed — so it is a
requirement on clients, and a client that removes a member without rotating has
produced an interface that says "removed" and means "cannot post".

An admin **MAY** rotate at any other time. Rotating on a schedule bounds how much
of a channel any one stolen key opens; rotating on every join is a defensible
policy for a channel that wants no history sharing at all.

### Sealing an entry

A channel key is shared by every device in the channel. A shared key with a
per-sender counter as its nonce is a nonce-reuse machine: two senders that both
start at zero collide on their first message, and ChaCha20-Poly1305 fails
catastrophically when they do — it leaks the XOR of the two plaintexts and its
authentication guarantee is gone. SIP-12 avoids this between two peers with a
direction byte in the nonce. The general case needs the separation to be in the
key:

```
sender_key = SHA-512(KEY_CONTEXT || channel_id || epoch || channel_key
                     || device)[0..32]
nonce      = msg_seq(8) || 0(4)
body       = ChaCha20-Poly1305(sender_key, nonce, plaintext)
```

`epoch` is its four bytes big-endian. **`device` is the sending client's Ed25519
identity — not its account** (SIP-20). That distinction is the whole of this
paragraph's correctness: a person may run several clients, and if the subkey
were derived per account they would share one and both count from zero, which is
the collision this construction exists to prevent. A device is the unit of
separation because a device is the thing that holds a counter.

`msg_seq` is that device's own counter within the epoch, starting at 0, and
travels in the clear in the SIP-16 entry header alongside the device it belongs
to.

Every member can derive every other device's subkey from the entry header, so
there is no distribution cost beyond the channel key itself and no lookup. **The
price is that this construction authenticates a sender not at all**: a valid
ciphertext under Bob's subkey proves only that some member made it, since any of
them can derive that subkey and seal with it. Sender authentication is SIP-31's,
and it is worth knowing which document to look in. Two
devices at `msg_seq` 5 do not collide, because they are not using the same key.

A receiver **MUST** reject an entry whose `(device, epoch, msg_seq)` it has
already seen. A repeat is a nonce reuse, and the check costs nothing because the
counter is in the header the exchange already stores. A sender **MUST NOT**
reset its counter within an epoch.

**A client that has lost its counter recovers it from the exchange rather than
guessing.** The exchange records, for each `(device, epoch)`, the highest
`msg_seq` it has ever accepted, and reports it to that device as `my_msg_seq` in
SIP-16's `Info`; a device resumes at that value plus one. This is
exact rather than approximate, and it needs no authority the device does not
already have — which matters, because the alternative of minting a fresh epoch
is an admin's act and would leave an ordinary member of a group unable to
recover from a reinstall at all.

The exchange **MUST** keep that high-water mark independently of the entries
themselves. Pruning removes entries, so the highest *surviving* `msg_seq` may
understate what was used, and resuming from it would reuse a nonce. The mark is
a few bytes per device per epoch and outlives the entries it summarises.
Redaction needs no special handling: it keeps the entry header, and the header is
where the counter lives.

**Two clients MUST NOT share a device identity.** Not two processes on one
machine, not a client and a copy of its key on a second machine, not a restored
backup running beside the original. The consequence is not a degraded mode or a
dropped message: it is nonce reuse under a shared key, which surrenders the
confidentiality of both plaintexts and the integrity of everything sealed under
that subkey. A second client is a second device with its own credential, which
costs one signature. SIP-12 states the same rule for its own sessions, where the
penalty is merely a call that cannot be heard; here it is worse, and a rule
without its consequence gets relaxed by somebody who mistakes it for fussiness.

The single hash truncated to 32 bytes, rather than HKDF, follows SIP-12 and
SIP-5, which both derive this way. Consistency across the four constructions is
worth more here than the marginal argument for an extract-and-expand step over
inputs that are already uniformly random.

### Envelopes

The channel key reaches each **device** sealed to that device's identity, using
SIP-5's construction with its own context. Sealing per device rather than per
account is what lets a person add a client without every other member learning a
new account key, and it means a revoked device is simply one that receives no
further envelopes:

```
DH1 = X25519(ephemeral_secret, recipient_static)
DH2 = X25519(ephemeral_secret, recipient_prekey)
okm = SHA-512(ENVELOPE_CONTEXT || ephemeral_pub || recipient_static
              || prekey_public || DH1 || DH2)
key = okm[0..32]      nonce = okm[32..44]
```

`recipient_static` is the forward derivation of the recipient **device's**
Ed25519 identity. `recipient_prekey` is a single-use key that device published
in advance and the exchange served once, per **SIP-23**; a sender obtains it
with `POST /prekey/take` before sealing.

A fresh ephemeral keypair per envelope is **MUST**; the long-lived identity key
**MUST NOT** be used as the ephemeral. The nonce is derived rather than
transmitted, because a fresh ephemeral makes each envelope key unique and there
is nothing to disambiguate. Every term **MUST** be rejected if non-contributory
(small-order).

The two terms do different jobs and both are required. DH1 binds the envelope to
the recipient's identity — without that key it does not open, which is the
property this construction had before SIP-23 and keeps. DH2 is the forward
secrecy: the prekey secret is destroyed on use, so nobody can recompute the term
afterwards, and an attacker who copied envelopes and later obtained the identity
key gets nothing from them.

```
Envelope | channel[32] | recipient[32] | from_epoch: u32 | to_epoch: u32
         | prekey_id: u32 | ephemeral[32] | ciphertext |

plaintext = key[32] × (to_epoch - from_epoch + 1), ascending by epoch
```

Reusing SIP-5's shape rather than SIP-12's is deliberate and is the reason an
invitation works at all. SIP-12 requires both identities to have named each
other before either can derive anything, so a session cannot be established with
somebody who is not there. An envelope is sealed to a public key the inviter
already holds, so the recipient can be offline, can be offline for a week, and
collects it whenever they next connect.

That an account may have several devices does not multiply the per-message cost
at all: an entry is sealed once under the channel key whatever the membership
looks like. It multiplies only the envelopes, which are published on a rotation
or an invitation and not otherwise.

**A `Put` MUST cover every registered device of every member it is publishing
for, that has published prekeys.** The recipients are devices and the membership
is accounts, so a publisher enumerates them: for each member account,
`POST /device/list` (SIP-22), then one envelope per device returned.

The qualification is not a loophole; without it the rule is impossible to obey.
SIP-23 forbids sealing to a device that has published no prekeys, and SIP-22
lets an existing device register a new one **before it has ever connected**, so
"registered, with no prekeys yet" is an ordinary state and not an error. A
publisher **skips** such a device.

Note that SIP-22 offers two branches and the reference client takes the other
one: the new client registers *itself*, with the credential it was given, and
publishes prekeys in the same breath. That makes the gap a few milliseconds
inside one process rather than the open-ended state described above. Both are
permitted and this rule has to hold for either, because an implementation is
free to choose — but a reader should not take the register-first branch for
what linking necessarily looks like.

Nothing is lost by skipping, and the publisher is not expected to come back. Once
the new device publishes its prekeys it obtains the current epoch key from
another device of its own account, which the rule below permits without an
admin. That is the case the same-account rule exists for: an admin who rotates a
group has no way to know when somebody else's phone finishes setting itself up,
and should not have to. Sealing one envelope per *account* is the mistake
this rule exists to forbid — it is what an earlier draft of this SIP described,
before accounts and devices were distinguished, and it leaves the devices it
missed holding no key.

Nothing reports that failure, which is why the rule is a MUST rather than
advice. A device with no envelope for the current epoch fetches entries
successfully, cannot open any of them, and looks from every other member's side
exactly like somebody who is not reading. The Security considerations below say
the same thing about a member an admin never sealed to; a publisher that
enumerates carelessly produces it by accident.

The same applies to a **rotation**, which is where it is most easily got wrong:
minting an epoch and sealing it to the devices you happened to have listed
earlier silently blinds every device registered since. Enumerate at the moment
of publishing.

**Any device of an account MAY publish envelopes to that account's other
devices**, without being an admin of the channel. This is how a newly linked
client obtains the current key: the person's existing client seals it across.
Publishing to a device of *another* account requires being an admin, which is
what an invitation is.

### History is the inviter's choice

The epoch range is how much of the past an envelope hands over. `from_epoch`
equal to `to_epoch` gives the current key and nothing else: the new member reads
what is said from now on, and the entries above are ciphertext they cannot open.
A wider range hands over history as far back as the inviter chooses.

The default **SHOULD** be the current epoch only. Widening it is an explicit act.

This is the one policy decision in the chat set that is visible on the wire, and
it is there on purpose. Every group chat system answers "can the person I just
added read what we said last week", most answer it invisibly, and the answer
matters to the people already in the room rather than to the one arriving. A
client SHOULD tell the existing members which answer was given.

Note what this does and does not achieve. Withholding old keys means the new
member cannot read old entries **from the exchange**. It does nothing about any
member choosing to forward them, and nothing about a client that keeps a local
copy. It is a default, not a boundary.

### Messages

```
Put     | type: u8 = 0x01 | channel[32] | epoch: u32 | count: u16
        | count × (recipient[32] || from_epoch: u32 || to_epoch: u32
                   || prekey_id: u32 || ephemeral[32] || len: u32
                   || ciphertext) |
Get     | type: u8 = 0x02 | channel[32] | since_epoch: u32 |
Missing | type: u8 = 0x03 | channel[32] |

PutAck  | accepted: u8 | epoch: u32 | now: u64 |
Got     | now: u64 | count: u16
        | count × (from_epoch: u32 || to_epoch: u32 || prekey_id: u32
                   || ephemeral[32] || len: u32 || ciphertext) |
Absent  | epoch: u32 | now: u64 | count: u16
        | count × (account[32] || device[32] || has_prekeys: u8) |
```

`Put` carries a **SIP-31 action** when — and only when — its `epoch` advances the
channel's current one, because that is the case which writes a `rotated` system
entry and there must be no unsigned way to produce one. A same-epoch `Put` adds
envelopes, writes nothing, and signs nothing; who published which envelope
therefore remains a transport observation, which SIP-31 names as its nearest
residual gap rather than closing.

Endpoints `POST /channel/key/{put,get,missing}`, each refusing a connection
with no advertised identity.

`Put` requires the caller to be an admin of `channel`, **or** to be publishing
only to devices of its own account — the case that lets a person link a client
without an admin's involvement. An admin may publish to any member's devices;
that is what an invitation does, and it is the only way a new member is ever
given a key. It publishes envelopes for one epoch in one request, and `epoch`
MUST be either the channel's current epoch or exactly one greater. A non-admin
publishing to its own devices MUST use the current epoch, since minting one is
ordinarily an admin's act.

**Ordinarily, but not always: a member may rekey after revoking one of its own
devices.** An exchange MUST accept an epoch-advancing `Put` from a member who is
not an admin when SIP-22 holds a revocation by that member's account made since
the current epoch was minted. That permits exactly one rotation per revocation
per channel and no others, and the exchange can check it because it holds both
facts.

Without this, the advice to rotate after a revocation is advice nobody can
follow. Losing a phone in a group where you are an ordinary member would leave
you able to revoke the device and unable to change the key, so whoever holds the
phone keeps reading every future message until an admin — who may be
unreachable, or asleep, or long gone — happens to act. It is not an escalation:
the member already holds the current key and can already read everything. All
they gain is the ability to stop a key they know to be compromised from
continuing to work.

One greater **advances the channel's current epoch**: that is a rotation, and it
is what a removal is followed by. Equal to the current epoch adds envelopes
without rotating, which is how a new member is handed the key that is already in
use, and how the envelopes for a large channel or a deep history range are split
across several requests when they do not fit one body. An exchange **MUST**
refuse an envelope for a `(channel, recipient, epoch)` that already has one, and
MUST answer `accepted: 0` with the epoch that actually stands rather than an
error, changing nothing.

That last rule is what settles the direct-message creation race in SIP-16. Both
ends create the channel naming each other, so whichever create is processed
first makes both of them members and an admin of the winner; both then mint
epoch 1 and publish envelopes to both identities. One `Put` wins. The loser —
whose recipients already hold envelopes for epoch 1 — is told `accepted: 0`,
discards its key and calls `Get`. Because neither may post under an epoch it did
not publish or receive, no entry is ever sealed under the key that was thrown
away.

`Get` returns every envelope addressed to the caller for that channel with
`to_epoch` at or above `since_epoch`, oldest first. An exchange serves an
envelope **only to the recipient it names**. It stores envelopes opaquely and
holds no key that opens one.

`Missing` lists the devices of the channel's members that hold **no envelope for
the current epoch**, and whether each has published prekeys. It is answerable to
an admin, and to a member about its own account's devices.

It exists because otherwise a device can be stranded with no way to say so. Its
own account's other devices can seal to it, which covers the ordinary case of
adding a second client — but somebody whose only device was lost and replaced
has no sibling to ask, and nothing in this SIP lets a device request an
envelope. Without `Missing` such a member waits for a rotation that nobody knows
is needed, and the wait is invisible: they fetch entries successfully and open
none of them, which looks exactly like not reading.

An admin healing that gap publishes at the **current** epoch, which is the
same-epoch `Put` and needs no rotation. A client SHOULD check `Missing` after
inviting somebody and after any device registers, and SHOULD show a member whose
devices are all listed as a member who cannot read.

An envelope is retained as long as the channel retains any entry sealed under an
epoch it covers, and MAY be dropped once the last such entry is pruned. A member
that has read an envelope keeps its own copy of the keys; the exchange's copy is
for collection, not for storage.

### Limits

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| Envelopes per `Put` | 256 |
| Epochs per envelope range | 1 024 |
| Epochs per channel | 65 536 |
| Envelope ciphertext | 16 + 32 × range bytes |

These follow from the 64 KiB request body, and the arithmetic is worth showing
because the recipient count is now **devices** rather than accounts. One
envelope is 80 bytes of header plus 16 + 32 × range of ciphertext, so at the
current epoch it is 128 bytes and 256 of them come to 32 KiB — comfortably
inside the body, with room for the range to be wider than one. A single envelope
granting the maximum 1 024-epoch range is 32 KiB and also fits. The cap is set
by the request body and by wanting margin against it, not by any property of a
channel.

A fully populated channel now needs several `Put` calls: 256 accounts at the
recommended maximum of 8 devices each is 2 048 envelopes, so eight requests at
the same epoch. That is what the same-epoch `Put` is for, and it is why the
round-one rule that a `Put` may repeat an epoch matters more than it appeared
to. A `Put` that would exceed the request cap MUST be refused rather than
truncated.

## Rationale

**Why a group key here when SIP-13 rejected one there.** SIP-13's reasoning is
sound at its size and for its purpose: mesh voice between eight people saves
nothing at the exchange, because it holds eight connections and cannot
multicast, so its egress is N(N−1) frames either way. The saving was a client's
uplink only, and the cost was a distribution and rotation policy. Two things
change here. The exchange **stores** rather than relays, so one ciphertext
instead of 255 is a real reduction in what it holds, not a re-arrangement of
what it sends. And a stored log has to be readable by someone who was not
present when it was written, which pairwise sessions cannot express at all.
Meanwhile the cost SIP-13 declined — a rotation policy — is the feature: it is
what makes removal mean anything.

**Why the subkey is per device rather than per person.** A person is the unit
everything else in the chat set counts in — membership, mentions, receipts,
display — and it is the wrong unit here. Two clients of one person are two
independent counters, and a construction that gives them one key makes their
collision a matter of timing rather than an impossibility. The rule this SIP
follows is that separation must be structural: the same argument that rejects a
random nonce below rejects trusting two devices to coordinate a counter.

**Why the subkey is per sender rather than a random nonce.** A 96-bit random
nonce under a shared key is safe at these volumes, and it was still rejected. It
gives up duplicate detection, which the per-sender counter provides for free; it
makes correctness depend on the quality of a random number generator at every
sender rather than on arithmetic; and a shared key with random nonces is one
refactor away from a shared key with a shared counter. Deriving the separation
into the key makes reuse between senders impossible rather than improbable.

**Why the counter is in the clear.** It could be inside the sealed body, at the
cost of the receiver being unable to check for reuse until after decrypting with
a nonce it had to guess. The exchange already knows who posted each entry and in
what order, so `msg_seq` tells it that a sender's messages are numbered, which
it could have counted anyway. There is one genuine disclosure: gaps reveal that
a member sealed something it did not post, or posted to a channel the observer
cannot see. It is small, and it is stated here rather than left to be found.

**Why an admin mints keys rather than a member-agreement protocol.** A proper
continuous group key agreement — every member contributing, no single party able
to choose the key — exists, is well studied, and is far larger than this. It
would remove the trust in an admin, which Security considerations names as this
design's weakest point. It was rejected for the first version because the rest
of the model already trusts the admin with membership: an admin who wants to
read a channel adds an identity of their own, which no key agreement prevents.
Paying for a protocol that closes one door while the door beside it is open is
the wrong first trade. This is the natural place for a later SIP, and the
envelope wire would not have to change.

**Why rotation is not automatic on join.** Rotating on every join means no new
member ever reads history, which is a policy some channels want and many do not.
Making it the inviter's choice keeps both available. Rotation on *removal* is
not a choice, because there the alternative is a removal that does not remove.

**Why the exchange stores envelopes at all.** It could be left to the members to
hand keys over out of band, which would be one less thing the exchange sees. But
the envelope is already sealed to a key the exchange cannot use, so what it
learns is who was given a key and when — and it knows who was invited and when
regardless, from the membership it enforces. In exchange, an invitation works
while the invitee is asleep, which is the difference between a usable system and
one where adding someone requires a rendezvous.

## Compatibility

Purely additive. Two new routes. It changes no existing wire format and depends
on no change to SIP-3, SIP-5 or SIP-12; it reuses SIP-5's sealing construction
under a different context string, so the two derivations produce different keys
over the same inputs and neither can be confused for the other.

An exchange implementing SIP-16 without this SIP can carry only public channels.
A client that cannot rotate can read but MUST NOT be permitted to remove a
member, because a removal it cannot follow with a rotation would misrepresent
what happened.

## Security considerations

**A revoked device keeps every key it held.** Revocation stops the exchange
serving it and stops the next epoch's envelope reaching it — and the rotation
that achieves the second part is one any member may perform for their own
revocation, so it does not wait on an admin. It does nothing
about the epochs it already collected, which sit on a machine somebody has lost
control of — which is the whole reason a rotation should follow a revocation as
it follows a removal. SIP-20 makes the same point about the credential itself:
revocation is prospective, everywhere in this design.

**Rotation is revocation, and it is not forward secrecy.** The two are easy to
confuse and this SIP should not help: rotation stops a removed member reading
what is said *next*, and does nothing whatever about what was said before. That
member keeps every key it was ever given, and every entry sealed under those
epochs stays readable to it forever, from its own disk, whatever the exchange
subsequently does. No construction here reaches backwards, and an interface that
presents removal as erasure is lying to the people who asked for it.

Forward secrecy proper — an attacker who records ciphertext today and obtains a
key tomorrow still cannot read it — is addressed for the envelope path by
SIP-23, and is not achieved per message by anything here. See the paragraph
below on identity-key compromise, and SIP-23's own security section for what it
does and does not buy.

**An admin is trusted by construction, and this is the weakest point in the
design.** An admin can mint an epoch and seal it to any identity at all,
including one the other members would object to and one that is not in the
member list. The member list in SIP-16's `Info` is what makes the second case
visible, and clients MUST show it — but an admin who adds an identity and then
removes it has still handed over a key that stays valid until the next rotation.
There is no defence within this model. There is a defence in a group key
agreement, and this SIP does not have one.

**Compromise of an identity key no longer opens recorded envelopes, and that is
SIP-23's doing.** Before it, the seal was one-sided in the manner of SIP-5 — the
recipient's static key was the only long-term secret in the derivation — so an
attacker who kept a copy of the traffic and later obtained that key read every
channel key the device had been given, and frequent rotation made the haul
larger rather than smaller. DH2 removes it: the prekey secret is destroyed on
use and cannot be recomputed, so a recorded envelope stays shut.

**What remains exposed is what the device itself holds.** A device that can show
last month's conversation is storing last month's channel keys, and taking the
device takes them. SIP-23 protects key distribution, not key storage, and the
distinction is the difference between an attacker who copied the exchange's
envelopes and one who has somebody's laptop. Only the first is defended against
here, and an implementation MUST NOT describe the result as forward secrecy
without saying which of the two it means.

**The exchange sees the envelope graph.** Who was given a key, for which epochs,
by whom, and when. Combined with the membership it already enforces, it can tell
when a channel rotated, and therefore when somebody was removed, and it can tell
which members were granted history and which were not.

**A member who never reads is indistinguishable from one who cannot.** A member
that was never sent an envelope for the current epoch — by accident or by an
admin's choice — appears in the member list, can fetch entries, and simply
cannot open them. Nothing in the protocol reports this. A client SHOULD notice
that it cannot decrypt the current epoch and say so plainly rather than showing
an empty or broken conversation.

**Nonce reuse is the failure mode to test for.** If two senders ever share a
subkey, or one sender reuses a `msg_seq`, ChaCha20-Poly1305 leaks the XOR of the
two plaintexts and its authentication guarantee collapses. The per-sender
derivation makes the first impossible and the receiver-side duplicate check
catches the second. An implementation SHOULD have a test that a repeated
`(sender, epoch, msg_seq)` is rejected, and SHOULD refuse to seal if its counter
did not advance.

**The key is only as private as its holders.** Up to 256 people hold the channel
key. Any one of them can publish it, and then anyone who can reach the exchange
and guess or learn the channel identifier can read everything for that epoch —
though not post, because posting requires membership the exchange enforces. This
is inherent to a shared key and is the honest description of what a private
channel protects against: the exchange, and the network. Not its own members.

## Reference implementation

Implemented in [sqex](https://github.com/wave-cl/sqex) v0.9.0, on squic v0.15.0
(SIP-3), with two rules of this document arriving only in v0.10.3 — see below,
because the gap between them is the more useful part:

- `sqex-proto::channel_key` holds `ChannelKey`, the envelope, the two-term
  derivation and the sender-key KDF. `ChannelKey` does not print itself in
  `Debug`.
- `Replay` is the receiver's side of the `(device, epoch, msg_seq)` rule, and it
  is a shipped type rather than a note in this document because the exchange
  cannot check the counter and the recipient is the only party who can.
- `sqexd::channel` stores envelopes under `(channel, recipient, epoch)` and
  serves each one only to the recipient it names. That primary key is also what
  settles the direct-message creation race: both ends mint epoch 1 and publish,
  one `Put` wins, and the loser is told so and collects instead.
- The recipient is a **device**, and the membership check resolves it to an
  account through a closure the exchange passes in — the same shape SIP-21's
  block check uses, so the channel store keeps no dependency on the device
  registry.
- Routes: `POST /channel/key/{put,get,missing}`.

**Two rules here were text for a whole release line, and the reason is worth
recording.** Until v0.10.3 the envelope was addressed to an *account* at both
ends: `Put` checked the recipient against the member table, which is keyed by
account, and `Get` looked envelopes up the same way. So the same-account rule
above — the one that lets a person link a client without an admin — could not be
obeyed, and neither could the per-device sealing this SIP requires.

It went unnoticed for the whole 0.9 line because an account with no registered
devices *is* its own device (SIP-22), so the two keys were identical and the
narrowing had no observable effect until somebody linked a second client. A
degenerate case had made a MUST untestable. The lesson is not that the
specification was insufficient; it is that a conformance test needs a case where
the two identities actually differ.

The post-revocation rekey was unimplemented for the same span, and dormant for
the same reason: with one device per account nobody had a device to revoke. It
needed two facts the exchange was not keeping — which account a revoked device
belonged to, since the device row is deleted and takes the account with it, and
when the current epoch was minted.

The per-device subkey is the correction of a defect this SIP had while it was
being written, and the implementation is where the cost of the alternative
became concrete: two clients under one identity share a key, and both start
their counter at zero. The exchange keeps a high-water `msg_seq` per
`(channel, device, epoch)` **independently of the entries**, since pruning
would understate it and a client resuming from an understated mark would reuse a
nonce.

Integration tests cover two people holding a conversation the exchange cannot
read; a removed member refused and the next epoch not theirs; an envelope served
only to its named recipient; an envelope that cannot be published twice for one
epoch; a public channel having no keys to distribute; a removal leaving a record
of who did it; and a forged edit ignored by the reader because the exchange
cannot check it.

Since v0.10.3 they also cover the two rules named above, and each is written so
that it fails against the code that preceded it: two devices of one account
sealing under different subkeys with independent counters; a device taking the
current epoch from a sibling with no admin involved; a member rekeying after
revoking one of its own devices, and being refused when it has revoked nothing;
`Missing` naming a device rather than an account, and staying silent when every
device is sealed; and a key arriving after the entries it opens, which used to
leave them shut for good.
