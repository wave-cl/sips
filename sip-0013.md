## Abstract

A set of identities discover one another at an exchange by presenting the same
room secret, and then talk in pairs. The exchange keeps a roster and nothing
else: it never learns the room secret, cannot join a room it relays, and cannot
forge a member. Media is not specified here — every pair runs an ordinary
SIP-12 session, so a room adds multi-party conversation without adding any
cryptography.

## Motivation

SIP-12 carries a conversation between two identities through an exchange that
can read none of it. Three is the obvious next question, and the SIP-12 key
agreement cannot answer it: DH1 and DH2 each require one peer's *static*
private key, which is exactly what denies the exchange a place in the middle.
There is no three-party form of that construction without new cryptography, and
mixing at the exchange is worse than useless — it would have to be able to hear
the call, which is the one thing the design refuses.

The way out is to notice that a room does not need to be a media primitive. If
the members can find each other, SIP-12 already connects each pair, and mixing
is a client's job anyway. What is missing is only the finding: a way for a set
of identities to learn who else is present, without the exchange being the one
who decides.

That is what this SIP is. It is a roster.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. Every operation is bound to the caller's
transport identity (SIP-3). A connection carrying no identity has no membership
and MUST be refused.

### The room secret

A room is named by a **secret**: 32 bytes, generated uniformly at random. It is
not a name, not chosen by a person, and not derived from anything. A room is as
private as its secret, and guessing is not a threat model at this width.

Three values are derived from it. `ROOM_CONTEXT` is the ASCII string
`sqex-room-v1` and `MEMBER_CONTEXT` is the ASCII string `sqex-room-member-v1`.

```
handle = SHA-256(ROOM_CONTEXT || secret)
proof  = HMAC-SHA256(secret, MEMBER_CONTEXT || identity)
```

The **handle** is what the exchange routes on. The secret itself MUST NOT be
sent to the exchange, and an implementation MUST NOT provide any operation that
would transmit it. The exchange therefore cannot join a room it carries, and
its memory and logs contain nothing that would let anyone else join one.

The **proof** binds a member to the secret. It is computed over the member's
Ed25519 identity, so it cannot be replayed under a different name.

The exchange MUST NOT verify proofs — it holds no secret to verify them with —
and MUST relay each member's proof unaltered in the roster. **Verification is
the members' job**: a client MUST check every other member's proof against the
secret it holds, and MUST NOT open a session with, decrypt from, or display a
member whose proof does not verify. Verification MUST be constant time.

### Messages

```
Join    | type: u8 = 0x01 | handle[32] | proof[32] |
Leave   | type: u8 = 0x02 | handle[32] |
Roster  | now: u64 | count: u16 | (identity[32] || proof[32]) * count |
```

`POST /room/join` records the caller's identity as a member of `handle` and
returns a `Roster`. It is idempotent, and doubles as the heartbeat and the
roster poll: a member calls it every `HEARTBEAT_SECS` and needs no other
request. The roster **excludes the caller** — a member knows its own name — and
MUST be sorted ascending by identity bytes, so that two members comparing
rosters compare the same thing.

`POST /room/leave` removes the caller from `handle` immediately. It is not
required; it is a courtesy that saves the other members a TTL of waiting.

A join whose `handle` names a room at `MAX_MEMBERS` MUST be refused, and MUST
NOT evict an existing member. A refusal discloses that the room is full, which
is unavoidable, and nothing else.

### Membership is soft state

Membership expires `TTL_SECS` after the member's last join. A member that
crashes, loses its connection or is killed therefore leaves the room without
having said anything, which is the common case and needs no protocol.

There is no create and no destroy. A room exists exactly while it has members;
an exchange MUST forget a room once its last membership expires. Room state
MUST be held in memory only — a room is live coordination between connected
peers, and a restart honestly ends it.

Recommended values: `TTL_SECS` 30, `HEARTBEAT_SECS` 2, `MAX_MEMBERS` 8.
`MAX_MEMBERS` is a policy choice an operator may lower; see Rationale for why
raising it is not free.

### What the members do with the roster

Nothing in this SIP. A member that wants to talk to another opens a SIP-12
session naming it, exactly as if it had learned the identity any other way.
Because every member does this for every other member, the pairs establish
themselves, and SIP-12's requirement that **both** parties name each other is
satisfied honestly rather than waived. A room grants no consent on anyone's
behalf.

An exchange MUST NOT treat room membership as consent to anything. In
particular it MUST NOT pre-open, suggest or infer sessions between members.

## Rationale

**Why a roster and not a group key.** The alternative is a shared group key with
per-sender derivation, which lets each member encrypt once instead of once per
peer. It was rejected at this size. The saving is in a *client's* uplink only:
the exchange holds N separate connections and cannot multicast, so its egress is
N(N−1) frames either way. The cost is a key distribution mechanism, a rotation
policy for join and for leave, and a class of failure — a member decrypting what
it should not — that pairwise sessions simply do not have. Paying that to
relieve a load that does not move is a poor trade. For rooms an order of
magnitude larger the arithmetic changes, and this SIP does not pretend
otherwise; that is a different proposal, and it can reuse this roster.

**Why mesh media is affordable here.** At 24 kbit/s an eight-person room costs a
client about 168 kbit/s of uplink, one Opus encoder — the frame is encoded once
and sealed per peer — and seven decoders. That is unremarkable on any connection
that could carry the call at all. It scales as the square, which is why
`MAX_MEMBERS` exists and is small.

**Why the exchange never sees the secret.** It only needs to group members, and
a hash groups them just as well. Withholding the secret converts "the exchange
is trusted not to join" into "the exchange is unable to join", and makes a leak
of its state worthless to an attacker. This costs one hash.

**Why members verify each other rather than the exchange verifying.** Because
the exchange cannot: it holds no secret. That is the point rather than a
limitation. Verification placed with the members is what stops the exchange
adding an identity of its own to the roster and listening, and it is the reason
this SIP can offer a bearer-secret room without also offering a bridge anyone
who runs an exchange can tap.

**Why no create, and no owner.** An owner implies revocation, revocation implies
an authorization list, and an authorization list is a different room model — one
where the exchange decides who may enter and must therefore be trusted with that
decision. This model puts the decision entirely in who holds the secret. Both are
defensible; only one of them needs the exchange to be honest.

**Why leave is optional.** A protocol whose correctness depends on clients
announcing their departure is a protocol that is wrong whenever a laptop lid
closes. The TTL is the mechanism; leave is an optimisation.

## Compatibility

Purely additive. Two new routes, no change to any existing wire format, and no
change to SIP-3 or SIP-12. An exchange without this SIP answers 404 and a client
without it never asks, so old and new interoperate in both directions on
everything else. No flag day.

## Security considerations

**The secret is a bearer capability, and has no revocation.** Anyone who obtains
it can join, including a member who was legitimately given it and later should
not have it. Removing someone means agreeing a new secret and moving. This is
the deliberate trade of a room that no one owns, and an implementation MUST NOT
present it as anything else.

**The roster is the only defence against an unwanted member, so clients MUST
show it.** Proof verification bounds *who can be inserted* by the exchange or by
a network attacker; it does nothing about who has been told the secret. A member
who cannot see who is present cannot exercise the one control they have.

**The exchange learns the roster.** A pairwise SIP-12 session discloses a pair;
a room discloses a group, which is a stronger statement about a set of people
than anything else in this stack reveals. It learns identities, arrival and
departure times, and — because the media is relayed — who is talking to whom
inside the room. It learns nothing of the content, and nothing about the room
itself beyond a handle it cannot invert.

**Timing and volume are not hidden.** As in SIP-12, an exchange carrying media
observes frame sizes and cadence. Across a room this is more revealing than it
is between two: an observer can tell how many people are speaking and, from the
pattern, plausibly who is talking to whom about what turn.

**A room multiplies an already unpoliced relay.** SIP-12 notes that the
unreliable path is not queued and needs a rate policy of its own; a room turns
one media stream per pair into N(N−1) of them for as long as the call lasts. An
operator exposing rooms publicly needs that policy before doing so, not after.

**Denial of service against a specific room** is possible for anyone holding the
secret: joining repeatedly under fresh identities fills it to `MAX_MEMBERS` and
keeps everyone else out. There is no defence within this model, because the
model has no authority to appeal to. An exchange MAY rate-limit joins per
connection, which raises the cost without changing the conclusion.

**Voice activity detection is not specified and is not free.** An implementation
that simply stops sending while its speaker is silent creates gaps that a
receiver cannot distinguish from loss, and a jitter buffer will conceal them —
inventing speech out of silence. Doing this properly requires the codec's own
discontinuous transmission and a receiver that understands it. It is the obvious
way to cut mesh traffic to roughly 2(N−1), and it is deliberately left to a
later proposal rather than half-done here.

## Reference implementation

Shipped in [sqex](https://github.com/wave-cl/sqex) v0.7.0, on squic v0.15.0
(SIP-3):

- `sqex-proto::room` holds the layouts and the three derivations. `RoomId` will
  render its secret only when asked to and does not do so in `Debug`, which
  prints the handle instead.
- `sqexd::room` is the roster: handle → identity → `{proof, last_seen}`, with
  expiry on access and rooms that cease to exist with their last member. It
  stores proofs and does not read them.
- Routes: `POST /room/{join,leave}`, both refusing a connection with no
  advertised identity.
- `sqex-voice room <secret>` joins, verifies every listed member's proof, opens
  a SIP-12 session with each one that passes, and mixes what comes back. It
  shows the roster and who is speaking, because the roster is the only control
  a member has. `--new` mints a secret.

Integration tests cover three identities finding each other and each hearing
the other two and not itself, measured note by note through a real exchange;
every pair in a room holding a different key, so one member's two sessions
cannot read each other; a room refusing a ninth member without evicting an
eighth; a member leaving and the others noticing; the room secret being absent
from the bytes that reach the exchange; and — the one that earns the design — an
identity that joins under the right handle with a proof it could not have
computed being accepted by the exchange, which cannot know better, and rejected
by the members, who can.

One implementation note that follows from the mesh rather than from this SIP: a
client with no discontinuous transmission sends continuously, so a peer that
goes silent for several seconds has a broken session rather than nothing to say.
The reference implementation treats that as a signal, discards the session and
builds it again — which recovers the case where a peer restarted and its frames
now arrive under a session id nobody holds.
