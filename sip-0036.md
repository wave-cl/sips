## Abstract

Starting a call from a conversation, using media that already ships. The
invitation is an ordinary SIP-19 body sealed into a SIP-16 channel, so it
inherits SIP-31's signature, SIP-17's sealing and SIP-34's receipt without
adding cryptography; it carries a SIP-13 room secret the exchange never sees.
Ring state — ringing, accepted, declined, busy — travels as SIP-16 signals,
which are ephemeral and forgeable and are used for nothing durable. The record
of what happened is a second entry targeting the first, signed like any other
message. **No new media, no new key agreement, and one new rule: a call signal
is delivered per device, not per account.**

## Motivation

The README's account of the chat set names this as a successor and says what it
would be:

> **Calls started from a chat** would be signalling only — ring, accept,
> decline, and a missed-call entry — since SIP-13 and SIP-15 already carry the
> media, though video appears nowhere in SIP-15.

Both halves are already built and shipped. SIP-12 relays a session two peers
cannot otherwise establish; SIP-13 makes a room out of a secret; SIP-15 puts
Opus through it with comfort noise. The chat set is Active across SIP-16 to
SIP-24. What is missing is the twenty bytes that connect them: a way to say
*let us talk now* inside a conversation that already exists, to a person whose
devices you already know about.

SIP-19 anticipated it in its forward-compatibility rule, naming "a call record"
as an example of what a later body type is for. This is that body type.

### Why this needs a document rather than a client convention

Three things in it are not obvious and two of them are wrong by default.

**Signals are delivered per account and at most once.** That is correct for
typing and exactly wrong for ringing: SIP-22 gives a person several devices, and
a ring that reaches one of them is a call the other three never hear. SIP-30
compounds it with a rule that is right everywhere else — a signal event MUST NOT
go to the account that caused it — which is precisely the account whose *other*
devices need to stop ringing when one accepts.

**A room secret is a bearer capability with no revocation.** SIP-13 says so. Put
one in a durable log and it stays valid as long as the room does, retrievable by
anyone who can read the channel, for as long as retention keeps it.

**The room caps at eight and a channel does not.** SIP-16 allows 256 members and
SIP-13's mesh is 2(N−1) streams. Something has to happen when the ninth person
accepts, and it should be the same thing in every implementation.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. This document adds two SIP-19 body types,
one SIP-19 signal kind, and one SIP-30 event kind. It adds nothing to SIP-16's
request wire and no cryptography anywhere.

### The invitation

A new SIP-19 body type:

```
| 0x06 | Call | media: u8 | ring_secs: u16 | secret[32] |
```

`secret` is a SIP-13 room secret, generated uniformly at random by the caller as
SIP-13 requires. In a private channel the body is sealed under SIP-17 like every
other body, so the secret reaches exactly the members and the exchange carries a
room it cannot join.

`media` is a bitfield. Bit 0 is audio. **Every other bit is reserved and a
receiver MUST ignore bits it does not know** — including bit 1, which an
implementation may be tempted to read as video. There is no video framing in this
stack: SIP-15 defines audio and says nothing about a second stream, and a client
MUST NOT infer one. Video needs its own document, and this byte is where it will
attach when somebody writes it.

`ring_secs` is how long the caller intends to wait before giving up. It is
advisory to the callee's user interface and load-bearing for exactly one thing,
below: deciding that an unanswered call was missed.

**A `Call` body SHOULD carry a short `expires_after`.** SIP-16 makes
`expires_after` a sender's instruction that the exchange enforces as a backstop,
and a call invitation is the clearest case for one anywhere in this stack — the
entry holds a bearer capability that outlives its usefulness within a minute. An
implementation SHOULD set it to a small multiple of `ring_secs`. A client MUST
NOT rely on this for security; it shortens an exposure that cannot be closed.

### The record of what happened

```
| 0x07 | CallEnd | target: u64 | outcome: u8 | duration: u32 |
```

`target` is the `seq` of the `Call` entry, in the shape SIP-19 already uses for
`Reaction`, `Edit` and `Redact`.

| `outcome` | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0x01 | Answered — `duration` is seconds of media |
| 0x02 | Declined by a callee |
| 0x03 | Missed — nobody answered within `ring_secs` |
| 0x04 | Cancelled by the caller before an answer |
| 0x05 | Failed — no session could be established |

`duration` is 0 for every outcome but answered.

A `CallEnd` is an ordinary entry: signed by the device that posted it, chained,
receipted, and sealed. **It is the only durable account of the call**, and it is
as verifiable as any other message, which is the point of putting it here rather
than deriving it from signals.

Any member MAY post one. The caller normally does; the answering device posts
one for a call it ended. Two `CallEnd`s targeting one `Call` are not an error —
two parties observed the same call ending — and a reader SHOULD display the
first by `seq` and MAY reconcile the rest.

**A `Call` with no `CallEnd` and `posted + ring_secs` in the past is missed.**
A reader derives this rather than waiting for an entry, because the party that
would have posted it is the party whose client crashed, lost its connection, or
was closed — which is the common case and needs no protocol. An implementation
MUST make that derivation rather than displaying a call as perpetually ringing.

### Ring state

A new SIP-19 signal kind, relayed by SIP-16 and never stored:

```
| 0x03 | CallState | target: u64 | state: u8 | device[32] |
```

| `state` | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0x01 | Ringing — this device is alerting |
| 0x02 | Accepted — this device has joined the room |
| 0x03 | Declined — this device's user refused |
| 0x04 | Busy — this device is already in a call |
| 0x05 | Ended — this device has left the room |

`device` is the SIP-22 device key of the signalling client, which is what makes
the fan-out rules below expressible.

**Nothing durable may be derived from a `CallState`.** SIP-31 states that signals
carry an account the exchange asserted and are forgeable by it permanently, and
SIP-16 sets the standard of service plainly: "an exchange that dropped every
signal it was ever given would still conform", and "signals are a courtesy, and
nothing may depend on one arriving." A client MUST NOT write a call's outcome
from a signal, MUST NOT show a signal's claim as a fact about a person, and MUST
fall back on the derivation above when signals do not arrive. They drive a
ringing screen and nothing else.

### The two delivery rules this document exists for

SIP-16 delivers a signal to a channel's other members, at most once per
recipient. SIP-30 sends the corresponding event to the channel's members and
MUST NOT send it to the account that caused it.

For signal kind `0x03` only, both change:

1. **A `CallState` is delivered per device, not per account.** An exchange MUST
   deliver it to every connection of every member, at most once per *device*,
   rather than at most once per account.
2. **A `CallState` is delivered to the causing account's other devices**, and
   MUST NOT be delivered back to the device named in `device`.

Rule 1 makes ringing work at all. SIP-22 gives a person up to eight devices; a
signal that reaches one connection has rung one of somebody's phones and left the
rest silent, and no client-side arrangement can recover a message the exchange
delivered elsewhere.

Rule 2 is how the other devices stop. It is a deliberate exception to SIP-30's
rule, and that rule's justification is what shows it to be an exception rather
than a contradiction: *a client does not need telling that its own keyboard is in
use*. A device does need telling that its user answered somewhere else. The
exclusion narrows from the account to the one device that already knows.

An exchange MUST apply both rules for kind `0x03` and MUST NOT apply them to
kind `0x01`. An exchange that does not implement this SIP relays `0x03` under
SIP-16's ordinary rules; ringing then works for single-device accounts and
degrades to one-device-rings for the rest, which is a visible, ordinary-looking
failure and is called out in Compatibility for that reason.

### The event

SIP-30 gains one kind:

```
| 0x09 | [32] channel, [8] seq | a call is ringing in this channel |
```

It names the channel and the invitation's `seq` and carries nothing else, per
SIP-30's central constraint. A client fetches to learn the rest.

SIP-30's existing `0x02` already announces a waiting signal, so a dedicated kind
needs justifying. The justification is priority, not information: SIP-30 exists
because clients were polling nine times a second and its whole purpose is to let
an idle client be quiet, so a client that has throttled its signal fetching is
behaving correctly — and must not throttle this one. A distinguishable kind is
what lets it tell the difference between somebody typing and a phone ringing.

`0x09` is sent to the accounts presently in the channel, on the same terms as
`0x01`–`0x04`. It is subject to SIP-21's blocking rules like everything else: an
exchange MUST NOT deliver it between accounts where either has blocked the other,
which is what stops this becoming a way to make a blocked person's phone ring.

### The flow

1. The caller mints a room secret, posts a `Call` body, and joins the SIP-13
   room. It signals `ringing`.
2. The exchange writes the entry, receipts it (SIP-34), and emits `0x09` to the
   channel's members.
3. Each of the callee's devices opens the entry, learns the secret, and alerts.
   Each signals `ringing`.
4. A device accepts: it signals `accepted`, joins the room, and opens a SIP-12
   session with each member already present, exactly as SIP-13 specifies. Media
   is SIP-15 framing on SIP-12 datagrams.
5. The callee's other devices see the `accepted` naming a sibling device and
   stop alerting.
6. When the last participant leaves, whoever ended it posts a `CallEnd`.

Nothing in steps 4 or 5 is new. The room, the session, the key agreement and the
framing are SIP-13, SIP-12 and SIP-15 unchanged, which is the reason this
document is short.

### Two devices accepting at once

Two of one person's devices may accept before either sees the other's signal.
SIP-13 offers no arbitration and cannot: a room is named by a secret, holding it
is what membership consists of, and there is no authority to appeal to.

So arbitration is best-effort and by convention: **a device that learns of an
`accepted` for the same `target` from a sibling device whose key sorts lower
MUST leave the room.** Lexicographic order on key bytes, the same tiebreak
SIP-12 uses to decide which peer is `first`.

An implementation MUST NOT treat this as a guarantee. If the signal is dropped —
and SIP-16 permits dropping every signal — both devices stay in the room, and
one person is present twice. That is cosmetic: both devices belong to the same
person, both are legitimate members holding the secret, and no security property
depends on the count. Saying so is better than specifying a consensus protocol
for a wart.

### Group calls, and where they stop

SIP-13 caps a room at `MAX_MEMBERS`, recommended 8, and its media is a mesh of
2(N−1) streams. SIP-16 allows 256 members in a channel. These do not reconcile
and this document does not pretend otherwise.

A join past the cap is refused by SIP-13's existing rule, which "discloses that
the room is full, which is unavoidable, and nothing else". A client MUST present
that refusal as the call being full and MUST NOT retry it as an error.

**A channel larger than `MAX_MEMBERS` cannot hold a call everyone joins, and
raising the cap is not the fix.** SIP-13 explains why in its own rationale: the
mesh is quadratic, and the alternative — mixing at the exchange — "would have to
be able to hear the call, which is the one thing the design refuses." A
selective forwarding unit is the standard answer and it is the same answer: it
forwards streams it cannot read only if the keys are per-pair, which is the mesh
again, or it is given the keys, which is the refusal. This is a real limit of the
architecture rather than a gap in this document, and an implementation should
show a large channel that a call is limited rather than letting it fail at the
ninth person.

## Rationale

**Why the invitation is an entry and the ring is a signal.** They have different
lifetimes and different trust requirements, and the existing mechanisms already
match them. The invitation must be durable (a missed call is a thing you see
tomorrow), sealed (it carries a secret), and attributable (it is a claim about
who called). An entry is all three for free. Ring state must be fast, must not
accumulate, and is worthless an hour later. A signal is exactly that, and SIP-16
says a typing indicator that persisted for thirty seconds was already the wrong
shape.

**Why the outcome is an entry and not derived from signals.** Signals are
forgeable by the exchange, permanently, and are permitted to be dropped
entirely. A call history built from them would be the exchange's word about who
called whom — which is the class of claim SIP-31 spent a document removing from
the log. A `CallEnd` is signed by a device and chained, so "you declined my call"
is as verifiable as "you sent me this message".

**Why the room secret travels in the entry rather than being derived.** A secret
derived from the channel and a counter would need no bytes and would be
reproducible by every member, which is the problem: it would also be reproducible
by every *former* member, and by anyone holding an old channel key. SIP-13
requires a room secret to be generated uniformly at random and not derived from
anything, and the reason is that a room's privacy is exactly its secret's
unguessability. Thirty-two bytes in a sealed body is the cheap, correct option.

**Why not a new SIP-12 session directly, skipping the room.** For a two-party
call it would work and would be simpler. It was rejected because a direct message
is a channel like any other and a group channel is the same code path — building
the two-party case on sessions and the group case on rooms would mean two
signalling designs, and SIP-13 is already the two-party case plus one. A room of
two is a session with a roster in front of it.

**Why `ring_secs` is in the entry rather than a constant.** A constant would have
to be agreed by every implementation to make the missed-call derivation
consistent, and a caller is the party that knows how long it intends to wait.
Putting it in the invitation makes every reader derive the same outcome from the
same entry, which is the property that matters — two clients showing one call
differently is the failure mode SIP-31 rejected a second clock to avoid.

**Why a per-device signal rather than a new per-device route.** The alternative
was a dedicated call route with its own delivery semantics, which would have
duplicated SIP-16's relaying, its TTL, its caps and its membership checks in a
second place. Changing the delivery rule for one signal kind is a smaller change
than a parallel mechanism, and it keeps calls inside the quotas that already
bound a channel.

**Why not presence.** A client cannot tell whether ringing is worth attempting
without knowing who is online, and SIP-4's beacon could answer that. It is
deliberately not used: SIP-21 rejected presence as "a further disclosure with a
different threat model", and a call that simply rings and times out gets the same
outcome without publishing anybody's availability continuously. The ring is its
own presence check, scoped to one moment and one caller.

## Compatibility

Purely additive. Two SIP-19 body types, one signal kind, one SIP-30 event kind —
every one of them in a space whose forward-compatibility rule already says what
happens.

A client that does not implement this SIP ignores body types `0x06` and `0x07`
under SIP-19's rule that an unknown body "is well-formed; it is simply from a
later version of this document", ignores signal kind `0x03`, and ignores event
`0x09` under SIP-30's identical rule. It shows a conversation with nothing in it
where the calls were. **This is the case those rules were written for**, and an
implementation SHOULD have the test SIP-19 asks for: an unknown type decodes to
"nothing to show" rather than an error.

**An exchange that does not implement this SIP produces a degraded ring, not a
broken one.** It relays `CallState` under SIP-16's ordinary per-account rules, so
one of a person's devices rings and the others do not, and a device that accepts
does not silence its siblings. Calls work; multi-device behaviour is wrong. An
implementation SHOULD detect this — a caller that never sees a sibling's
`ringing` from a multi-device account has evidence of it — and SHOULD say so
rather than presenting it as the callee ignoring the call.

The two delivery rules are the only normative change to an existing behaviour in
this document, and both are scoped to one signal kind that did not previously
exist. An exchange implementing them cannot affect typing.

Nothing needs both implementations to move together.

## Security considerations

**A call invitation is a bearer capability with no revocation, written to a
durable log.** SIP-13 says of every room secret that it "is as private as its
secret", that "anyone you give it to can join, and can pass it on", and that there
is no owner and no way to remove someone. Sealing it into a channel entry
restricts *who is given it* to the channel's members and their devices, which is
the strongest available answer, and does nothing about the rest: the secret stays
valid for as long as the room lives, anybody who read the entry can rejoin later,
and a member removed from the channel tomorrow keeps every secret they read
today. `expires_after` shortens the window in which the entry can be re-read; it
does not shorten the room's life. **An implementation MUST NOT describe a call as
having ended for the purpose of access** — the participants left, and the door is
still open.

**Ringing discloses reachability.** An exchange that relays a `CallState`
`ringing` learns which of a person's devices are connected right now, and the
per-device delivery rule sharpens that from "this account is online" to "these
four devices are". SIP-4's beacon is careful to withhold liveness from callers
who should not have it, and SIP-21 refused presence outright as a disclosure with
its own threat model. A call publishes a moment of it to the exchange, and to the
caller, every time somebody rings. That is the cost of the mechanism and it
cannot be avoided while a phone rings on demand.

**A caller can ring a channel repeatedly.** There is no rate limit specific to
calls here; a `Call` is an entry and is bounded by SIP-16's per-identity post
limits, which bound the volume and not the nuisance. SIP-16 makes the same
observation about posting and reaches the same answer — an admin can remove the
offender — and SIP-21's block list is the individual remedy, since `0x09` is
subject to blocking like every other event. An exchange SHOULD rate-limit
`Call` bodies per identity per channel more tightly than ordinary posts, because
the cost of one is somebody's phone ringing rather than a line of text.

**Signals remain forgeable and this document leans on that being understood.** An
exchange can synthesise a `CallState` naming any member and any device, so it can
make a client show that somebody declined, or silence a ringing device by
claiming a sibling accepted. Neither reaches the log. The defence is the rule
stated in the specification — nothing durable is derived from a signal — and an
implementation that violated it would let the exchange write call history.

**The media is unchanged and so are its properties.** SIP-12's three-term
agreement means the exchange cannot read the call and cannot stand in the middle;
SIP-13 means it cannot join the room or forge a member. What it learns is what it
always learned: who is talking to whom, when, for how long, and how many bytes
each way. This document adds one thing to that set — it is now legible as a
*call* rather than as a session between two identities, because the invitation
that preceded it is an entry the exchange carried.

**There is no echo cancellation and no floor on what the media reveals.** Out of
scope here, noted because a call started from a chat is likelier to be answered
on a speaker than one started from a demo.

## Reference implementation

None yet.

The media half exists: `sqex-voice` implements SIP-12 sessions, SIP-13 rooms and
SIP-15 framing, including the room mesh. What is unimplemented is the signalling
in this document and its client-side behaviour in `sqex-chat`.
