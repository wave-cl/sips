## Abstract

An exchange signs, under the SIP-9 key its clients already pin, that it placed
a given entry at a given position in a given channel at a given time. The
signature commits to a **running head** — a hash chain over every entry the
channel has carried — so one receipt vouches for all the history behind it
rather than only for its own entry. Two receipts naming one position with
different heads are a portable, self-contained proof that the exchange told two
parties different things. This turns SIP-31's equivocation from *detectable by
comparison* into *attributable by arithmetic*, and gives `posted` the signature
it has never had.

## Motivation

SIP-31 designed this and declined it, in as many words:

> The second form, an exchange receipt over `(channel, seq, posted, entry hash)`
> signed with its SIP-9 key, is a better idea and was declined only for this
> version… It is the natural content of a later SIP, and nothing in this wire
> would have to change to accept it.

This is that SIP. Three things in the existing set are waiting for it.

**`posted` is unsigned, and a revocation needs a time.** SIP-31's security
section states the problem and cannot solve it: deciding whether a device's
credential was valid *when it signed* requires a time, and the only time
available is the origin's unsigned word. SIP-32 then made revocation a signed
artifact with a time of its own, which sharpens the asymmetry rather than
resolving it — one side of the comparison is signed and the other is an
assertion. A signed `posted` completes it.

**Equivocation is detectable, not attributable.** SIP-31 says so plainly: an
origin can present one log to one party and a different log to another, every
entry in both validly signed, both chains internally consistent, and only
comparing what two parties hold reveals it. Comparison is not a mechanism. It
requires the two parties to meet, to trust each other, and to agree afterwards
about who saw what — and at the end of it neither can show a third party
anything.

**Replication has no order to verify.** SIP-31 is explicit that the interleaving
of two devices' entries "is a choice made by one party with no way to prove it
was made honestly", and this repository's account of SIP-32 closes by naming
ordering as one of four things still blocking a channel from replicating. A replica cannot verify an order
nobody signed. It can only repeat it.

### Why the exchange is the right signer, having spent the whole set avoiding it

Every other document here works to keep the exchange out of the trust model, so
adding its signature deserves an argument rather than a shrug.

The exchange is already the sole authority on the three facts a receipt carries.
It assigns `seq`. It stamps `posted`. It decides what it serves. SIP-16 says the
author of an entry is "the exchange's observation of the connection that posted"
and SIP-31 spent a whole document removing that dependence — but it removed it
for **authorship**, which is a claim about somebody else, and left **ordering**
where it was, because ordering is a claim about the exchange's own act.

A signature over one's own act does not add trust. It removes deniability. That
is the entire mechanism here: nothing an exchange can say with a receipt was
unavailable to it before, and everything it says becomes something it cannot
later un-say.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian.

```
RECEIPT_CONTEXT = "sqex-receipt-v1"   (ASCII, 15 bytes, no terminator)
```

### The entry hash

```
entry_hash = SHA-256( the entry's SIP-31 signing input )
```

That is `entry_input` for a member entry and `action_input` for a system entry,
each as SIP-31 defines it, hashed once more. A verifier recomputes it from the
entry's own fields and needs no key of any kind, which preserves SIP-31's
property that a party who cannot read a channel can still verify its structure.

Committing to the signing input rather than to the entry as served is what makes
a receipt survive redaction. SIP-31's signature commits to `SHA-256(body)`, so
the signing input is fully recomputable after the body is gone; a receipt
inherits that unchanged, and a tombstone's receipt still verifies. Committing to
the served bytes instead would have forced the same bad choice SIP-31 names —
discard the receipt on redaction, making every deleted message look forged, or
refuse to redact.

### The running head

```
head_0 = 32 zero bytes
head_n = SHA-256( head_{n-1} || entry_hash_n )
```

`n` runs over the channel's entries in the order the exchange numbered them,
starting at the channel's first `seq`. An exchange MUST maintain one head per
channel, MUST advance it exactly once per entry as it is numbered, and MUST
**never** recompute it from the entries it currently holds.

That last rule is the same one SIP-31 gives for its per-device chain mark and it
fails the same way when ignored: pruning removes entries, so a head recomputed
from a pruned log is a different head, and every receipt issued before the prune
stops verifying against every receipt issued after. **The head is a property of
the channel's history, not of its current contents.** An exchange that loses the
head loses the ability to issue verifiable receipts for that channel and MUST
stop issuing them rather than starting a fresh chain, which would be
indistinguishable on the wire from equivocation.

### The receipt

```
signing_input = RECEIPT_CONTEXT || SHA-256( exchange[32] || instance[32]
                                            || channel[32] || seq(8)
                                            || posted(8) || entry_hash[32]
                                            || head[32] )

receipt = Ed25519(exchange_secret, signing_input)
```

`exchange` is the exchange's own Ed25519 identity — the key a client pins under
SIP-9 and, since SIP-33, may have discovered over DNSSEC. It is redundant with
the key the signature verifies under and is bound anyway, following SIP-31's
belt-and-braces treatment of `account`.

`instance` is SIP-31's per-incarnation value, and carries SIP-31's reason
unchanged: a direct message's identifier is derived from its two accounts, so
one conversation has byte-identical channel bytes on every exchange in
existence and across every recreation. Without both terms a receipt lifts from
one exchange into another's copy of the same conversation, or from a destroyed
channel into its successor, and verifies there.

The signer is the exchange's SIP-9 identity and **MUST NOT** be a separate key.
A second key would need its own distribution, its own pinning, and its own
rotation story, and SIP-33 has just finished building exactly one of those. The
key a client pins to talk to an exchange is the key that vouches for what the
exchange said.

### Wire

Three additions to SIP-16, all appended so that a decoder reading the previous
layout stops cleanly at a short read rather than misinterpreting a field:

```
Entry    | … | body_hash[32] | sig[64] | head[32] | receipt[64] | len: u32 | body |

Posted   | seq: u64 | posted: u64 | now: u64 | head[32] | receipt[64] |

Entries  | now: u64 | first: u64 | last: u64 | count: u16 | count × Entry
         | signals: u16 | signals × Signalled
         | tip_seq: u64 | tip_head[32] | tip_receipt[64] |
```

`head` is carried per entry rather than recomputed because a reader that begins
mid-log — after a prune, or with `since` above `first` — holds nothing to
recompute from, and the head is an input to the signature it must check. Thirty-
two bytes per entry buys verification of a single entry in isolation.

The tip fields on `Entries` are the head and receipt of the channel's `last`,
whether or not `last` appears in the batch. **This is what a reader who fetched
nothing still learns**, and it is the field that makes withholding visible: a
reader whose tip advances while its entries do not knows the exchange is
carrying something it is not serving, which before this SIP was indistinguishable
from an idle channel.

`tip_seq` MAY be `0`, meaning the channel has no entries and there is no tip to
sign. `tip_head` and `tip_receipt` are then 32 and 64 zero bytes.

### Verifying a receipt

A verifier, given an entry, the channel's `instance`, and the exchange identity
**it pinned**, MUST perform these checks and MUST stop at the first failure:

1. **Key.** The receipt is verified under the key the verifier pinned for this
   exchange — SIP-9's pinned key, or SIP-33's. A verifier MUST NOT take the
   signing key from the response, from a field, or from the connection alone.
2. **Signature.** Ed25519-verify `receipt` over `signing_input`, recomputed from
   the entry's own fields and the verifier's own `exchange` and `instance`.
3. **Linkage**, where the verifier holds the entry at `seq - 1` for this
   channel: `head` MUST equal `SHA-256( head_{seq-1} || entry_hash )`.

Step 3 is conditional and its absence is ordinary — see *A gap is not a
divergence*. Steps 1 and 2 are not conditional: a receipt that is present and
fails either is **repudiated**, and a client MUST surface it exactly as SIP-31
surfaces a forged entry. It is not a missing receipt.

### Two receipts that cannot both be true

```
Equivocation | exchange[32] | instance[32] | channel[32] | seq: u64
             | posted_a: u64 | entry_hash_a[32] | head_a[32] | receipt_a[64]
             | posted_b: u64 | entry_hash_b[32] | head_b[32] | receipt_b[64] |
```

A proof of equivocation is two receipts that verify under one exchange key,
naming one `(exchange, instance, channel, seq)`, and differing in any of
`posted`, `entry_hash` or `head`. It is 376 bytes, self-contained, and needs
neither party's cooperation to check: a third party holding only the exchange's
public key can verify both signatures and observe the contradiction.

An implementation that holds one MUST be able to serialise it in this form. SIP-35
defines what a replica does with one; a client SHOULD retain it and SHOULD make
it exportable, because the value of the artifact is that it travels.

**The running head is what makes this cover omission.** With a receipt over the
entry alone, two receipts conflict only when they name the same `seq` with
different content — so an exchange could show one reader a log containing entry
5 and another a log that skips from 4 to 6, and no pair of receipts would
contradict, because the two readers hold receipts for different positions.
Because `head_n` commits to every `entry_hash` before it, the two readers'
receipts at any later position disagree, and one comparison anywhere after the
divergence proves it. The cost is 32 bytes of exchange state per channel and one
SHA-256 per post.

### A gap is not a divergence

Two failures of step 3 look alike and mean different things, exactly as SIP-31's
gap and fork do.

- A **gap** — the verifier does not hold `seq - 1` — is produced by pruning, by
  retention, by `expires_after`, and by a member joining a channel with history
  it was not given. It is ordinary. A client SHOULD show that it cannot verify
  continuity across the gap and MUST NOT present it as misconduct.
- A **divergence** — the verifier holds `seq - 1` and the linkage fails — cannot
  occur without the exchange having advanced its head over an entry this verifier
  was not shown. It is evidence, and a client MUST surface it.

A verifier holding a contiguous run therefore verifies the run's internal
linkage and the endpoints' signatures, and needs nothing else. A verifier
holding fragments verifies each fragment and reports the gaps between them.
**A receipt chain is verifiable from any contiguous run plus the head of its
first entry**, which is the property that lets it survive a retention window
shorter than the conversation.

### Limits

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| Added per entry (`head` + `receipt`) | 96 bytes |
| Added per `Posted` | 96 bytes |
| Added per `Entries` (tip) | 104 bytes |
| Exchange state per channel | 32 bytes |
| `Equivocation` | 376 bytes |

At SIP-16's `MAX_BATCH` of 64 entries this adds 6 KiB to a fetch bounded at
512 KiB, and at its 50 000 entries per channel about 4.8 MB against a 128 MiB
cap. Combined with SIP-31's 136 bytes an entry now carries 232 bytes of overhead,
which at the 50 000-entry limit is 11.6 MB — under a tenth of the cap, and the
cap binds on bodies long before it binds on headers. **No limit in SIP-16,
SIP-31 or SIP-32 needs revisiting**, which is the check SIP-32 failed when it had
to halve `MAX_ENVELOPES`, and is therefore worth stating rather than assuming.

One Ed25519 signature per accepted entry is the real cost, and it falls on the
exchange at post time. It is comparable to the verification the exchange already
performs on every SIP-31 entry it accepts.

## Rationale

**Why a running head rather than SIP-31's entry hash.** Argued above: the entry
hash alone cannot catch omission, because two readers shown different subsets
hold receipts for different positions and never contradict. The head makes every
receipt a commitment to all of history, at 32 bytes.

**Why a hash chain rather than a Merkle tree.** A tree would additionally let the
exchange prove *inclusion* of an old entry against a recent head in log space,
which is what a transparency log does and is genuinely better for a reader
auditing a large history. It was rejected for this version on the grounds that
it buys a property nothing here consumes: SIP-16 fetches contiguous ranges from a
cursor, not scattered entries, so a reader following a channel already holds the
run. A tree also requires the exchange to retain interior nodes for pruned
entries, turning "32 bytes per channel" into a structure proportional to history
— which is precisely the cost that made a per-channel chain unavailable to SIP-31
for a different reason. If a later document wants inclusion proofs, the head
defined here is the natural leaf accumulator to replace, and the receipt's
signing input would not otherwise change.

**Why the exchange's existing SIP-9 key.** A dedicated signing key is better
hygiene in the abstract — it could live offline, and compromise of the transport
key would not forge history. But it cannot live offline, because it signs once
per post; so the alternative is a second online key, with a second distribution
problem, that must itself be vouched for by the first. That is a longer chain
with no shorter root.

**Why `posted` and not a client-asserted time.** SIP-31 rejected a client
`sent` for a reason this document must not undo: two times for one message that
two clients may display differently is a worse failure than one time nobody
signs. This signs the one time there already is, and adds no second clock.

**Why receipts are per entry rather than per fetch.** A receipt over "what I
served you just now" would be cheaper — one signature per response instead of one
per entry — and is what a reader auditing its own view actually needs. It was
rejected because such a receipt is not transferable: it commits to a batch
assembled for one caller, so a third party holding an entry lifted out of it can
verify nothing. Transferability is the whole point, and SIP-31's argument
applies unchanged: an attestation repeated to third parties who never saw the
connection must carry its own proof.

**Why the tip is served even when the batch is empty.** Without it, a reader that
fetches and receives nothing learns nothing, and "nothing happened" is
indistinguishable from "I am not being shown what happened". The tip costs 104
bytes on a response that was otherwise empty and converts silence into a signed
claim of silence.

**Why not sign the roster, the retention policy, or the epoch.** All three are
things the exchange asserts and all three would benefit. SIP-32 has already
signed the membership actions that change a roster and the constitution that
founds a channel, so the exchange's *summary* is checkable against signed events
a reader can obtain. Extending receipts to cover summaries would duplicate that
at a second layer. Where SIP-32 left something genuinely unsigned — a blocking
decision, a read cursor — it did so deliberately, and this document does not
reopen those.

## Compatibility

Purely additive on the wire, and additive in the weaker direction too: three
existing structures gain trailing fields.

**An exchange that does not implement this SIP omits the fields.** A client MUST
NOT treat their absence as failure, MUST NOT refuse to display an entry that
arrives without a receipt, and MUST NOT report it as forged. Absence means the
exchange makes no claim.

**A client MUST NOT treat a present-but-invalid receipt as absence.** This is the
asymmetry the whole compatibility story turns on, and an implementation that
gets it backwards has built a mechanism an exchange can disable by corrupting
its own signatures. Present and invalid is *repudiated* and is surfaced; absent
is *unclaimed* and is not.

A client that does not implement this SIP ignores the trailing fields, which
requires that it decode `Entry` by its declared lengths rather than to the end of
the buffer. SIP-31 already required a distinct `Post` type byte for the same
class of hazard in the other direction; no such change is needed here, because
every field added is at the end of a structure whose earlier fields are
fixed-width and whose final `len`-prefixed body is unaffected.

Both implementations need not move together. An exchange may serve receipts to
clients that ignore them, and a client may ask for them from exchanges that do
not send them.

## Security considerations

**What this buys.** The exchange can no longer say two different things about
one position without leaving a 376-byte artifact that proves it to anybody. It
can no longer withhold an entry from one reader while serving it to another
without the two readers' tips diverging. And `posted` becomes a claim its author
has signed, which is what SIP-31's revocation-dating problem was missing.

**What it does not buy, and this is the larger half.**

*Receipts do not stop withholding — they stop it being deniable, and only once
the entry has been numbered.* An exchange that refuses a `Post` outright, or
accepts it and never advances its head, has issued no receipt and contradicted
nothing. **The mechanism covers what the exchange said, never what it declined to
say.** A poster does learn its own entry was numbered, because `Posted` now
carries a receipt — so silent acceptance-and-discard is closed — but a reader
cannot distinguish a channel where nothing was said from one where nothing was
accepted.

*A receipt says nothing about who wrote the entry.* That is SIP-31's job, and the
two must both be checked. A verifier that checks a receipt and skips SIP-31's
steps has confirmed that an exchange carried something, and learned nothing about
who wrote it. The reverse omission is the more likely one and SIP-31 warns about
it in the same terms.

*Detection still requires two views to meet.* The proof is portable where a
comparison was not, and that is a real improvement, but somebody must still hold
both receipts. A single client with a single view of a dishonest exchange sees a
perfectly consistent log. Nothing here is unilateral.

*The exchange can equivocate about channels, not only within them.* Two
`instance` values for what members believe is one channel produces two internally
consistent histories that never contradict, because `instance` is bound into
every receipt. SIP-31 requires `instance` to be reported in `Created` and
`Channel`, so members can compare it — and comparing it is now worth doing.
An exchange that hands two members different `instance` values for one channel
has partitioned them, and no receipt will say so.

**A new disclosure: the receipt makes the operator's carriage provable.** Before
this, a leaked log proved that some member could have forged all of it (before
SIP-31) or that a named device signed it (after). Now it additionally proves that
a named exchange, at a named key, carried a named message at a named time. That
is a transferable, non-repudiable statement about an operator, obtainable from
any member. SIP-31 gave up deniability between members and said so; this gives
up the operator's, and says so. An operator who does not want that property
should not implement this SIP, and the fields being optional is deliberate.

**Head loss is indistinguishable from equivocation.** An exchange that restarts
without its head and begins a fresh chain issues receipts that fail linkage
against every receipt from before, which reads exactly like divergence. This is
why the head MUST be persisted with the entries and why an exchange that has lost
it MUST stop issuing receipts rather than resume. The failure is not
theoretical: SIP-16's channels are the only durable state in this stack, and
SIP-31's per-device chain marks already have to survive a restart for the same
reason.

**Clock trust is unchanged.** Signing `posted` makes it attributable, not
accurate. An exchange can sign any time it likes, and a verifier dating a
revocation against a receipt is trusting the exchange's clock exactly as far as
before — the difference is that a lie is now permanent and comparable across
readers, rather than merely asserted.

## Reference implementation

None yet.

The natural place is `sqex-proto::channel` beside SIP-31's entry signing, with
the head maintained in `sqexd`'s channel store next to the per-device chain
marks it already persists for SIP-31 — the two have the same durability
requirement and the same failure mode when lost.
