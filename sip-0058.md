## Abstract

An account whose key lives on a hardware token cannot be a transport
identity (SIP-3) and so, on an exchange that whitelists transport keys
(SIP-8), cannot connect at all. It can sign. This document lets it do
everything by signing: a SIP-20 credential naming a software device,
issued offline by the token; an exchange administrator submitting that
credential over the signed administration protocol, so the device is
registered (SIP-22) and admitted (SIP-47) without the account ever
connecting; and a SIP-32 revocation submitted the same way. From there
the device acts for the account -- in a channel, as an admin -- with
every act a signed entry every copy derives, which is what a row edited
by hand at one exchange could never be.

## Motivation

The first channel on the federation has an admin whose key is a YubiKey.
The exchange the channel lives on has no whitelist, and the admin acts
there; the copy at squic.org, which does, has never seen a signed
promotion from that key and never will, since the key cannot connect to
sign one. Two further admins were added by editing the origin's database,
and the copy does not know them either: it derives its roster from the
log (SIP-35), and the log has nothing to say. Nothing in the stack was
wrong, and the result was a channel whose authority was invisible from
one of its homes.

Everything needed already exists in pieces. SIP-20 makes a credential a
self-contained, offline-verifiable grant; SIP-22 registers one presented
by the delegate or by a sibling; SIP-47 admits a registered device
because its account is listed; SIP-32 keeps the credential so a copy can
bind the device's signature to the account. What is missing is the one
step a hardware-held account cannot take -- presenting the credential to
an exchange it cannot reach -- and the answer is the person who *can*
reach it and already acts on the operator's behalf: an administrator,
over the protocol that exists for exactly such signed, audited acts.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian.

### Issuing offline

A SIP-20 credential is signed by the account; SIP-20 says nothing about
where the key is. An implementation MUST be able to issue one with any
Ed25519 signer, including a hardware token that signs a message and
never yields the key, and MUST be able to do so with no exchange in
reach: the credential is `account`, `delegate`, `scope`, `issued`,
`not_after` and a signature, and needs nothing an exchange knows. The
same holds for a SIP-32 `Revocation`.

The device named is an ordinary software identity (SIP-11), generated
where it will run. Its key is given to the token's holder out of band --
shown, typed, scanned, as SIP-47 shows a phone's -- and the credential
comes back the same way, in base58.

### Registering by an administrator

Two operations join the administration protocol (SIP-6/9), signed by an
exchange administrator like every other:

```
DeviceRegister | tag: u8 = 0x12 | credential (SIP-20, self-contained) |
DeviceRevoke   | tag: u8 = 0x13 | revocation (SIP-32, self-contained) |
```

`DeviceRegister` registers `credential.delegate` as a device of
`credential.account` exactly as SIP-22's `Register` does when the delegate
presents it itself: verified for scope `sqex-chat` now, refused if the
device is already another account's, idempotent for one already mapped,
and retained (SIP-32). The administrator is not the account and is not
checked against it: the credential is the account's signed word, the
administrator is the courier, and the audit log records which
administrator carried it. An exchange whose transport is whitelisted
admits the device from then on under SIP-47's rule, because the account
is listed; an exchange that does not list the account registers the
device and admits nothing, which is what listing is for.

`DeviceRevoke` applies `revocation` as SIP-32 has the account apply it:
signed by the account, exempt from SIP-22's seniority rule, removing the
device and its admission. A device-initiated revoke (SIP-22's, by a
sibling) is unchanged and not this.

Neither operation is a way past the whitelist for an account that is
not listed, nor a way for an administrator to name a device the account
did not: the signature is the account's, and an administrator can
register only what the account signed.

### Acting

Registered and admitted, the device connects as itself and acts for the
account: it posts, invites, promotes, mutes, replicates, rehomes. Every
such act is a SIP-31 action signed by the device, with `actor` the
account and `actor_device` the device, verified by the exchange against
the credential it holds and by every copy against the credential the
origin serves (SIP-32). A promotion made this way is an entry in the
log, and a copy derives the admin from it.

A channel whose roles were set by hand -- a row written at the origin
with no entry behind it -- is repaired by the account's device
re-making the grants as entries: promoting the same accounts again. The
origin, which already had the rows, writes the entries; the copies, which
had nothing, derive them.

### Limits

None beyond SIP-20's and SIP-22's.

## Rationale

**Why an administrator and not a new door.** The whitelist is on the
transport: an unlisted key is dropped before the first byte of any
route, which is the property that makes it worth having, and any door
cut into it for "just this credential" would be a route reachable by
strangers. The administration protocol is already the way signed
decisions reach a closed exchange, it is already audited, and an
administrator carrying a credential is doing what an administrator does
-- vouching for nothing, since the credential vouches for itself.

**Why the account's signature and not the administrator's.** An
administrator who could register a device for any account could act as
any account. The operation carries the account's own signed grant, and
the exchange verifies it exactly as it would from the delegate; the
administrator's signature is on the transaction, not on the grant.

**Why offline issuance is a requirement and not a note.** A token that
can only sign inside a session with the exchange is a token that cannot
help an account the exchange will not talk to. The credential's whole
design is that it needs no session, and an implementation that made one
necessary would have lost that.

**Why not derive an X25519 key from the token.** SIP-8 admits the X25519
image of an Ed25519 key, and a token that never yields its Ed25519
secret cannot produce it. A second, software-held X25519 key for the
transport would be a second identity bound to the first by nothing but
configuration -- which is a credential with the verifiability taken out.

## Compatibility

Additive: two administration operations and a requirement on clients
that hold signers. An exchange from before this document refuses the
operations as unknown tags; an administrator falls back to listing the
device by hand (`WhitelistAdd`) and the device registers itself, which is
what SIP-22 allowed and SIP-47 was written to make unnecessary.

## Security considerations

**The device is the account, for what it is scoped to.** That is SIP-20's
and SIP-22's trade, unchanged: a software key on a machine, revocable by
the token that made it. A person who holds their key on a token to keep
it off machines has now put a delegate on one, and should know it -- the
delegate signs for the account in every channel, and everything it does
is the account's until revoked.

**An administrator learns what the account signed.** A credential is
public by construction (SIP-20); an administrator carrying it learns the
account has a device, which the exchange's device list would have shown
them anyway.

**A revocation cannot be withheld by the courier's absence for long.**
The account can hand a revocation to any administrator, or to any of its
other devices (SIP-22), or connect from a listed device of its own. An
account whose only route to the exchange was one administrator has one
administrator's availability as its revocation latency, which it should
weigh when choosing how many people can carry for it.

## Reference implementation

Planned: `sqex-proto` issues credentials and revocations with any
`Signer`; `sqex device link <key> [--days] [--yubikey]` prints a
credential with no connection; `sqex device revoke <key> [--yubikey]`
prints a revocation; `sqex admin device register <credential>` and
`sqex admin device revoke <revocation>`; `sqexd` applies both and admits
the device under SIP-47.
