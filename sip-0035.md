## Abstract

An exchange serves a channel it did not originate. It pulls entries from the
origin, verifies every one under SIP-31's signatures, SIP-20's credentials and
SIP-34's receipts, and refuses to store anything that fails — so a replica is a
**witnessed copy** rather than a cache, and a party reading from one checks the
origin's signatures, not the replica's word. Records that have a winner —
profiles, credentials, revocations — replicate whole and highest-serial-wins, as
`sqns` has been doing between servers for a year. Logs replicate only as far as
their receipts carry them. A channel has exactly one origin, and nothing here
moves it.

A replica may **wait** on its origin instead of polling: one held
request per origin, naming the channels it holds and where each stands,
answered the moment any of them changes -- SIP-16's long poll between
exchanges, in the pulling direction only. And an exchange may peer
**open**: serve the peering routes to any identified caller and grant
nothing by it, since what a peer may pull, carry or be told is exactly
what a member's signed statement entitles it to; the writes a stranger
can cause are rate-limited per caller and the requests it can hold open
are capped.

## Motivation

This repository's own account of SIP-32 closes by naming what still blocks a
channel from replicating:

> what still blocks replicating one — ordering across devices, equivocation,
> retention divergence, and the fact that two exchanges have no way to peer at
> all — is the next proposal's problem, not this one's.

This is that proposal. Three of the four are now answerable, one because SIP-34
answered it and two because they were always questions of what to *state* rather
than what to solve.

The reason to want it is availability. Everything in this stack is arranged so
that an exchange cannot read what it carries, and nothing is arranged so that it
cannot stop carrying it. SIP-12 says the exchange "can deny, delay and reorder";
SIP-16 says "retention is a promise, not a property"; SIP-9's security section
observes that a deployment's recovery procedure is the real bound on how fast a
compromised exchange can be replaced. A conversation whose only copy sits at one
operator is one seizure, one bankruptcy or one disk from gone, and no amount of
end-to-end sealing changes that.

**What makes this possible now is SIP-31, not SIP-34.** Before entries were
signed, a replica could not enforce membership: the roster was the exchange's
word, so a second exchange serving a private channel would have been asserting
an access rule it had no evidence for. SIP-31 and SIP-32 made membership a
sequence of signed actions and a signed constitution. A replica can now
*derive* who may read a channel from the log itself, which is the difference
between replicating a conversation and being told about one.

SIP-34 supplies the other half: an order that is signed rather than asserted, so
a replica repeats a commitment instead of a claim.

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-61.* SIP-43 made a copy a place to write, SIP-59 made it a person's home, and
SIP-60 put the first message of a conversation there. Between them the
copy became where people read, and it reads late. A post *through* a
copy is pulled back at once, because the replica knows it forwarded
something; a post made at the origin by the other party is news the
replica has no way to hear until its interval comes round. Two people
on two exchanges talk with one side's replies arriving in a burst every
half minute, which is not a conversation.

The origin could push. It would need to reach every peer holding a
copy, and a replica is by construction the side that reaches out: it
is admitted at the origin, it holds the connection, it may be behind a
NAT or a whitelist the origin is not on. SIP-16 faced the same shape
between a client and its exchange and chose a held request over a push
or a fast poll, for reasons it states; they hold here unchanged, with
one more -- a replica holding copies from many origins holds one
request per origin, whatever the number of channels.

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-63.* The first live run of SIP-60 failed on a line of configuration. Two
people at two exchanges, one homed at each, opened a direct message; the
origin told the home, as the document says, and the home refused the
call -- its operator had never listed the origin as a peer, because until
that moment there had been no reason to. The fix was an operator editing
a file and restarting a service, for a conversation between two people
who had each already said, in signed statements, where they lived and
whom they were talking to.

That is the shape of every remaining cross-exchange step. A person moves
home (SIP-59): the old exchange serves the new one only if listed. A
person is put in a channel at an exchange their home never heard of
(SIP-60): the home learns of it only if that exchange is listed. A person
looks somebody up by name at a domain (SIP-60): only if the domain is a
listed peer. Each list is the operator's, each must be kept on both ends,
and none of them decides anything the members have not already decided:
this document says in as many words that the disclosure is the members'
and the whitelist is not what authorises a copy. The list survived
because §Peering also said an origin MUST hold one -- a rule written for a federation of a
few known exchanges, before anyone could move between them.

The rest of the stack has already answered the question. An exchange
admits clients it never heard of and lets the log say what each may
read; SIP-56 gave an open exchange the three controls that makes
necessary. Peering is the same question at the same door: an exchange is
an identity like any other (§Peering), and an exchange that admits any
identity has no reason to admit an exchange's differently, provided the
door still opens on nothing.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian.

```
PEER_CONTEXT = "sqex-peer-v1"   (ASCII, 12 bytes, no terminator)
```

### Terms

- **Origin** — the exchange that created a channel, assigns its `seq` and
  `posted`, holds its SIP-34 head, and is the only exchange that accepts posts to
  it.
- **Replica** — an exchange serving a copy it pulled. Read-only for that channel,
  forever.

**One origin per channel.** Explicitly out of scope, and stated here so no
implementation invents them: a channel originated jointly by two exchanges,
membership spanning exchanges, a channel whose origin moves, and any merge of
two histories. A channel that must change origin is a new channel, and the
members create one.

### What replicates

| Artifact | Replicates | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Channel entries (SIP-16, SIP-31) | Yes | Signed, ordered, receipted |
| Membership actions (SIP-31, SIP-32) | Yes | Signed by the actor |
| The constitution (SIP-32 `0x09`, `0x0a`) | Yes | Signed, and founds the channel |
| Channel key envelopes (SIP-17, SIP-32) | Yes | Signed by the publisher |
| Blobs (SIP-18) | Yes | Named by the hash of their ciphertext |
| Profiles (SIP-32) | Yes | Signed records, serial-ordered |
| Credentials and attested revocations (SIP-32) | Yes | Signed artifacts |
| Prekeys (SIP-23) | **No** | At-most-once cannot be held by two servers |
| Signals, read cursors (SIP-16) | **No** | Permanently the exchange's word |
| Block lists (SIP-21, SIP-32) | **No** | Deliberately unsigned; an access rule, not a claim |
| Local revocations (SIP-32) | **No** | Rest on `added` times only one exchange holds |
| Beacons (SIP-4), sessions (SIP-12), rooms (SIP-13) | **No** | Live coordination between connected peers |

Three of the refusals carry reasons worth stating rather than tabulating.

**Prekeys must not replicate, and this is the sharpest case in the table.**
SIP-23's entire value is that a prekey is served *once* and destroyed on use.
Two exchanges each holding the pool each serve the same prekey to a different
sender, both believing they are the only one, and the recipient's duplicate check
— SIP-23's own defence — fires on a condition that is now normal. An
implementation MUST NOT replicate a prekey pool under any consistency scheme; the
correct behaviour for a replica asked for a prekey is to refuse and name the
origin.

**Nothing forgeable replicates.** SIP-31 lists signals and read cursors as
permanently the exchange's word — a typing signal carries an account the exchange
asserted, a `read` cursor is an unsigned client claim it relays. Repeating either
across a peering link would turn one exchange's assertion into two, which reads
as corroboration and is not.

**Block lists stay put deliberately.** SIP-32 says why they are unsigned: a
signed block list would be a portable, non-repudiable statement about somebody a
person wants nothing to do with, which is the opposite of what it is for. Making
it replicate would require exactly that.

### Peering

An exchange is an identity like any other. A peering connection is an ordinary
sQUIC connection carrying a SIP-3 identity, where that identity is the peer's
SIP-9 key — the same key its clients pin and its receipts verify under. A replica
finds an origin by SIP-33 discovery or by configuration, and pins it. Nothing new
is needed to connect.

**Both ends gate the link operationally.** An origin MUST hold a list of the peer
keys it will serve replication to, and MUST answer every peering route
identically to a caller not on it — the same refusal whether the peer is unknown,
the channel does not exist, or the channel exists and is not replicated. This is
SIP-24's rule for its admission endpoint and SIP-4's for a withheld beacon,
applied here for the same reason: peering routes are reachable by strangers, and
a reply that varied would make them an existence oracle for private channels.

```
Hello   | type: u8 = 0x01 | version: u8 | since: u64 |
Hi      | now: u64 | version: u8 | exchange[32] | window_secs: u32 |
```

`POST /peer/hello` establishes nothing but agreement on a version and reports the
responder's own identity and retention window. It is not authentication —
the connection already did that.

### Open peering

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-63.* An exchange is configured to peer **listed** or **open**. Listed is
§Peering as first written: the peering routes are served to the keys the
operator lists and refused uniformly to everyone else. Open changes three
gates and nothing about entitlement:

1. **The peering routes** (`/peer/*`, and every route later documents
   added to them) are served to **any identified caller**. A
   caller that is not listed is a peer with no standing grant: it may
   pull, wait on, carry into or be told of a channel exactly where a
   member's signed statement entitles it -- an admin's `0x0b` naming
   its key (above), or the recorded home of a present member (SIP-59)
   -- and is refused uniformly everywhere else, as an unlisted key is
   refused today. A listed peer keeps what its listing grants (an
   operator's `for` list, §Peering) in addition. **Open peering entitles
   nobody to anything.** It removes the question of whether a caller
   may ask.

2. **A home hears from any origin.** `/peer/invited` (SIP-60) and
   `/peer/moved` (SIP-59) are accepted from any identified caller. Both
   were already harmless from a stranger: a Move is its own authority,
   and a hint is acted on only for an account whose Move names this
   exchange, by pulling from an origin that gates the pull itself.

3. **Locating goes anywhere.** `/account/locate` (SIP-60) at an open
   exchange reaches the exchange SIP-33 discovers for the domain,
   listed or not; the discovered key is the pin, as it is for a client.
   The home it follows is found the same way.

The reference configuration is `open_peering = true` (default: false,
listed). An exchange MUST report which it is at startup.

### What stays

**Calls are SIP-39's and SIP-65's.** Whether this exchange rings its users for a call
from another, or dials out to one, is the federation boundary SIP-39
draws and SIP-46 publishes, and open peering does not move it: a ring
costs a person's attention, a bridge costs the exchange a relay, and
SIP-56 has no limit for either yet. An open exchange federates calls
with its listed peers as before, and on a member's word (SIP-65).

**Uniform refusal.** Every peering route at an open exchange answers a
caller not entitled to what it asked exactly as it answered an unlisted
caller before: one refusal, empty, for an absent channel, a channel
not authorised to this caller, and an origin that cannot serve
(above). Nothing in open peering adds a reply that varies by whether
a channel or an account exists. `/peer/hello` answers anyone; what it
says -- the exchange's key, version and window -- is already published
(SIP-33, SIP-46).

**The transport whitelist.** An exchange that whitelists transport keys
(SIP-8) is closed at the door whatever its peering mode; open peering
at such an exchange means any *admitted* key. The two settings are
independent; what the whitelist admits for an account's home is
SIP-47's (§The phone is a device, the recorded home).

### What a stranger can cost

An open exchange serves reads to anyone entitled, and SIP-56's rule that
a limit is never a refusal to read holds for peers as for clients: a
pull, a shape, a standing, cursors, signals and tombstones are not
limited here. Two things a stranger can cause without
being entitled to anything are bounded:

**Writes a peer causes are limited per caller.** SIP-56's table gains
one row:

| What | Counted per | Reference default |
|---|---|---|
| `peering` | caller key | 60 per hour |

counted at `/peer/moved`, `/peer/invited`, `/peer/rehomed` (SIP-53),
and the registration a carried forward (SIP-59 `Carried`) performs for
a device the origin had not seen; the act inside a forward is counted
against the member as SIP-56 already says. A request over the limit is
answered `rate_limited` with the seconds to wait, as SIP-56 answers a
client. This is the one reply on a peering route that is not the
uniform refusal, and it may be: it is about the caller, and says nothing
of any channel or account. A listed peer is subject to it like any
other; an operator MAY set it to unlimited.

**Held requests are capped per caller.** An exchange MUST bound the
number of `/peer/wait` (§Waiting) requests it holds open for one caller
key at a time; the reference bound is `MAX_WAITS_PER_PEER = 8`. A wait
past the bound is answered with the uniform refusal, which a replica
already reads as "this origin does not wait" and falls back from to
polling. A replica following §Waiting holds one wait per origin and
never meets the bound.

### Reporting

`Moved.peered` (SIP-59) at an open exchange is `1` for any home: a pull
from there will be served. At a listed exchange it is as SIP-59 says.

### A channel replicates only when a member says so

An operational whitelist decides which peers an origin will talk to at all. It
does not decide which channels replicate, because the disclosure belongs to the
members rather than to the operator.

**That is the rule for a replica that serves other people's clients**, which is
what the rest of this section specifies. There is a second case it does not
cover, and treating the two alike makes one of them needlessly hard.

A peer may instead be named with the **accounts it acts for** — a read replica
that answers only for its own operator's users, or a person's own exchange
syncing the conversations they are already in. Such a peer may pull the channels
those accounts are present members of, and needs no per-channel authorisation,
because **those accounts can already fetch every one of those entries as
clients**. The entries arriving on their own machine is the same disclosure in a
different place, not a new one. Requiring a channel admin's signature for it
would be asking somebody's correspondents for permission to keep their own
messages.

A read replica and a personal exchange are one rule at different scales, and an
implementation should not pretend otherwise: the enforcement is "may pull what
these accounts may read" in both cases, and a configuration that named them
differently would be drawing a distinction the code does not make.

**What this does not establish is that the peer really belongs to those
accounts.** That is the origin operator's assertion, and a private group's other
members did not agree to it — an operator could name any peer as acting for any
account and copy every channel that account is in. The origin operator already
holds all of that in the clear, so the marginal trust is small; it is not zero,
because the copy lands somewhere the operator does not control. **The stronger
form is an authorisation signed by the account itself**, the way `0x0b` is signed
by a channel admin, and it is the recommended upgrade for a deployment that will
not trust its operator this far.

Two events join SIP-16's system space, both carrying the actor's SIP-31 action:

| `event` | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0x0b | `actor` authorised replication of this channel to `subject` |
| 0x0c | `actor` withdrew that authorisation |

`subject` is the replica's Ed25519 exchange key. `arg` is empty. The actor MUST
hold the admin role, exactly as for `added` and `removed`.

An origin MUST NOT serve a channel's entries to a peer without a surviving `0x0b`
naming that peer, and MUST stop serving on `0x0c`.

**Authorisation is an entry, so the members see it.** It lands in the log they
already read, signed by whoever did it, receipted like anything else, and it
replicates with the channel — so a reader at a replica can see the authority
under which the replica holds what it is showing them. An out-of-band arrangement
between two operators would have been simpler and would have made a channel's
copies invisible to the people in it.

**Withdrawal does not delete anything.** A replica that receives `0x0c` MUST stop
accepting new entries and SHOULD stop serving, but the entries it holds were
lawfully obtained and no protocol can unsend them. An implementation MUST NOT
describe `0x0c` as revocation of a copy. It is the end of a subscription.

### Pulling

```
Pull    | type: u8 = 0x02 | channel[32] | since: u64 | max: u16 |
Pulled  | now: u64 | instance[32] | origin[32] | first: u64 | last: u64
        | window_secs: u32 | count: u16 | count × Entry
        | tip_seq: u64 | tip_head[32] | tip_receipt[64] |
```

`POST /peer/pull` is `/channel/fetch` for a peer: the same `Entry` layout,
including SIP-31's signature block and SIP-34's `head` and `receipt`, plus the
three things a member's fetch does not need because a member already knows them —
the channel's `instance`, the origin's key, and the origin's retention window.

`instance` is served unaltered. A replica MUST NOT mint one: SIP-31 binds it into
every signature, so a replica that generated its own would hold a channel whose
entries all fail verification.

Envelopes, blobs, profiles, credentials and revocations pull through parallel
routes — `POST /peer/{envelopes,blobs,records}` — each returning the artifact in
the shape its own SIP defines, with its signature intact. None needs a new
format, which is the point: SIP-32 made every one of them a self-contained signed
object precisely so that a copy-holder could check it.

Three details of those routes are not obvious until they are built:

- **An envelope must be served with the epoch its `Put` was made at.** SIP-17's
  signature binds that epoch, and a peer — unlike a member, who asked for a
  particular one — has no other way to know it. Without it a replica can hold an
  envelope it can never verify.
- **The origin lists a channel's blobs, because the replica cannot.** An
  attachment is named inside a SIP-19 body, and in a private channel that body is
  sealed, so a replica reading the whole log sees no blob identifiers at all. The
  exchange registers every attachment against its channel for quota and
  collection, so it knows the set without reading anything.
- **Credentials need no peering route.** SIP-32 made the registry serve them on
  an ordinary client route, so a replica answers SIP-31's step 2 with a request
  any caller could make — and verifies the SIP-20 artifact it gets back rather
  than trusting the mapping.

### Waiting

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-61.* ```
PeerWait | type: u8 = 0x11 | wait_secs: u16 | count: u16
         | count × (channel[32] | since: u64) |
Changed  | now: u64 | count: u16 | count × channel[32] |
```

`POST /peer/wait` is a peering route: gated as §Peering gates them and
refused uniformly like every other. `since` is the highest `seq`
the replica holds for that channel. The origin answers **at once** with
every listed channel the peer may pull (the entitlement above, SIP-59's
union) that has an entry past `since`; when there is none it holds the
request open for up to `wait_secs`, bounded by SIP-16's `MAX_WAIT`,
and answers as soon as any listed channel it may serve the peer gains
an entry, a signal, a read mark or a redaction -- everything a pull
would carry -- naming the channels that changed. On the wait running
out it answers with none. A channel the peer may not pull is left out
of every answer and is never waited on, so an answer says nothing
about it.

The origin MAY answer early at any time, and a replica MUST treat an
empty answer as ordinary. `count` is at most `MAX_WAIT_CHANNELS`; a
replica holding more from one origin waits on them in turns.

**What a replica does with it.** A replica holding copies from an origin keeps **one** wait open to it,
naming every channel it holds from there. On an answer it pulls the
channels named, as §Pulling and SIP-43 §Read marks at a replica have it
pull, and waits again. It still pulls everything on its interval (the
one above, SIP-59's `home_secs`), which becomes the safety net rather than the delivery:
a wait can be lost with the connection, an origin can answer early
with nothing, and a full pull costs what it always cost.

An origin from before §Waiting (0.78.0) refuses `/peer/wait` as an
unknown peering message; the replica notes it and pulls on its interval as it
did, trying the wait again after `WAIT_RETRY_SECS` in case the origin
was upgraded.

### What a replica MUST verify before storing

A replica performs, for every entry, the checks SIP-31 and SIP-34 define for a
verifier, and stores nothing that fails.

**An origin must therefore store its receipts rather than re-derive them**, and
so must a replica. Ed25519 is deterministic, so an origin re-signing an entry's
fields reproduces the same 64 bytes and could keep none — but a replica holds no
origin seed, so a receipt it did not keep is one it can never produce again, and
comparing two receipts for one position is exactly what detecting equivocation
consists of. An implementation that derived them would find nothing here.

The checks:

1. SIP-31 step 1 — the device's signature over the entry's own fields.
2. SIP-31 step 2 — a SIP-20 credential binding the device to the account, with
   scope `sqex-chat`. The credential is available because SIP-32 made the
   registry retain and serve it.
3. SIP-31 step 3 — the device's chain, where the replica holds the predecessor.
4. SIP-34 steps 1–3 — the receipt under the **origin's** pinned key, and the head
   linkage against the entry before it.

An entry failing 1, 2 or 4 MUST be refused and MUST NOT be stored. A failure of 3
that is a gap is stored; a failure that is a fork is stored **with** the
conflicting pair, because a fork is evidence and discarding it destroys the only
copy of it.

**A replica that skips these has built a cache, and a cache of somebody else's
assertions is worth less than nothing** — it launders one exchange's word into
two. The verification is the whole difference between this SIP and a mirror.

### Membership at a replica

A replica derives who may read a channel from the constitution event and the
signed membership actions in the log it holds, not from a roster the origin sent
it. It MUST apply SIP-16's visibility rules against that derived membership, and
MUST refuse a private channel's entries to an identity it cannot show a signed
`added` or `joined` for.

Where the replica's history is too short to derive membership — the origin
pruned, or replication began mid-channel — the replica MUST refuse the channel
rather than fall back on the origin's summary. Serving a private channel on the
strength of an unsigned roster is precisely the trust this document exists to
avoid, and the failure is safe in the right direction.

### Retention divergence

Two exchanges pruning on different schedules hold different subsets. This is
ordinary and permanent, and the rules are about honesty rather than agreement.

- A replica MUST report its own window in `Pulled` and to its own clients, and
  MUST NOT present its window as the origin's. It follows that a replica **holds
  on its own terms rather than adopting the origin's window as its own**: an
  implementation that copied that number into its local retention policy would
  prune in step with the origin and could never hold more, which the next rule
  explicitly permits.
- A replica MAY hold **more** than the origin — it pulled entries the origin has
  since pruned — and this is a feature, since outliving the origin's window is
  half the reason to replicate. It MUST NOT claim those entries are still
  available at the origin.
- A replica MUST NOT hold entries past `expires_after`. SIP-16 makes that a
  sender's instruction rather than an operator's policy, and it is the one
  retention rule that follows the entry rather than the channel.
- Gaps are reported as SIP-34 reports them: continuity unverifiable across the
  gap, not misconduct.

### Equivocation

A replica holding two receipts that verify under one origin key, naming one
`(exchange, instance, channel, seq)` and differing in `posted`, `entry_hash` or
`head`, holds SIP-34's `Equivocation`.

On detecting one it MUST:

1. Stop accepting further entries for that channel from that origin.
2. Retain both branches and the proof.
3. Serve the proof to any client that fetches the channel, and to any peer that
   pulls it. **Serving the proof and serving the channel are different acts**:
   the fetch itself is refused, because a replica that answered it would be
   presenting one of the two branches, and the proof goes out on a route of its
   own. A replica never serves `/peer/pull` for a channel it replicates — one
   origin per channel — so the peer half of this rule has no implementation and
   needs none.
4. **Not choose.** A replica has no basis to decide which branch is the real
   history, and picking one silently converts evidence into a disagreement
   between two honest-looking servers.

A client receiving an `Equivocation` for a channel MUST surface it and MUST NOT
present either branch as the conversation.

### What a replica refuses

A replica MUST refuse `POST /channel/{post,invite,remove,leave,retain,redact,
signal,cursor}` for a replicated channel, naming the origin's key in the refusal
so a client can discover and connect to it. It answers `/channel/{fetch,info,
mine}` normally.

**Writes go to the origin, always.** A replica accepting a post would be a second
origin, and everything above depends on there being one.

### Limits

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| `Pull` `max` | 256 entries |
| Peers per origin | 16 |
| Replicas per channel (surviving `0x0b`) | 4 |
| `PEER_MIN_INTERVAL` | 1 s |
| Pull response bytes | 1 MiB |

Four replicas per channel is a policy choice bounding fan-out and, more to the
point, bounding the metadata disclosure: each authorisation is another operator
who learns the channel's shape. An origin MAY lower it. Raising it is not free
and the reason is in Security considerations.

| `peering` limit (SIP-56) | 60 per hour per caller key |

## Rationale

**Why pull and not push.** A push protocol makes the origin responsible for a
replica's progress, needs retries and acknowledgements, and gives the origin a
way to flood a replica. Pull makes the replica responsible for its own state,
which is the party that cares. SIP-30's event stream is the natural way to make
pulling prompt without making it polling, and a replica MAY subscribe as a client
would; this document does not require it.

**Why the authorisation is a signed entry and not operator configuration.** Two
operators agreeing privately would have been simpler, and would have meant a
channel's members could not tell how many copies of their conversation existed.
The disclosure is theirs, so the decision is theirs, and putting it in the log
makes it visible without a new mechanism to make it visible.

**Why an admin rather than unanimity.** Unanimous consent is the honest answer to
"who may disclose this conversation's metadata" and was rejected as unworkable:
SIP-16 allows 256 members, membership changes constantly, and a rule requiring
every member to sign would make replication impossible to establish and trivial
to block. It is the same trade SIP-17 makes for channel keys, and it inherits the
same weakness — SIP-17's security section already names trust in an admin as the
design's weakest point, and this adds to what an admin can do.

**Why a replica verifies rather than trusting the origin it pinned.** Pinning
proves which exchange is speaking, not that what it says is true. The origin is
exactly the party a replica exists to be independent of; taking its word would
make the copy worthless in the case the copy is for.

**Why records replicate and logs only partly.** SIP-32 states the distinction and
this document is built on it: *a record replicates trustlessly because it has a
winner; a log does not, because it has an order.* `sqns` has replicated signed
records between servers for a year on exactly that basis — a peer that alters a
record breaks its signature, and a peer that replays an old one loses to the
higher serial already held. Nothing in that argument needed inventing here; it
needed only to be pointed at.

**Why not a consensus protocol.** Several exchanges agreeing on one order is a
solved problem with well-known costs, and it solves a problem this stack does not
have. There is no dispute about who assigns `seq` — the origin does, by
definition — so the question is not "what is the order" but "did the origin
commit to this one", which a signature answers at a fraction of the price. A
consensus protocol would additionally require exchanges to trust each other,
which is the property this whole set is arranged to avoid needing.

**Why a fork is stored rather than refused.** Every other verification failure
is refused, so storing this one is a deliberate exception. A fork is the only
failure that is *evidence about the origin* rather than evidence of a corrupt
transfer, and a replica that refuses it destroys the artifact and leaves nothing
to show. Refusing corrupt data is hygiene; refusing evidence is amnesia.

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-61:*

**Why a held request and not a push.** Stated in the motivation: the
replica is the side that reaches, and the request it holds rides the
connection it already has, admitted by the list that already admits it.
A push would need the origin to hold a peer list with addresses, or to
discover every replica, and to be admitted at each -- three things
§Peering was careful not to require of an origin.

**Why one wait per origin and not one per channel.** A home (SIP-59)
holds every channel its accounts are in; a person in forty
conversations across three exchanges is three held requests, not
forty. The origin's cost is the same either way -- a notifier per
channel it already keeps for its own clients' long polls.

**Why `since` rides along.** Without it the origin would have to
remember where each peer stands, which is state about peers that
§Pulling kept out of the origin on purpose; with it, an entry that landed
between the replica's last pull and this wait is answered at once, and
the wait never sleeps past something already there.

**Why the interval stays.** A held request is delivery, not
correctness: the log is what it is, and a pull on the interval reads
it whatever the waits did. Removing the interval would make the first
lost wait a silent gap; keeping it makes a lost wait a thirty-second
delay, which is where §Waiting started and is the worst case
rather than the ordinary one.

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-63:*

**Why a mode, and not the rule.** SIP-56 opens with the observation
that every exchange so far had been closed, and that the design had been
safe to be generous because of it. An exchange whose operator knows
every key it serves has no reason to serve peering to strangers, and a
list is the right shape for it; an exchange that admits anyone has no
reason to treat an exchange as more of a stranger than a person. Neither
is wrong, the operator knows which they run, and the default is the
closed one because a default that opened a door on upgrade would be the
kind of default SIP-47 warns of.

**Why the list stays.** A listed peer can be granted what no signed
statement grants -- an operator's `for` list for a read replica of the
operator's own users (§Peering). That is an operator's assertion about an
operator's own service and has no signed form; it remains in the
configuration where §Peering put it, and open peering adds to it rather
than replacing it.

**Why the entitlement does not change.** It could not, and saying so is
the point of open peering. The whitelist above was two things in one line
of configuration: whom the exchange talks to, and -- for a `for` list --
what it may have. Only the first was ever the operator's to relax, and
open peering relaxes only that. A stranger at an open exchange may pull
a channel where an admin signed for it or a member lives there, and a
member could always have fetched every entry of that channel as a client
and handed them to whom they pleased; the copy arriving at the stranger's
exchange is the member's own disclosure in a different place (above).
Nothing a stranger can ask for is anything a stranger could not already
have been given.

**Why hints and Moves from anyone.** A Move carried by `/peer/moved` is
checked as `/account/move` checks it, and `/account/move` was open to
any identified caller from the start, because the signature is the
authority and the carrier proves nothing (SIP-59). `/peer/invited` is a
hint that the home acts on by pulling from the origin it names, and the
origin gates that pull; a false hint costs the home one refused pull,
and the limit bounds how many. Gating either by a list protected
nothing, and the second is what failed live.

**Why locate goes anywhere.** A client may resolve a name at any
exchange SIP-33 finds, and does; `/account/locate` is the same lookup
made by the client's exchange on its behalf, and the peer-list gate on
it was inherited from SIP-39's call path, where it belongs. The
discovered key is the pin; an answer from the wrong key is discarded as
SIP-46 discards one.

**Why the wait cap refuses rather than answering empty.** An empty
`Changed` returned at once would send a well-behaved replica straight
back into another wait, which is a busy loop at the origin's expense.
The uniform refusal is what §Waiting already specifies for an origin that
does not wait, and a replica that meets it pulls on its interval and
tries again later, which is the behaviour wanted from a peer over its
share.

**Why calls stayed listed at first.** Everything open peering opens is a
copy: a
pull, a forward, a hint, a lookup. A call reaches a person. The reason a
member's word suffices for a copy -- they could have made it themselves
-- does not carry to making a phone ring in a room the caller was never
admitted to, and SIP-56's remedies for an open exchange do not yet have
a row for it. When they do, SIP-39's list can be opened the same way.

## Compatibility

Purely additive for clients. An exchange that implements this SIP gains routes
under `/peer/` and two system events; one that does not is unaffected and
interoperates unchanged, because nothing on the client-facing wire changes shape.

A client that does not implement this SIP ignores events `0x0b` and `0x0c` under
SIP-19's forward-compatibility rule, which is exactly what that rule is for. It
will not know that a channel is replicated. That is a disclosure it cannot act
on rather than a failure, and it is an argument for clients implementing at least
the display half.

**A client fetching from a replica MUST verify receipts under the origin's key,
not the connection's.** This is the trap in the whole document. SIP-9 pins the
server you connected to, and here that server is not the one that signed. The
origin's key arrives in `Pulled` and in the replica's `Channel` response, and a
client MUST obtain and pin it independently — by SIP-33 discovery, by
configuration, or by having connected to the origin before — and MUST NOT accept
it on the replica's say-so. A replica that supplies both the entries and the key
they verify under has been handed the forgery power this document spends its
length removing.

Both implementations need not move together. Replication is between exchanges;
neither implementation's client code is required to change at all.

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-61.* Additive: one peering message. A replica from before §Waiting (0.78.0)
pulls on its interval and nothing changes for it; an origin from before
it refuses the wait and the replica falls back. The two can be upgraded
in either order.

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-63.* An exchange from before §Open peering (0.80.0) is a listed exchange, and
the default keeps it one. A listed exchange behaves exactly as this
document first specified. A replica or home from before it talking to an
open
origin sees nothing new: its requests are served where a statement
entitles them, and refused as before elsewhere. The `rate_limited` reply
on a peering route is new; a replica from before answers it as it
answers any refusal on a best-effort route, by trying later, and a
`/peer/invited` or `/peer/moved` so refused is carried again on the
sender's next occasion. SIP-56's table and the `peered` flag of SIP-59
are amended as stated.

## Security considerations

**What this buys.** A conversation survives its origin. A reader can obtain it
from a party that never held the power to write it, and can check that what they
were given is what the origin signed.

**Replication is a metadata disclosure and cannot be anything else.** A replica
cannot read a private channel — bodies are sealed to members under SIP-17 and it
holds no key — but it learns everything around them: who is a member, when each
joined and left, who posted and when, how large each message was, and the whole
shape of the conversation over time. SIP-5 and SIP-12 each say the exchange's
metadata is "a social graph with timing" and that the result must not be
described as private communication. **Every replica is another operator holding
that graph.** This is why the count is capped, why the authorisation is signed
into the log where members can see it, and why an implementation MUST show
members that a channel is replicated and to whom.

**A replica cannot forge, and can do everything else an exchange can do.** It can
deny, delay, serve a stale subset, or claim a shorter window than it has. Every
one of those is available to the origin too, and the defence is the same: a
reader holding receipts knows what it has not been shown at the tip. What
replication adds is a second party who can deny, and a first who can now be
caught.

**An origin's equivocation becomes findable, which is most of the value.**
Detection under SIP-34 alone requires two clients to meet and compare. A replica
pulls continuously and compares by construction — it holds a receipt chain and
checks every new entry against it — so an origin that equivocates against a
replicated channel is contradicting a party whose whole function is to notice.
This is the strongest argument for replicating a channel one has no availability
concerns about.

**A hostile replica cannot be evicted.** `0x0c` ends a subscription; it does not
recall a copy. Authorising replication is irreversible in the only sense that
matters, and an implementation MUST present it that way — as publication to
another operator, not as a setting.

**A replica is a second place to compel.** The threat model that motivates this
SIP is an operator who is seized or coerced; the same model observes that
replication multiplies the number of operators who can be. For a channel whose
risk is disclosure rather than loss, replication is the wrong trade, and the
document's default — no replication without a signed act by an admin — is set
accordingly.

**Prekey replication would break forward secrecy silently.** Stated in the
specification and repeated here because it is the one entry in the table whose
violation produces no error: two exchanges serving one prekey pool each believe
they are enforcing at-most-once, the recipient's duplicate check fires against
honest senders, and SIP-23's guarantee degrades with nothing reporting it.

**Peering routes are an existence oracle if the refusals differ.** An origin that
answered "no such channel" differently from "not replicated to you" would let any
peer probe for private channels by identifier — and a direct message's identifier
is derived from two accounts, so those identifiers are guessable by anyone who
knows two people. Identical refusals are not a nicety here.

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-61.* **The gate is the same gate.** A wait names channels; the origin
answers about the ones it would pull to that peer and is silent about
the rest, in every answer including the empty one, so the route
discloses nothing a pull did not.

**A held request is a held stream.** One per replica per origin, bounded
by `MAX_WAIT` and by the peer list; an origin's client long polls are
already the same shape at larger numbers.

**What the origin learns.** Which channels a replica holds from it --
which it knew from the pulls -- and that the replica is up.

*Added 2026-09-17, was SIP-63.* **What an open exchange discloses.** `/peer/hello` to anyone: the key,
version and retention window, all published already. Nothing else
answers a stranger with more than the uniform refusal, and no reply
varies by the existence of a channel or an account -- the refusal is
computed from the entitlement, which for a stranger is the same
computation as for an unlisted key today. `/account/home` already told
any identified caller where an account lives (SIP-59), so the existence
of a Move is not a new disclosure through `/peer/mine`.

**What a stranger can pull.** Nothing, absent a member's statement; and
with one, what that member could have handed over. A person who names a
hostile exchange as their home has disclosed their own conversations to
it, which was theirs to do and which no list at the origin prevented --
a listed home is a home the operator knows, not one the operator vouches
for.

**What a stranger can cost.** Reads are served to the entitled and
refused to the rest at the cost of one lookup. Writes -- hints, Moves,
rehome notices, carried registrations -- are limited per caller; each is
also verified before anything is written, so the limit bounds
verification work and log lines, not damage. Waits are capped. Pulls are
reads and not limited, by SIP-56's rule; an exchange under a pull flood
from an entitled peer is being asked for what a member let it have, and
throttling that would be the member paying for a peer's appetite.

**A hint from a stranger points the home at an origin.** The home
resolves the hint's domain by SIP-33 and checks the key, asks
`/peer/mine` there for the account, and pulls what it is told; every
step is gated by the origin, and a stranger who controls the origin
learns only that the home asked. The limit bounds how often.

## Reference implementation

`sqex` 0.32.0, both halves; 0.78.0 (waiting); 0.80.0, 0.80.1 (open
peering).

**The origin**: `POST /peer/{hello,pull,envelopes,blobs,records}` in `sqexd`,
gated by an operational whitelist of peer keys and, per channel, by a surviving
`0x0b`. Every peering refusal is one refusal with no detail, for the reason
given above.

**The replica**: `sqexd::replica`, split so the halves can be tested apart.
`take` is synchronous verification and storage — SIP-31's signature and
credential steps, SIP-34's receipt under the origin's *pinned* key, the head
linkage, a gap stored and a divergence refused — and `pull_once`/`run` are the
transport over `sqexd::peer_client`. That client is eighty lines of
h3-over-sQUIC rather than a dependency on `sqnr`, which links libpcsclite
unconditionally for a YubiKey no server touches.

Splitting them was not tidiness. **An equivocating origin can be played against
`take` directly**, which a test driving a full loop could not do without writing
a dishonest exchange.

Origins to replicate from are `[[replicate]]` entries in `sqexd.toml`, each
pinning the origin's key; a member authorises a replica with `/replicate` in
`sqex-chat`, which words it as publication to another operator because this
document requires that.

**One thing this document declares and never uses.** `PEER_CONTEXT` is defined
in the Specification and appears in no construction anywhere in it: peering is
an ordinary sQUIC connection carrying a SIP-3 identity, and no signature here is
made over that label. It is kept only so a later revision that needs a domain
separator does not silently reuse the name for something else. An implementation
should not define it, and this one does not.

**Waiting** (§Waiting, sqex 0.78.0). `sqex-proto::peer::{PeerWait, Changed}`. `sqexd` answers
`/peer/wait` on the per-channel notifiers its clients' long polls use,
and wakes them from every event it tells its members -- an entry, a
signal, a read mark, a redaction, a membership -- so a wait fires on
everything a pull carries; the three replica loops (configured origins,
channels learned from a rehome, the home's) wait between pulls, keep
their interval as the safety net, and back off for `WAIT_RETRY_SECS`
from an origin that refuses the route. The client is untouched. Tests:
on the wire, a wait answers at once when behind, runs out empty when
not, wakes within the second on a post and on a signal, and never names
a channel the peer may not pull; end to end, a replica on a
twenty-second interval shows a post made at the origin in under five.

**Open peering** (§Open peering to §Reporting, sqex 0.80.0). `sqexd`:
`open_peering` in the configuration (reported
at startup); the §Peering gate answers any identified caller with a peer
that holds no grant when open, and the entitlement functions are
untouched; `/account/locate` and its home-follow skip the peer-list
check when open; `Moved.peered` is true when open; SIP-47's transport
sync admits the recorded home of every whitelisted account (SIP-47 §The
phone is a device); a `peering`
limit kind counted per caller key at the four writes; a per-caller count
of held waits, refused uniformly past `MAX_WAITS_PER_PEER`. No wire
format changes. Tests: two exchanges with neither listing the other --
a Move carried from a home nobody listed opens the pull, an invitation
reaches a home that never heard of the origin, and a name is located at
an unlisted domain -- each refused when either side is listed instead;
a stranger's pull of an unauthorised channel is refused identically
open or listed; the fourth hint in an hour is `rate_limited` and the
ninth wait is refused; and a closed origin admits a home its whitelisted
member named, and stops when she moves on.

Run live on 2026-09-17 between squic.org and trunk.exchange, both open,
with the line SIP-60's run had needed removed from squic.org's list. A
throwaway exchange on a laptop, keyed at random and listed nowhere, was
named home by an account at trunk.exchange: while trunk.exchange was
listed it was told nothing (`peered = 0`, no channels); eight seconds
after trunk.exchange restarted open it held the account's three
channels, the sealed direct message read whole there, and a post
through it reached the other party's home. A group made at
trunk.exchange with a squic.org account in it was told to squic.org
and pulled within seconds, over the link no operator had listed.

Two findings beside the document. A client that learned a direct
message's re-keyed channel (SIP-62) reading through its home could not
find the conversation at the origin: the alias was filed by the
exchange it was learned at, and a conversation keeps its channel at
every copy -- fixed in 0.80.1, where the alias is a fact about the
correspondent. And a home learned by hint holds no record of the
origin's earlier keys (SIP-40), so entries receipted before a rotation
are `Repudiated` at it -- trunk.exchange rotated on 2026-09-14, and
its oldest channel's first four entries never reached the laptop. An
origin's handover chain is signed by each retiring key and could be
served to a peer that asks; SIP-64 specified that the next day. The replica's log now says which entries a pull
refused and why, which is how the second was found.
