## Abstract

A bound identity periodically connects to an exchange and beats. The exchange
records when it last did so, and answers questions about it. No signature is
sent: the connection is the proof.

## Motivation

There is no way to ask whether a service is alive.

sqns answers where a service is, durably and without trusting the answering
server: records are signed by an identity key and verify on their own. That
property is exactly what stops sqns answering this question. The identity key
is deliberately kept offline — it is the thing that survives a service key
being stolen — and you cannot sign a heartbeat every sixty seconds with a key
that is in a safe.

The beacon works precisely because it needs no signature. SIP-2 establishes
that the connection has already proven the caller's transport key, so an
identity that can open a connection can assert its own liveness with no key
material online beyond what the connection already required.

One thing SIP-2 does *not* hand the exchange for free is the caller's Ed25519
name. SIP-2 delivers the verified X25519 key and leaves Ed25519 naming to a
consumer that already knows its callers — which the exchange, taking beats from
identities it has not pre-registered, does not. So the beacon needs a step that
binds an Ed25519 identity to the transport key the first time an identity is
seen: the withdrawn SIP-3 was one such step, and the TLS-client-auth path named
in SIP-2's rationale is another. This SIP assumes that binding exists; which
mechanism provides it is settled before the beacon is built, not here.

This is the smallest useful thing that could not be built before, which is why
it is the exchange's first feature rather than its most interesting one.

## Specification

### Beating

A bound identity opens a connection and sends a beat. The exchange records the
current time against the identity and replies with an acknowledgement.

A beat is an **explicit message**, not a side effect of connecting.

```
| type: u8 = 0x01 | interval_secs: u32 | flags: u8 |
```

| Field | Notes |
|---|---|
| `interval_secs` | how often the identity intends to beat |
| `flags` | bit 0: withhold from public queries. Others reserved, MUST be zero. |

The exchange MUST reject a beat from a connection whose verified transport key
(SIP-2) it cannot bind to a known Ed25519 identity, and MUST record the
observation against that Ed25519 identity rather than against the X25519 key the
transport verified.

### Reading

Anyone holding the exchange's public key may ask about an identity:

```
| type: u8 = 0x02 | ed25519_pubkey: [32] |
```

The reply carries the identity, when it was last seen, the interval it declared
and the exchange's current time:

```
| found: u8 | last_seen: u64 | interval_secs: u32 | now: u64 |
```

`now` is not redundant. A consumer's clock may be wrong, and "last seen at
timestamp T" is only interpretable against the observer's own clock. Consumers
SHOULD compute staleness as `now - last_seen`, not against local time.

### Interpreting a beacon

`interval_secs` is what makes an answer readable. "Last seen five minutes ago"
means nothing on its own; against a declared interval of sixty seconds it means
four beats have been missed.

Consumers SHOULD treat an identity as stale after a small multiple of its
declared interval — three is a reasonable default — and MUST NOT treat a single
missed beat as absence. Networks drop packets, and this ecosystem's own
transport has a 10s handshake timeout.

Exchanges MUST NOT compute liveness on the consumer's behalf. Reporting a
timestamp and an interval lets each consumer choose its own tolerance; reporting
"up" or "down" forces the exchange's choice on everyone.

### What is not recorded

The exchange MUST NOT record the observed source address in anything a public
query returns. It necessarily sees the address; publishing it turns a liveness
service into a location service, which is SIP-6's business and requires both
parties to consent.

### Withholding

An identity that sets the withhold flag is recorded but not disclosed to
queries from other identities. It may still read its own record.

## Rationale

**Why an explicit beat rather than counting connections.** An identity that
connects to collect mail (SIP-5) has not asserted anything about its
availability, and a service that happens to be reachable is not the same as one
declaring itself in service. Conflating them means an identity cannot use the
exchange without also advertising liveness it did not intend.

**Why the identity declares its own interval.** Only it knows how often it
intends to beat. A fixed global interval would either waste connections for
slow-moving services or be too coarse for fast ones, and the alternative — the
exchange inferring the interval from history — is a guess presented as fact.

**Why the exchange does not answer "is it up".** Because it cannot know. It
knows when it last saw a beat, which is a different and smaller claim.
Everything past that is a threshold, and thresholds belong to the consumer.

**Why not put last-seen into an sqns record.** Covered in Motivation: the
signing key is offline by design. This is the constraint that produced sqex.

## Compatibility

New protocol, nothing to break. Requires SIP-2.

## Security considerations

**This is the exchange's observation, not the identity's claim.** Every other
assertion in this ecosystem is self-authenticating — an sqns record verifies
against the key it describes, with no trust in whoever served it. A beacon
answer does not and cannot: only the exchange saw the connection. A consumer
trusting a beacon is trusting the operator, and that is a genuinely different
trust model from the rest of the stack. It should be stated in documentation
aimed at users, not just here.

An exchange can therefore lie in both directions: claim an identity is fresh
when it has not been seen, or withhold one that has. Signing answers does not
fix this — it authenticates the answer, not the observation behind it.
Consumers who need better should beat against more than one exchange.

**Last-seen is a disclosure.** It tells an observer when a machine is up, and
over time it maps working hours, maintenance windows and outages. That an
identity chose to beat does not mean it chose to be surveilled. The withhold
flag is the minimum answer; whether it is sufficient depends on the deployment.

**Correlation.** An exchange sees every bound identity's connection times and
source addresses together. Even without publishing addresses, it can tell that
two identities are on the same host or the same network. This is inherent in
being a central observer and cannot be fixed within this SIP.

**Absence proves nothing.** A silent beacon may mean the service is down, the
network is broken, the exchange is unreachable from it, or the operator turned
the beacon off. Consumers MUST NOT treat staleness as evidence of failure in
any context where the difference matters.
