## Abstract

The two things an account says about how other people may see it and reach it. A
**profile** is a display name, a self-declared title and a small avatar,
published to an exchange and served to whoever asks, optionally withheld from
strangers; every part of it is a claim its subject makes rather than a fact
anyone attests to. A **block list** names
accounts whose invitations and direct messages the exchange quietly declines to
deliver. This is the only place in the stack where a person is named by
something other than a key, and the security section is mostly about what
follows from that.

## Motivation

Everywhere else in this stack an identity is 32 bytes rendered as base58, and
that is exactly right for a protocol and unusable for a conversation. SIP-16
gives a channel a member list; the list is a column of keys. SIP-19's `Mention`
carries an identity and deliberately carries no name, on the grounds that a name
inside a message is a name the sender chose. Both are correct and both leave the
same hole: nothing anywhere says what a person is called.

The obvious alternative is to leave it to each client — a local address book,
petnames the reader assigns. That remains available and is strictly safer, since
a name the reader chose cannot impersonate anybody. It also does not work for
the case that actually arises, which is being added to a channel with eleven
people in it and knowing none of the keys. Somebody has to publish something.

This is a small service and its risk is not proportional to its size. The rest
of the stack has no impersonation vector at all, because identity is a key and
keys do not resemble one another. A display name is the first thing here that
two different people can make look the same.

Blocking is here for the same reason and not by accident of filing. SIP-16
bounds unsolicited contact with a quota, which limits how much of it arrives and
does nothing about who it arrives from. Both halves of this document are the
same question from opposite ends — what a person shows to others, and who is
allowed to reach them — and both are account-scoped state that no channel owns.
A block also governs a profile: the account you have blocked should not be
rendering an image in your member list.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. Every operation is bound to the caller's
transport identity (SIP-3). A connection carrying no identity has no profile and
MUST be refused.

A profile belongs to an **account** (SIP-20), not to a device. Every device of a
person publishes and reads the same profile, and the exchange resolves the
calling device to its account exactly as SIP-16 does.

### Messages

```
Put   | type: u8 = 0x01 | flags: u8 | name_len: u8 | name
      | title_len: u8 | title | avatar_len: u16 | avatar |
Get   | type: u8 = 0x02 | account[32] |

PutAck| now: u64 |
Got   | found: u8 | updated: u64 | now: u64 | flags: u8
      | name_len: u8 | name | title_len: u8 | title
      | avatar_len: u16 | avatar |
```

Endpoints `POST /profile/{put,get}`, both refusing a connection with no
advertised identity.

`Put` replaces the whole profile; there is no partial update. `name` and `title`
are UTF-8, of at most `MAX_NAME` and `MAX_TITLE` bytes, and either MAY be empty.
`avatar` is an image of at most `AVATAR` bytes and MAY be empty.

`title` is a short descriptor its subject chooses — a job, a team, a line about
themselves. It carries **no authority of any kind** and an exchange MUST NOT
interpret it. It is called `title` and not `role` deliberately: `role` already
means member or admin in SIP-16, where the exchange holds it and attests to it,
and reusing the word for a field its subject writes about itself would invite
exactly the confusion the security section below is about.

`Get` returns the profile of `account`. A profile that does not exist, and one
withheld from the caller, are reported identically: `found: 0` with every other
field zeroed. This follows SIP-4, and for SIP-4's reason — answering "exists but
hidden" would itself be the disclosure that hiding was meant to prevent.

### Flags

| Bit | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0 | Withhold from accounts sharing no channel with the subject |

Bits other than 0 are reserved and MUST be zero; a `Put` setting one MUST be
refused. This is SIP-4's rule for its beat flags, restated because a reserved
bit that is merely ignored is a reserved bit somebody will use.

An account always sees its own profile, whatever the flag says.

### The avatar is inline

The avatar travels in the message as bytes, not as a SIP-18 reference.

SIP-18 blobs are attached to channels and pruned on a channel's retention
window, because that is what lets an exchange expire an attachment without
reading the message that points at it. A profile is not in a channel and has no
retention window, so an avatar carried as a blob reference would need a second
lifetime rule inside SIP-18 that exists only for this — more machinery than the
thing it carries. `AVATAR` is 8 KiB, the same budget SIP-18 gives an inline
preview; a 96×96 JPEG is a couple of kilobytes.

A client SHOULD downscale before publishing rather than relying on the exchange
to refuse an oversized one, and MUST treat a fetched avatar as hostile input to
an image decoder, exactly as SIP-18 requires of every blob.

### Blocking

```
Set     | type: u8 = 0x03 | account[32] | op: u8 |   (0x01 add, 0x02 remove)
Blocked | type: u8 = 0x04 |

Blocks  | now: u64 | count: u16 | count × account[32] |
```

`POST /block/{set,list}`. The list is per account, applies everywhere, and is
returned only to its owner.

An exchange **MUST** honour it in three places, all of which are SIP-16
operations and none of which SIP-16 needs to know about:

- An `Invite` naming an account that has blocked the caller is **silently
  dropped**: the exchange answers `Ack` and adds nobody.
- A direct-message `Create` by a blocked account succeeds and does not add the
  blocker, leaving a channel the caller is alone in and may post into
  indefinitely.
- A `Get` of the blocker's profile answers `found: 0`, indistinguishably from
  withheld and from absent.

Existing shared channels are unaffected. Blocking somebody does not remove
either of you from a group, which is what both comparators do and what people
expect; the remedy for an unwanted member of a channel is SIP-16's `Remove`,
which is a different act by a different person.

That is the exchange answering untruthfully on the blocker's behalf, and this
document calls it that rather than describing it as a filter. The alternative, a
refusal the caller can detect, tells a harasser they have been blocked, and a
block that announces itself is worse than none.

**It is not undetectable, and MUST NOT be described as though it were.** Every
request succeeds, but no delivery cursor ever advances and SIP-16's `Info` shows
a channel with one member in it, so a determined caller can infer the block —
exactly the inference a WhatsApp user draws from a tick that never becomes two.
What this buys is that nothing *states* it, so the inference takes effort and is
never confirmed. That is the whole of the guarantee.

### Limits

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| `MAX_NAME` | 64 bytes |
| `MAX_TITLE` | 64 bytes |
| `AVATAR` | 8 KiB |
| Blocked accounts per account | 1 024 |
| Profile updates per account | 32 per hour |

An overrun MUST be refused distinguishably from a malformed request. The update
rate limit exists because a profile is served to everyone who shares a channel
with its subject, so rewriting it repeatedly is a cheap way to make an exchange
serve a great deal of traffic on somebody else's behalf.

A profile has no TTL and no expiry. It persists until replaced or until the
account is forgotten, which is a departure from SIP-4's deliberately volatile
observations and matches SIP-16's durability instead: a name that vanished on
restart would be worse than no name, because a client would show the key again
and a reader would not know why.

## Rationale

**Why the exchange rather than sqns or a signed record.** A signed profile,
published the way sqns publishes a record, would let a reader verify the name
came from its subject without trusting an exchange. It was rejected for the same
reason SIP-28 gives about resolution: the consumer already pins the key it asked
about, so a dishonest exchange can withhold a profile or serve a stale one, but
substituting a *different* name under the right key gains it nothing an attacker
could not achieve by simply choosing that name themselves. Signatures would buy
integrity against a threat that a display name does not have, at the cost of a
signing step on every rename.

**Why replacement rather than partial update.** Two fields. A partial update
needs a presence marker per field and a rule for clearing one, which is more
wire than sending both. SIP-28 made the same call about endpoint sets, for the
same reason.

**Why withholding is one bit and not a list.** A per-account visibility list is
an address book at the exchange, which is a much larger disclosure than the
profile it protects. Sharing a channel is a relationship the exchange already
knows about, so keying on it costs nothing new.

**Why it is a title and not a role, and why it is not attested.** An attested
role — the exchange reporting that an account holds some standing, unforgeable
by its subject — was considered and is a different proposal. It would make the
exchange an authority on something new, needing storage for the assignment and
administrative operations to set it, and the two things people actually want
here are mostly not that: a job and a team are facts about somebody's life that
no exchange is positioned to verify. Leaving it self-declared keeps this SIP
small and keeps the exchange out of a judgement it cannot make. The cost is the
impersonation surface in the security section, which is why the naming matters —
`role` would have implied the attested thing while providing the claimed one.

**Why no petname or verification field.** A client that lets a reader assign
their own name to a key has built something strictly better than this SIP, and
that name is the reader's business and does not belong on the wire. Nothing here
prevents it, and clients are encouraged to it — a locally assigned name should
take precedence over a published one wherever both exist.

**Why blocking is enforced at the exchange rather than by clients.** A
client-side block hides what has already been delivered, which means the sender
still consumes the recipient's quota, still occupies their storage, and still
learns from receipts that a device collected the message. Refusing at the
exchange stops the traffic. It costs the exchange a list it must consult on
every invite, which is the same lookup it already does for membership.

**Why a block is not symmetric.** Blocking somebody does not stop you seeing
their messages in a channel you both belong to, and does not remove either of
you from it. Symmetry sounds fairer and produces a worse outcome: a block that
ejects people from shared groups turns a personal boundary into an act visible
to everybody in the room, which is precisely what the invisible refusal is
trying to avoid.

**Why not a status message, a phone number, or presence.** Each is a further
disclosure with a different threat model, and a profile service that accumulates
them becomes the most revealing thing the exchange holds. A name and a picture
are what a member list needs to be legible. Anything else should have to argue
for itself in its own proposal.

## Compatibility

Purely additive. Four new routes, no change to any existing wire format. An
exchange without this SIP answers 404, a client without it shows keys and blocks
nobody, so old and new interoperate in both directions.

The three enforcement points are stated here rather than in SIP-16 deliberately,
so that SIP-16 does not have to require this document. An exchange may implement
channels with no notion of blocking at all; one that implements both MUST apply
the rules above.

A client MUST work with no profile service at all and MUST work when a profile
is absent. A member list is legible without names and unusable without keys, and
an implementation that treats a name as required has the dependency backwards.

## Security considerations

**Since SIP-32 a profile is a signed record, and this changes less than it
sounds.** The subject signs its fields and orders them with a counter it
controls, so a party holding a copy cannot rewrite it and cannot put an old one
back over a new one. What it does *not* do is anything about the danger this
section is mostly about: a signature proves an account chose a name, not that
the name is unlike somebody else's. Every warning below stands unchanged.

**A title is a claim about authority that nobody checked, and it is the most
dangerous field here.** A name that resembles somebody else's requires a reader
to mistake one person for another; a title requires nothing, because it asserts
standing directly. Anyone may set theirs to "Exchange Administrator",
"Moderator" or "Support", and the profile then performs the social engineering
unprompted.

Two rules follow and neither is optional. A client **MUST** render a title so
that it cannot be mistaken for a status the system assigned — never in the
styling it uses for a channel role, never as a badge, never adjacent to a
verification mark. And where authority actually matters, a client **MUST** show
the authority the exchange holds rather than anything from a profile: SIP-16's
`Info` says who administers a channel and SIP-24's admin list says who
administers the exchange, and both are facts. A title is what somebody typed.

An exchange MAY refuse titles matching an operator's reserved list. That is a
policy and not a defence — a determined impersonator spells it differently — and
it is worth having anyway, because it stops the accidental case where somebody
genuinely employed as a support engineer looks official by coincidence.

**A display name is a claim, not a fact, and it is the impersonation vector this
stack otherwise does not have.** Anyone may publish any name. Two accounts may
publish the same one, or names that differ by a homoglyph, a combining mark, or
a bidirectional override. A client **MUST** show the key alongside the name
wherever the distinction could matter — approving an invitation, reading a
mention, deciding whether a message is from who it appears to be — and MUST NOT
let a name be the only thing a person sees at those moments. This is the one
requirement in this SIP that is load-bearing.

**Unicode is the mechanism, so treat the name as hostile text.** The same
concerns SIP-19 raises about reaction emoji apply with more force to a field
whose whole purpose is to be recognised: normalisation, direction overrides,
zero-width characters and confusable scripts. A client SHOULD normalise for
display and SHOULD make invisible characters visible rather than silently
rendering them.

**The exchange learns who asks about whom.** A `Get` is an admission of interest
in a specific account, and the exchange sees every one. Withholding protects the
subject from strangers; it does nothing for the asker. A client SHOULD cache
profiles rather than fetching one each time a name is drawn, which reduces the
signal from a stream to an occasional point.

**Sharing a channel is the visibility rule, so joining a channel discloses a
profile.** An account that withholds its profile and then joins a public room
has published its name to everybody in that room, which is correct and is
unlikely to be what a person expects. A client SHOULD say so at the moment of
joining rather than in a settings screen.

**An avatar is an image from a stranger.** It is decoded by every client that
displays a member list, which makes it one of the most widely executed pieces of
attacker-supplied data in the system, and unlike a message attachment it is
fetched without anybody choosing to open it. The size cap bounds the traffic and
does nothing about the decoder. An implementation SHOULD decode avatars in the
same guarded path it uses for SIP-18 blobs, and SHOULD NOT decode one at all for
an account the reader has blocked.

**Blocking asks the exchange to lie, and it can decline.** The invisible refusal
depends entirely on the exchange implementing it. A dishonest one can tell the
blocked party, or deliver anyway, and no member can check. A block is a request
to a service, not a property of the protocol, and somebody relying on one to be
safe from a specific person should be told which of those it is.

**A block list is a durable record of who a person wants to avoid**, held in the
clear by the exchange. It is answerable only to its owner, and the exchange
holds it regardless. That is a more sensitive list than a member list and an
operator should treat it as one.

**A profile outlives the relationship that disclosed it.** Nothing here is
retracted when somebody leaves a channel, and a client that cached a name and
picture keeps them. The withhold flag governs future fetches only.

## Reference implementation

Implemented in [sqex](https://github.com/wave-cl/sqex) v0.9.0:

- `sqex-proto::profile` holds `Profile` — display name, role, avatar blob
  reference — and the block-list messages.
- `sqexd::profile` stores both, rate-limits updates, and answers a withheld
  profile exactly as it answers one that does not exist.
- Routes: `POST /profile/{put,get}` and `POST /block/{set,list}`.

Blocking is implemented as a function passed into the channel store rather than
a table the channel store reads, which keeps the two services from knowing about
each other's storage. What matters more is what it does: a blocked invitation is
**dropped and answered as though it landed**. The exchange says something untrue
on the blocker's behalf, because a refusal the caller can detect tells a harasser
they have been blocked, and that is the failure this feature exists to avoid.

Integration tests cover a profile published and read; a withheld profile
indistinguishable from an absent one; a blocked invitation dropped and
acknowledged; a blocked direct message leaving the sender alone in a channel
they may post into indefinitely; a blocked account told nothing about the
blocker; a block list returned only to its owner; and the store surviving a
restart.
