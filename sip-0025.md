## Abstract

Two identities behind NAT ask an exchange to introduce them. It tells each the
other's observed address and coordinates a simultaneous connection attempt, so
they can establish a direct sQUIC connection without either being reachable
from outside.

The address family is part of the match: a request is offered on the family
it arrived over, a pair is introduced only on a family both offered, a party
reachable on more than one offers each by asking over it, and a pair that
shares none is told so rather than being given an address that cannot be
dialled.

## Motivation

sQUIC is UDP, which makes hole punching viable, and the exchange is already
in the ideal position: every bound identity connects to it, so it observes
everyone's real source address. Nothing else in the stack can pair two peers
who are both unreachable.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-69.* A live run on 2026-09-18, phone to desktop. The phone was on a mobile
network with an IPv4 carrier-NAT address and no IPv6 at all; the desktop
reached the same exchange over IPv6. Both asked for an introduction, within
a second of each other, and the exchange paired them and answered both. The
phone was handed the desktop's IPv6 address and its transport refused it out
of hand: it had no IPv6 route and never sent a packet. The desktop was
handed the phone's IPv4 address, waited, and reported that the punch had
failed.

Three things were wrong, and only the first is about a call not connecting.

- **The attempt was never possible.** No packet either side sent could have
  reached the other. The eight seconds spent were spent on nothing.
- **The diagnosis was wrong, and stated confidently.** The listening side
  reported that this needs endpoint-independent mapping and that symmetric
  NAT defeats it -- this document's language, accurate in general and not what
  happened here. An operator reading that would look at the NAT.
- **Nothing could tell the difference.** A peer who never asked and a peer
  who asked from another family look identical to the caller: silence, then
  a relay. The failure that can be fixed and the failure that cannot are
  indistinguishable.

The relay carried the call, as §Failure and fallback intends. This is not about a call being
lost; it is about an introduction that could not work being attempted anyway,
and about the exchange knowing that and not saying it.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian.

```
Introduce  | type: u8 = 0x01 | peer[32] | wait_secs: u16 |

Introduced | ready: u8 | kind: u8 | addr[16] | port: u16
           | start_at: u64 | now: u64 |
```

Both parties connect and request introduction to the other's Ed25519 identity.
When both requests are outstanding the exchange answers each with the other's
observed address and a start time, and both begin connecting then.

### Consent, which is the whole design

**An introduction is served only when both parties have asked**, independently.
Until then the answer discloses nothing: not the address, and **not that anybody
asked**, which would itself be a signal about somebody who has not consented. A
waiting answer is byte-for-byte the same length as a ready one, so the size of
the reply says nothing either.

A standing "introducible to anyone" mode is **rejected**, and this settles the
question the sketch left open. Such a mode makes the exchange a location service
for every identity that enables it: an address is disclosed to whoever asks, and
the asker needs no relationship of any kind. SIP-4 declines to publish addresses
for exactly this reason and its argument applies unchanged. An identity that
wants to be reachable without an introduction should publish endpoints under
SIP-28, where the disclosure is deliberate, bounded by a TTL, and its own act.

An identity cannot ask to meet itself. That introduces a caller to its own
address for nothing and is the degenerate case a rule keyed on a pair must
exclude on purpose.

### The address is observed, never asserted

The address disclosed is the one the exchange saw the connection come from. A
caller-supplied address would make this route a way to aim a coordinated pair of
simultaneous connections at somebody who never asked for them — which is the
shape a reflection abuse takes, and is what "provided the requests are genuinely
independent" in the security section is protecting.

### Address families

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-69.* The match above pairs on identity alone, so two parties
that reached the exchange over different address families are each handed
an address they have no route to. The sections to §Asking for the third
answer make the family part of the match. The wire and its lengths are as
above.

### The family of a request is the family it arrived over

A request offers exactly one family: the family of the address the exchange
observed it arrive from. It is **observed, never asserted**, which is
§The address is observed, never asserted's rule for the address itself and holds here for the same reason -- a
party that could name a family it is not on could aim a coordinated pair of
connection attempts at an address it does not hold.

A pending request is held under the asker, the peer, and that family, where
the rule above held it under the asker and the peer alone.

### A pair is introduced on a family both offered

Both parties are introduced when each has a live request for the other **on
one family**, and are told that family's addresses. Everything else
above is unchanged: the same start time computed once and read back by the
other, the same long poll, the same silence for a peer who has not asked.

Where more than one family matches, the exchange SHOULD choose IPv6. A pair
that both reached the exchange over IPv6 is more often globally addressed
and not translated at all, and an introduction between two such parties has
the best chance of any of them.

### Offering more than one family

A party reachable on several families offers each by **asking over each**:
one request per family, from a connection of that family. There is no field
to set and nothing to declare.

This is deliberate. A declared capability would be an assertion, which
§The address is observed, never asserted excludes for addresses, and would also be a claim a host is in a poor
position to make: an address is not a route, and a host may hold an IPv6
address that reaches nothing. Asking over a family proves the exchange can
be reached over it, which is the closest available evidence that a peer
might be too.

### Both asked, and share no family

When both parties have a live request for each other and no family is common
to them, each is answered with `ready = 2`, a third value beside the
waiting and ready above, with `kind = 0` and the address bytes zeroed. The reply's
length is unchanged, so it is byte-for-byte the shape of the other two.

This answer is served **only when both have asked**. That is what makes it
safe to send: it says the peer asked, which §Consent, which is the whole design withholds from a
party who has not consented, to a party who has consented by asking. Nothing is
disclosed to anyone the sections above would not already have answered.

A client receiving it MUST fall back to [SIP-12](sip-0012.md) and SHOULD say
what happened in the terms it was told -- that the two are on networks with
no family in common -- and not in the terms of NAT traversal.

### Asking for the third answer

`Introduce` carries a type byte. A caller that understands `ready = 2` sends
type `0x02`; the fields and the length are otherwise `Introduce`'s. A caller
sending `0x01` is never answered `2`: where the families do not
meet, it is answered with the same waiting it would have received had the
peer not asked at all.

An exchange from before §Address families (sqex 0.88.0) refuses `0x02` as
malformed, which is how a caller discovers it and falls back to `0x01`.

### Timing

The request is a **long poll**. The first party to ask holds it open for up to
`wait_secs`, and the second party's request wakes it, so both are answered
within a wake-up of each other. Polling would have worked and would have put the
two answers as far apart as the poll interval, which is the one thing a
coordinated start cannot afford.

`start_at` is a moment on **the exchange's clock**, and `now` is that clock, so
each side computes its own offset rather than assuming three clocks agree. This
is SIP-4's rule for staleness applied to a start time, and for the same reason.

**Both sides are told the same moment.** Whichever request completes the pair
computes it; the other reads it back. A second caller told a later start than
the first was already given would defeat the point of coordinating at all.

A request whose partner never arrives is answered, after the wait, with the same
silence it began with. There is no failure to report, because nothing was
disclosed.

### What this traverses, and what it does not

Stated rather than implied, because implying universality here would be the
error the sketch warned against.

- **Endpoint-independent mapping** — a NAT that reuses one external port for a
  given internal socket whatever the destination — is what this needs, and is
  the common case for home routers.
- **Symmetric NAT**, which allocates a fresh external port per destination,
  generally defeats it: the port the exchange observed is not the port the peer
  will see, so the mapping being reused is not the mapping being punched.
  Nothing here works around that and nothing should pretend to.
- **Carrier-grade NAT** behaves as symmetric often enough that it should be
  assumed to.
- Where either side is directly reachable, an introduction is unnecessary:
  SIP-28 publishes the address and the peer dials it.

### Failure and fallback

[SIP-12](sip-0012.md) is the fallback and already exists: the exchange carries
the session instead of introducing the peers. It needs no transport change and
works behind symmetric NAT, at the cost of relaying the traffic.

The two are complementary rather than alternatives, and an implementation should
**punch where it can and relay where it cannot**. This document does not specify
the decision between them; a client that tried both and used whichever completed
would be within it.

## Rationale

**Introduction presupposes prior knowledge.** A sQUIC server refuses anyone
who does not already hold its public key, so an introduced peer must already
know the other's identity. The exchange therefore reveals an address, never a
key, and cannot introduce strangers. That is a limitation of the design and
also its safety property.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-69:*

**Why the exchange decides and not the client.** The client cannot. It
learns the peer's address only after the wait it is trying to avoid
spending, and a client told an address it cannot use still does not know
whether the peer is unreachable or simply never asked.

**Why not make the exchange translate.** It could hand each party an address
of its own family by relaying the punch, but that is the relay, and SIP-12
already is it. An introduction whose traffic goes through the exchange is
not an introduction.

**Why this does not promise more calls will connect.** It will not, for the
pair in the motivation: a carrier-NAT address behaves as symmetric often
enough that §Failure and fallback says to assume it does, so even matched on IPv4 that
punch would likely fail. What changes is that a pair that cannot meet learns
so at once instead of after the wait, and learns the true reason. Removing a
wasted attempt and a wrong answer is the whole of the claim.

**Why one family per request rather than a set.** A request carries the
address it came from and one family is what that is. A set would need the
exchange to hold addresses it observed at other times on other connections,
and to answer with one it did not see on the connection asking -- which is
the assertion §The address is observed, never asserted is avoiding.

## Compatibility

New protocol. Requires SIP-2 for the authenticated caller. Nothing existing
changes shape, and an exchange without the route refuses it as it refuses any
unknown path.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-69.* **A client sending `0x01` against an exchange with §Address families** is
strictly better off. It
sends `0x01`, is matched on family, and where there is no match receives the
waiting it understands. It is no longer handed an address it cannot dial.

**A client sending `0x02` against an exchange from before §Address
families** has `0x02` refused as
malformed, falls back to `0x01`, and is on the old behaviour.

**Whatever the exchange said**, a client MUST check that a disclosed address
is one it can dial before dialling it, and relay with a plain reason if it
is not. An exchange is not the authority on what a client's network can
reach, and this is the check that would have produced a true diagnosis on
the day.

## Security considerations

**Address disclosure is the entire mechanism**, so consent is not a detail. An
identity that can request introduction to anyone can locate anyone bound to the
exchange.

**Amplification.** Coordinated simultaneous connections to an address supplied
by a third party is a shape worth examining for reflection abuse: the exchange
must not be usable to point traffic at an address that did not ask for it.
Requiring both sides to have requested addresses this, provided the requests
are genuinely independent — and the implementation keeps them so by never
letting one request create the other, and by disclosing only the address it
observed rather than any address a caller named.

**The exchange learns who wants to talk to whom**, and unlike SIP-5 it learns
it even when no message is ever sent.

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-69.* **No new disclosure.** `ready = 2` is served only to a party that has itself
asked, which is the gate §Consent, which is the whole design already sets, and it carries no address.

**Probing is not made easier.** Learning whether an identity asked still
requires asking, and an asker is disclosing itself to do so. A party that
wants to know which families another is on learns nothing it could not learn
by being introduced.

**The family is not a new secret.** It is implied by the address §The address is observed, never asserted would
have disclosed to the same party under the same consent.

**Denial of service is unchanged.** The pending table gains a family in its
key, so an identity may hold one pending request per peer per family rather
than per peer. Two is the practical bound, and an exchange SHOULD apply its
existing per-identity limits across families rather than per family.

## Reference implementation

**Implemented in `sqex` 0.34.0 on squic v0.26.0 / squic-go v0.71.0; 0.88.0
to 0.88.2 (address families); Active since 2026-09-19 on the evidence in
§Proven where it counts, 2026-09-18.** Everything above is built and tested, two peers who both
ask are told where the other is and when to begin, and they then connect
directly with the exchange out of the path. What was missing was not code —
see §Proven where it counts, 2026-09-18.

`sqex-proto::rendezvous` (the wire), `sqexd::rendezvous` (the exchange's half),
`sqex-proto::direct` (the client's half: ask from a chosen port, punch, dial or
listen, agree a SIP-12 key over the connection), `sqex meet` and `sqex-voice
call --direct` on it, and **sigil**, whose direct-message calls try this first
and relay when it fails (SIP-36's `direct` bit) — the second implementer, and
the one that will produce the evidence below. In the transport, two options
that did not exist when this was written:

- **`local_bind`** — a dial that leaves from a chosen local port. This was the
  blocker. `dial` otherwise binds `:0`, so every attempt makes a fresh mapping
  the peer has never been told about.
- **`punch`** — a one-byte datagram to each named address right after binding,
  from both `dial` and `listen`, because a NAT delivers nothing inbound until
  something has gone out. The byte is `0x00`: a short header for a connection
  nobody has, dropped in silence by every squic endpoint and never answered. It
  is bounded at four targets and three datagrams, since it is a send primitive
  with a caller-supplied destination and that is the shape a reflection abuse
  takes. It amplifies nothing.

### One port, and what that costs

The address an exchange observes is the mapping a *socket* made, so the
connection that gets introduced and the connection that punches have to leave
from the same port. `sqex meet` drops the exchange connection and rebinds, which
works because a NAT keeps a mapping alive for tens of seconds after the last
packet.

**Holding it open would be better and is not possible.** Two QUIC endpoints
cannot share a socket, so a peer that kept its exchange connection while
accepting a punched one needs a single endpoint that both dials and accepts —
and that endpoint must add an envelope on send and validate-and-strip one on
receive, keyed differently in each direction. Those are two separate wrappers
today with two separate cookie paths. It buys robustness rather than capability,
which is why it is not here.

Simultaneous open is out for the same reason. One side dials and the other
listens, chosen by the lower key — the tiebreak SIP-12 already uses to decide
which peer is `first`, so there is one convention and not two.

### Both ends pin the other

The dialer pins the listener's identity, as every sQUIC dial does. The listener
admits **only** the dialer it was introduced to: its whitelist holds the
peer's transport key, and the advertised identity is checked as well before the
handshake completes, so a key that merely maps to the same point is not enough.
An introduction discloses an address to one named party; the port it opens must
not be a door for anyone who learns that address.

### The key is agreed on the connection

Once connected, the two agree a SIP-12 session key over one stream — each
sends a 32-byte ephemeral and reads the other's — from the same four terms as
a relayed session. The exchange was never party to it, the connection is
authenticated both ways, and the frames are sealed exactly as they would be
relayed, so a client runs one media loop over either path. The session id is
`1` by convention: nothing assigns one, and there is one session on the
connection.

### Address families (0.88.0 to 0.88.2)

*Added 2026-09-18, was SIP-69.* `sqex` 0.88.0, corrected in 0.88.1 and 0.88.2.
Both corrections are recorded here because each is a trap an independent implementation would fall into.

`sqex-proto::rendezvous`: `TYPE_INTRODUCE_FAMILIES = 0x02` and
`Introduce::family_aware`; `Answer::NoSharedFamily = 2` beside waiting and
ready, with `Introduced::no_shared_family` zeroing the address so all three
answers encode to one length. `sqexd::rendezvous`: `Family::{V4, V6}`, and a
pending table keyed on `(asker, peer, family)` where the pair alone had
been the key; `request` answers `no_shared_family` only when the peer holds a live
request on another family, and `waiting` when it holds none, which is what
keeps the two apart; `wait` returns a settled answer without polling, because
a pair that shares no family is not pending. `sqexd::server` downgrades that
answer to `waiting` for a caller that sent `0x01`. `sqex-proto::direct`:
`Meeting::NoSharedFamily`, and the caller's own check that a disclosed
address is one it can dial.

**Canonicalise an observed address before anything else touches it.** An
exchange listening on `[::]` is dual-stack and observes an IPv4 peer as
`::ffff:a.b.c.d`, which a standard library's "is this IPv6" will agree is
IPv6. Keyed, compared and disclosed in that form, two parties on IPv4 land in
different families and each is handed an address its socket cannot send to --
so §Both asked, and share no family's answer is served to pairs it was
never written for, and
reads to an operator as a fault in the network. That defeated the
implementation entirely until 0.88.2, on a pair where one end held no IPv6
address and no IPv6 route at all. The distinction §Both asked, and share no family does draw has
to survive the fix: a mapped IPv4 party and a real IPv6 party still share no
family.

**Say which side reached the conclusion.** An exchange refusing a pair and a
caller refusing an address are different findings, and while both produced
one sentence no log could show the exchange's half had run at all: two
devices' clocks are not close enough to order one answer against the other
by time. 0.88.2 separates the wording, and only the exchange's mentions that
both asked -- the one thing a caller cannot know.

A third correction belongs to §One port, and what that costs and was
surfaced by the address-family work.
Binding the reused port in the exchange's family moved the listen onto IPv6,
where the just-dropped exchange connection had not yet released the socket.
The party that completes the pair computes the start time; the other reads it
back and so has no lead left to spend, and that party is the listener.
0.88.1 waits for the port rather than assuming a fixed pause.

Tests: a pair asking on one family is introduced and told that family's
addresses; a pair asking across families gets `2`, and every answer encodes
to one length; a peer who has not asked is still silence; a party asking over
both meets on the one they share; a caller sending `0x01` gets waiting where one
sending `0x02` gets `2`; two mapped IPv4 parties are one family and are handed
dialable addresses, while a mapped IPv4 party and a real IPv6 one are not.
Each mapped-address test was checked by removing the canonicalisation and
watching it fail.

Deployed on two exchanges on 2026-09-18, both listening `[::]:443`. The first
direct call between a phone on a carrier-NAT IPv4 address and a desktop
followed: two public IPv4 addresses behind NAT, punched, Opus both ways,
packet counts mirroring exactly, no loss.

### Proven where it counts, 2026-09-18

**The property that matters cannot be demonstrated from a desk**, and for two
years of this document's life it was not. The tests put two peers through a
real exchange and have them connect on the ports it observed, which proves the
coordination produces a usable address and that a connection leaves from the
port that was introduced. On loopback there is no NAT. Whether any of it
survives one was a question for two peers behind two real ones, and that is
evidence rather than code.

The evidence this document asked for -- place a sigil call, which says on
screen whether it went direct -- now exists. A phone on a mobile carrier
network and a desktop on a fixed line, two different public IPv4 addresses,
both behind NAT and neither reachable from the other unbidden: introduced,
punched, and connected with the exchange out of the path. Opus both ways for
58 seconds, each side's sent count equal to the other's received count, 0.0%
loss, nothing concealed, and the hangup seen by the far end rather than timed
out. Repeated, with the same result.

Two honest limits on that. **The NAT types were not characterised**, so this
says a punch survived these two NATs and not that it survives symmetric ones;
this document already says it does not, and SIP-12 remains the answer there.
And both ends ran the same implementation, which is the general caveat on any
single-implementation evidence.

Getting there also took three faults that had nothing to do with NAT, all
fixed in `sqex` 0.88.x and recorded in §Address families (0.88.0 to
0.88.2) above. The most instructive: an exchange
listening dual-stack observes an IPv4 peer as `::ffff:a.b.c.d`, and while
that was compared as written, two parties on IPv4 were told they shared no
address family. The coordination in this document is only as good as the
address it carries.

Meanwhile SIP-12 relays, works behind symmetric NAT today, and needs none of
this.
