## Abstract

A framing for voice inside a SIP-12 relayed session that lets a speaker stop
transmitting while they are not talking, and **describes the silence instead of
concealing it**. A media timestamp separates *where audio belongs in time* from
*which packets were sent*, so a receiver can tell deliberate silence from a lost
packet. A type byte distinguishes audio from a two-byte descriptor carrying the
**level and spectral tilt of the room's noise floor**, which the receiver
synthesises comfort noise from rather than replaying the codec's concealment. A
pause costs about one packet a second instead of fifty, and the far end neither
goes dead nor pulses.

## Motivation

A voice client with nothing to suppress transmission sends fifty frames a second
for as long as the call lasts, whether or not anyone is speaking. In a two-party
call that is merely wasteful. In a SIP-13 room it is quadratic: eight people is
fifty-six relayed streams, all running flat out, mostly carrying silence — and
the exchange pays for every one of them.

**A gap is ambiguous, and that is the first problem.** SIP-12's sequence number
is both the AEAD nonce and the only ordering a receiver has. If a sender simply
skips frames, the receiver sees missing sequence numbers and cannot distinguish
them from loss — and a jitter buffer that treats them as loss asks the codec to
conceal, which invents speech out of silence. The result is worse than sending
the packets. Hence the media timestamp: two counters, one for time and one for
packets, so a gap can be split into the part that was lost and the part that was
never sent.

**Leaving the decision to the codec is the second problem, and it was tried
first.** Opus has a discontinuous-transmission mode and its own speech detector,
and that detector barely engages against a real room: over a 3.8 s pause
carrying a noise floor at RMS 0.006, it still transmitted 37 frames of 190, 28
of them full size. The saving was about threefold where the one-byte silent
packet suggested thirty.

The obvious remedies were measured, and both fail with the same root:
**concealment is the wrong tool for describing silence.** Attenuating the input
does not move the codec's detector, and pushes its comfort noise toward
describing digital silence — which the receiver then renders as dead air.
Gating in the application works, taking that pause from 37 frames to 4, and
makes the far end swing 80:1: concealment extrapolated from an isolated noise
frame overshoots and then decays, so the room pulses once a second.

Concealment guesses at what a lost frame contained. A pause is not a lost frame,
and guessing at it is exactly wrong. Telephony codecs solved this decades ago:
send a **silence descriptor** — the level, and enough shape to be recognisable —
and let the receiver generate noise to match. The description is two bytes and
needs sending about once a second.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. This framing occupies the **plaintext of a
SIP-12 frame**, on either carriage; the exchange sees only ciphertext.

### Frame

```
| type: u8 | timestamp: u32 | body |
```

`timestamp` counts 20 ms media frames from an arbitrary origin chosen by the
sender, and increments for **every** frame the sender produces, including those
it does not transmit. It wraps, and a receiver MUST handle wrapping; at 20 ms a
`u32` lasts over two years.

Frame duration is fixed at 20 ms by this SIP; a sender using another duration
needs a different one. Sample rate is deliberately **not** carried: Opus decodes
any stream at whatever rate the receiver chooses, so the two ends need not agree
and there is nothing to negotiate.

| `type` | meaning | body |
|---|---|---|
| `0x01` | audio | one codec packet |
| `0x02` | comfort | `\| level: u8 \| tilt: u8 \|` |

Any other type MUST be ignored, which leaves room to add one without a flag day.

### The comfort descriptor

`level` encodes the noise floor's RMS in half-decibels below full scale:

```
level = clamp(round(-2 × 20 × log10(rms)), 0, 254)
level = 255                                  when rms is zero
```

So 255 is digital silence, 120 is −60 dBFS, and 0 is full scale. A receiver
**MUST clamp** what it synthesises to at most −30 dBFS: comfort noise is never
loud, and a peer claiming otherwise is either broken or hostile.

`tilt` is the fraction of the frame's energy above 1 kHz, as `round(255 × f)`.
0 is a rumble, 255 is a hiss, and most rooms sit low. It exists because white
noise at the right level still sounds nothing like a room, and one byte is
enough to tell the two apart.

### What a sender transmits

A sender decides **for itself** whether a frame is speech; it MUST NOT rely on
the codec's discontinuous transmission, which does not engage reliably against
a room. How it decides is not specified — an adaptive noise floor with hangover
is the obvious approach — but it MUST NOT clip the start of speech.

- **Speech** — encode and transmit an audio frame.
- **Not speech** — transmit no audio. Transmit a **comfort** frame:
  1. on the first non-speech frame after speech, and
  2. at least once per `KEEPALIVE` thereafter. RECOMMENDED one second.

SIP-12's sequence number increments **only on transmission**; the timestamp
increments regardless, since it counts time. That separation is the whole
mechanism, and it is what makes a gap classifiable.

A sender that does not implement comfort noise MAY transmit audio continuously,
and MUST NOT skip frames without describing what it skipped.

### What a receiver does

Given two successively received
frames `(seq_p, ts_p)` and `(seq_n, ts_n)`:

```
missing slots = ts_n - ts_p - 1
lost packets  = seq_n - seq_p - 1
```

At most `lost packets` of those slots may be treated as loss; the rest are
silence. Then, per slot:

| slot | rendering |
|---|---|
| audio frame | decode it |
| loss | decode nothing — the codec conceals |
| silence, a descriptor has been seen | **synthesise** noise at the last `level` and `tilt` |
| silence, no descriptor yet | decode nothing |
| comfort frame | adopt its `level`/`tilt`, and synthesise this slot |

A receiver **MUST NOT** render silence as digital zeros, and MUST NOT conceal
it. Zeros cut the room's noise floor off at a cliff fifty times a second, which
is audible as chopping; concealment invents speech nobody spoke.

Synthesis is not specified beyond the level clamp. Filtered white noise matched
to `tilt` is sufficient and is what the reference implementation does.

### Sounding right

Three details decide whether a pause sounds like a room or like a fault. None
is required for interoperation and all three were found by listening, after
measurements said the result was already smooth — a synthetic room is
stationary, and no real one is.

- A sender **SHOULD** describe the room from a *smoothed* estimate over the
  pause, not from the single frame its keepalive happens to land on. One 20 ms
  frame's level wanders enough that the far end otherwise steps to a slightly
  different level once a second.
- A receiver **SHOULD** glide toward a new description rather than stepping to
  it. A few hundred milliseconds is enough: fast enough to follow a room,
  slow enough to be inaudible. A step in a noise floor is a click.
- A sender's detector **SHOULD** use hysteresis — a lower threshold to stop
  than to start. With one threshold a level sitting near it makes the gate
  chatter, and each flip swaps a real room for a synthesised one. Those have
  different textures even at matched levels, and alternating them several times
  a second is audible.

## Rationale

**Why a timestamp rather than making the sequence number mean time.** Making
SIP-12's sequence number advance across untransmitted frames would preserve
timing, but it is the AEAD nonce and the loss signal; advancing it for packets
that never existed makes every silent run look like loss. Two counters is the
answer RTP reached for the same reason, and for the same reason here.

**Why a type byte in front rather than inferring the frame's kind.** There is no
unambiguous way to tell a descriptor from a codec packet without one: a codec
packet's first byte is already meaningful, and a zero-length body is a legal, if
useless, audio frame. One byte in front costs 2% of a comfort frame and nothing
that matters on an audio one.

**Why two bytes and not more.** A full spectral envelope is what a codec's own
descriptor carries, and it would sound better. Level and tilt are what can be
justified for a demo: level is what makes a pause stop being a hole, and tilt is
what stops the result sounding like tape hiss in a room that rumbles. The frame
is extensible by type if that turns out not to be enough.

**Why the sender decides and not the codec.** The codec knows more about speech
than an energy threshold does, which is the argument for letting it decide, and
it is not enough. Its detector is tuned to decide whether *encoding* is
worthwhile, not whether *transmitting* is — and against a noise floor it keeps
deciding yes. Once the receiver can synthesise the room, the application can
afford to be more aggressive than the codec, because being wrong now costs a
moment of synthesised noise instead of a hole.

**What the codec's own detector does, measured.** Three behaviours, none
documented as a number, all of which surprised the first implementation and all
of which are why the decision moved out of the codec:

- **It takes about ten frames to engage.** The first ~200 ms of a pause is still
  transmitted at full size, so a short pause saves much less than a long one and
  there is no clean edge.
- **It refreshes periodically.** Deep inside a silence it still emits a
  full-sized comfort-noise update every few hundred milliseconds.
- **The saving depends on the room.** Against digital silence it skips almost
  everything; against a real noise floor it has something to encode and skips
  less. Over the same twelve seconds — two of speech, ten of pause — where
  continuous transmission sent 600 packets:

  | pause is | packets |
  |---|---|
  | digital silence | 157 |
  | a quiet room (RMS 0.006) | 182 |

  An implementation should quote the second number, because the first describes
  a condition no microphone produces.

**Why a level clamp is normative.** The descriptor is the one place where a peer
states, rather than demonstrates, how loud something should be. Without a clamp,
one byte set to zero is a full-scale noise burst into somebody's headphones.

## Compatibility

**Both ends must implement this.** The framing is the plaintext both ends parse,
and there is no version negotiation in it: a sender using it against a receiver
that does not have four bytes of timestamp fed to its decoder as codec data, and
hears nothing usable. There is no earlier framing to interoperate with — the
only implementation moves as a whole.

Nothing between a peer and the exchange changes. SIP-12 and SIP-13 are
untouched, and an exchange cannot tell which framing is in use.

## Security considerations

**This trades privacy for bandwidth, and the trade is real.** With continuous
transmission a packet stream carries no information about speech activity: an
observer, the exchange included, sees a constant rate whatever is being said or
not said. Stopping between utterances makes **packet presence a voice-activity
signal**. An exchange relaying a call learns when each party speaks, how long
their turns run, who interrupts whom, and the rhythm of the conversation; in a
SIP-13 room it learns which members are talking and in what order, which is a
conversation graph over identities it already knows. SIP-12 notes that frame
sizes and cadence are visible to an exchange. This sharpens that from a side
channel into a clear one, and the descriptor sharpens it further — a pause is
now about one packet a second against fifty, so speech and silence are *more*
clearly distinguishable, not less. The content stays sealed; the pattern of
speech does not.

An implementation MUST keep continuous transmission available, and a deployment
where the pattern of who speaks when is sensitive SHOULD NOT enable this. The
keepalive masks nothing — one packet a second against fifty is plainly
distinguishable.

**Padding is not a fix here.** Constant-rate cover traffic would restore the
property, but it also restores the entire cost this SIP exists to avoid; an
implementation wanting both should simply transmit continuously.

**A malicious sender can lie about the timestamp**, claiming a large jump to
force a long silence, or a small one to compress time. It can do neither to
anyone else's stream — the frame is sealed under a key only the two peers hold —
so this is a peer misbehaving in its own conversation, which it could do more
simply by saying nothing. A receiver SHOULD bound how far a timestamp may jump
rather than allocate against it.

**A descriptor is an assertion, not a demonstration.** Every other field in this
stack is checkable — a signature verifies, a MAC1 proves a key, a codec packet
either decodes or does not. `level` is a number a peer simply states, and the
receiver acts on it by making a sound. Hence the mandatory clamp. `tilt` needs
no clamp: it selects between filters, and every value is harmless.

**A hostile peer can make a listener's room sound wrong** — claiming a hiss where
there is a rumble, or −30 dBFS of noise in a silent room. This is a peer
misbehaving in its own conversation, which it could do more simply by making
noise, and it is bounded by the clamp.

**The descriptor is inside the sealed payload**, so the exchange learns nothing
about the sender's acoustic environment from it. It sees a small packet, as it
does for a keepalive.

## Reference implementation

Shipped in [sqex](https://github.com/wave-cl/sqex) v0.8.0, in `sqex-voice`, on
SIP-12 carriage:

- `sqex_voice::media` holds the framing, the `Comfort` descriptor, the detector
  (`Gate`) and the synthesiser (`Noise`).
- `sqex_voice::jitter` keys its buffer on the timestamp, classifies gaps, and
  carries a `Playout::Comfort` beside `Frame` and `Conceal`. Rendering happens
  in one place — `Playback::render` — which is deliberate: the previous version
  let each call site choose, and one of them rendered silence as digital zeros.
- Both the two-party call and the SIP-13 room use it. `--no-dtx` restores
  continuous transmission for the privacy reason above.

Measured on a live exchange, twelve seconds of two seconds' speech and ten of
pause against a real noise floor: **600 packets** transmitting continuously,
**182** leaving the decision to the codec, **125** under this SIP. In the
end-to-end test a settled pause costs **3 packets in 180 slots**, where the
codec's own detector sent about 37.

The rendering rule was learned the hard way. The first implementation wrote
digital zeros for a silent slot; every test passed, because a synthetic test's
silence *is* zeros. Through a microphone it was immediately audible as chopping,
and the measurement showed why: 99 of 130 frames in a pause were digitally dead
between bursts of the noise floor returning.

**Two ways to write a detector that mutes the speaker**, both of which happened
here and both of which come from letting the floor estimate depend on the
decision it feeds:

- Let the floor rise while the gate is *open*, and a long uninterrupted stretch
  drags it up until the speaker is cut off — reproduced at ten seconds.
- Let it rise only while the gate is *shut*, and a call that opens mid-sentence
  never shuts: the floor starts below the room and nothing can lift it.

The implementation uses a minimum over a two-second window, which has no such
loop, plus a ceiling of −30 dBFS on the estimate. The ceiling is what makes the
failure one-directional: a genuinely loud room makes the gate permissive, which
costs bandwidth, and never aggressive enough to remove a voice. A minimum is
only the room if the window contains a gap, and a sustained vowel or a hum need
not contain one.

Verified by ear before being marked Active: never cutting the speaker off, and
pauses that sound like a quiet room rather than a hole, a pulse, or chopping.
