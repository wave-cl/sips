## Abstract

SIP-14's framing, plus a way to describe silence instead of transmitting it. A
frame gains a type byte; the new type carries the **level and spectral tilt of
the room's noise floor** in two bytes, and the receiver synthesises comfort noise
from it rather than replaying the codec's concealment. That lets a speaker stop
transmitting entirely between utterances — a pause costs about one packet a
second instead of forty — without the far end going dead or pulsing.

## Motivation

SIP-14 let a sender stop transmitting while not talking, and left the decision to
the codec's own detector. That detector barely engages against a real room: over
a 3.8 s pause carrying a noise floor at RMS 0.006, Opus still transmitted 37
frames of 190, 28 of them full size. The saving was about threefold where the
packet sizes suggested thirty.

SIP-14 records why the obvious remedies fail, and both failures have the same
root. **Concealment is the wrong tool for describing silence.** Attenuating the
input does not move the codec's detector, and pushes its comfort noise toward
describing digital silence — which the receiver then renders as dead air. Gating
in the application works, taking that pause from 37 frames to 4, and makes the
far end swing 80:1: concealment extrapolated from an isolated noise frame
overshoots and then decays, so the room pulses once a second.

Both are the same mistake, which SIP-14 could not avoid because it had no way to
say what silence *is*. Concealment guesses at what a lost frame contained. A
pause is not a lost frame, and guessing at it is exactly wrong. Telephony codecs
solved this decades ago: send a **silence descriptor** — the level, and enough
shape to be recognisable — and let the receiver generate noise to match. The
description is two bytes and needs sending about once a second.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. This framing occupies the **plaintext of a
SIP-12 frame**, on either carriage; the exchange sees only ciphertext.

### Frame

```
| type: u8 | timestamp: u32 | body |
```

`timestamp` is SIP-14's, unchanged: it counts 20 ms media frames from an
arbitrary origin and increments for **every** frame the sender produces,
including those it does not transmit. It wraps, and a receiver MUST handle
wrapping.

| `type` | meaning | body |
|---|---|---|
| `0x01` | audio | one codec packet |
| `0x02` | comfort | `\| level: u8 \| tilt: u8 \|` |

Any other type MUST be ignored, which leaves room to add one without a flag day.

### The comfort descriptor

`level` encodes the noise floor's RMS in half-decibels below full scale:

```
level = clamp(round(-2 × 20 × log10(rms)), 0, 254)
level = 255                                  when rms is zero
```

So 255 is digital silence, 120 is −60 dBFS, and 0 is full scale. A receiver
**MUST clamp** what it synthesises to at most −30 dBFS: comfort noise is never
loud, and a peer claiming otherwise is either broken or hostile.

`tilt` is the fraction of the frame's energy above 1 kHz, as `round(255 × f)`.
0 is a rumble, 255 is a hiss, and most rooms sit low. It exists because white
noise at the right level still sounds nothing like a room, and one byte is
enough to tell the two apart.

### What a sender transmits

A sender decides **for itself** whether a frame is speech; it MUST NOT rely on
the codec's discontinuous transmission, which does not engage reliably against
a room. How it decides is not specified — an adaptive noise floor with hangover
is the obvious approach — but it MUST NOT clip the start of speech.

- **Speech** — encode and transmit an audio frame.
- **Not speech** — transmit no audio. Transmit a **comfort** frame:
  1. on the first non-speech frame after speech, and
  2. at least once per `KEEPALIVE` thereafter. RECOMMENDED one second.

SIP-12's sequence number increments **only on transmission**; the timestamp
increments regardless. That separation is SIP-14's and is what makes a gap
classifiable.

A sender that does not implement comfort noise MAY transmit audio continuously,
and MUST NOT skip frames without describing what it skipped.

### What a receiver does

Gap classification is SIP-14's, unchanged. Given two successively received
frames `(seq_p, ts_p)` and `(seq_n, ts_n)`:

```
missing slots = ts_n - ts_p - 1
lost packets  = seq_n - seq_p - 1
```

At most `lost packets` of those slots may be treated as loss; the rest are
silence. Then, per slot:

| slot | rendering |
|---|---|
| audio frame | decode it |
| loss | decode nothing — the codec conceals |
| silence, a descriptor has been seen | **synthesise** noise at the last `level` and `tilt` |
| silence, no descriptor yet | decode nothing |
| comfort frame | adopt its `level`/`tilt`, and synthesise this slot |

A receiver **MUST NOT** render silence as digital zeros, and MUST NOT conceal
it. Zeros cut the room's noise floor off at a cliff fifty times a second, which
is audible as chopping; concealment invents speech nobody spoke.

Synthesis is not specified beyond the level clamp. Filtered white noise matched
to `tilt` is sufficient and is what the reference implementation does.

### Sounding right

Three details decide whether a pause sounds like a room or like a fault. None
is required for interoperation and all three were found by listening, after
measurements said the result was already smooth — a synthetic room is
stationary, and no real one is.

- A sender **SHOULD** describe the room from a *smoothed* estimate over the
  pause, not from the single frame its keepalive happens to land on. One 20 ms
  frame's level wanders enough that the far end otherwise steps to a slightly
  different level once a second.
- A receiver **SHOULD** glide toward a new description rather than stepping to
  it. A few hundred milliseconds is enough: fast enough to follow a room,
  slow enough to be inaudible. A step in a noise floor is a click.
- A sender's detector **SHOULD** use hysteresis — a lower threshold to stop
  than to start. With one threshold a level sitting near it makes the gate
  chatter, and each flip swaps a real room for a synthesised one. Those have
  different textures even at matched levels, and alternating them several times
  a second is audible.

## Rationale

**Why replace SIP-14 rather than extend it.** The frame needed a type byte, and
there was no unambiguous way to add one: a codec packet's first byte is already
meaningful, and a zero-length body is a legal, if useless, audio frame. Changing
the layout changes what SIP-14 means, and SIP-1 fixes a number's meaning once it
is Active. SIP-14 was Active for one day with one implementation, so replacing
it costs nothing and keeps the record honest — it was not wrong, it was not
enough.

**Why two bytes and not more.** A full spectral envelope is what a codec's own
descriptor carries, and it would sound better. Level and tilt are what can be
justified for a demo: level is what makes a pause stop being a hole, and tilt is
what stops the result sounding like tape hiss in a room that rumbles. The frame
is extensible by type if that turns out not to be enough.

**Why the sender decides, having said in SIP-14 that the codec should.** SIP-14
was right that the codec knows more about speech than an energy threshold does,
and wrong that this made it the right decider. Its detector is tuned to decide
whether *encoding* is worthwhile, not whether *transmitting* is — and against a
noise floor it keeps deciding yes. Once the receiver can synthesise the room,
the application can afford to be more aggressive than the codec, because being
wrong now costs a moment of synthesised noise instead of a hole.

**Why a level clamp is normative.** The descriptor is the one place where a peer
states, rather than demonstrates, how loud something should be. Without a clamp,
one byte set to zero is a full-scale noise burst into somebody's headphones.

## Compatibility

**Breaking, and a flag day.** A SIP-14 receiver reads this framing's type byte as
the top byte of a timestamp and its timestamps as codec data. There is no
version negotiation and none is proposed: the only implementation moves as a
whole, and SIP-14 is Replaced rather than left to be chosen between.

Nothing between a peer and the exchange changes. SIP-12 and SIP-13 are
untouched, and an exchange cannot tell which framing is in use.

## Security considerations

**Everything SIP-14 says about discontinuous transmission still applies, and
more sharply.** Packet presence is a voice-activity signal: an exchange relaying
a call learns when each party speaks, how long their turns run, and in a SIP-13
room the order of a conversation between identities it already knows. This SIP
makes a pause quieter on the wire — about one packet a second against fifty — so
speech and silence are now *more* clearly distinguishable to an observer, not
less. The content stays sealed; the pattern is plainer than before.

An implementation MUST keep continuous transmission available, and a deployment
where the pattern of who speaks when is sensitive SHOULD NOT enable this.

**A descriptor is an assertion, not a demonstration.** Every other field in this
stack is checkable — a signature verifies, a MAC1 proves a key, a codec packet
either decodes or does not. `level` is a number a peer simply states, and the
receiver acts on it by making a sound. Hence the mandatory clamp. `tilt` needs
no clamp: it selects between filters, and every value is harmless.

**A hostile peer can make a listener's room sound wrong** — claiming a hiss where
there is a rumble, or −30 dBFS of noise in a silent room. This is a peer
misbehaving in its own conversation, which it could do more simply by making
noise, and it is bounded by the clamp.

**The descriptor is inside the sealed payload**, so the exchange learns nothing
about the sender's acoustic environment from it. It sees a small packet, as it
does for a keepalive.

## Reference implementation

Shipped in [sqex](https://github.com/wave-cl/sqex)'s `sqex-voice`, on SIP-12
carriage:

- `sqex_voice::media` holds the framing, the `Comfort` descriptor, the detector
  (`Gate`) and the synthesiser (`Noise`).
- `sqex_voice::jitter` keys its buffer on the timestamp, classifies gaps, and
  carries a `Playout::Comfort` beside `Frame` and `Conceal`. Rendering happens
  in one place — `Playback::render` — which is deliberate: the previous version
  let each call site choose, and one of them rendered silence as digital zeros.
- Both the two-party call and the SIP-13 room use it. `--no-dtx` restores
  continuous transmission for the privacy reason above.

Measured on a live exchange, twelve seconds of two seconds' speech and ten of
pause against a real noise floor: **600 packets** transmitting continuously,
**182** under SIP-14, **125** under this SIP. In the end-to-end test a settled
pause costs **3 packets in 180 slots**, where SIP-14 sent about 37.

**Two ways to write a detector that mutes the speaker**, both of which happened
here and both of which come from letting the floor estimate depend on the
decision it feeds:

- Let the floor rise while the gate is *open*, and a long uninterrupted stretch
  drags it up until the speaker is cut off — reproduced at ten seconds.
- Let it rise only while the gate is *shut*, and a call that opens mid-sentence
  never shuts: the floor starts below the room and nothing can lift it.

The implementation uses a minimum over a two-second window, which has no such
loop, plus a ceiling of −30 dBFS on the estimate. The ceiling is what makes the
failure one-directional: a genuinely loud room makes the gate permissive, which
costs bandwidth, and never aggressive enough to remove a voice. A minimum is
only the room if the window contains a gap, and a sustained vowel or a hum need
not contain one.

Verified by ear before being marked Active: never cutting the speaker off, and
pauses that sound like a quiet room rather than a hole, a pulse, or chopping.
