## Abstract

SIP-5 promises a sender that what it left waits seven days for the
recipient, and promises the recipient that a fetch lost mid-way costs a
retry and never a message. Its reference implementation kept the
mailbox in memory and said so: "a restart drops undelivered mail",
"simply unfinished". Every other service that makes a promise over time
-- the log, the registry, the names, the prekeys, the backups -- keeps
it on disk. This document makes the mailbox's promise one an operator's
restart cannot break: items, their identifiers, collection records and
what a home has collected from a former home are durable for their
TTL, and an exchange that cannot keep them durably refuses to hold them
rather than holding them for a while.

## Motivation

Three deploys on 2026-09-18 restarted both exchanges, and each restart
emptied every mailbox on them. Two live runs of two documents (SIP-68,
SIP-70) lost their items between being sent and being read; the reason
was not found in either document, because it was in neither -- it was
in the one line of SIP-5's reference section that called persistence
the next step. An operator restarting a daemon to deploy a fix is doing
the ordinary thing, and a service that turns the ordinary thing into a
delivery failure for everyone who happened to have mail waiting is not
the service SIP-5 describes.

The mailbox is also, since SIP-68, the destination of a collection: the
home takes items from the former home, which deletes them, and holds
them for the account. A home that then restarts before the account
reads has lost what the former home no longer has. That is worse than
the original limitation, and this document is due before SIP-68 is
relied on.

## Specification

**Item**, **queue**, **TTL**, **collection record** and the quotas are
SIP-5's; **collected from** is SIP-68's.

### What is durable

An exchange MUST keep, across its own restarts and for the item's TTL:

1. every waiting item -- sender, recipient, time received, ephemeral
   and ciphertext -- under the identifier it was answered with;
2. every collection record -- identifier, sender, recipient, time
   received, time collected -- until the item's TTL, as SIP-5 §Collection
   is visible to the sender requires;
3. the identifier sequence, so that no identifier answered before a
   restart is answered again after it;
4. what it has collected from a former home (SIP-68): the origin, the
   identifier there, and the recipient, for the item's TTL, so a pull
   answered twice across a restart stores nothing twice.

Expiry (SIP-5's TTL) is measured from `received` as before, and applies
across a restart: an item received six days before a restart expires a
day after it.

### What is not

Nothing on the wire. SIP-5's messages, quotas and ordering are
unchanged; a client cannot tell a durable exchange from one that never
restarted, which is the point.

### An exchange that cannot keep the promise

An exchange with no durable store -- one run with state in memory only,
which SIP-8's reference implementation allows -- MUST still serve the
mailbox routes, and MUST answer `/status` (SIP-8) with a field saying
its mailbox is not durable, so an operator who reads it knows. A client
has no way to ask and is not given one: an exchange that cannot keep
mail should not be public, and SIP-56 already says what a public
exchange needs.

## Rationale

**Why now.** SIP-5 deferred persistence with no wire change needed, and
none is needed; what changed is that two later documents built on the
queue -- a home collecting it (SIP-68), a device sharing it (SIP-70) --
and both were tested against a daemon that forgot it on the next deploy.
The limitation stopped being a limitation of one service and became a
hole under two.

**Why durability is normative and not an implementation note.** A
second implementer reading SIP-5 could keep the mailbox in memory and
conform, and their users would lose mail on every restart without
knowing why. SIP-5's TTL is a promise measured in days; a promise
measured in days is not kept by a process that measures its life in
hours between deploys.

**Why the identifier sequence.** SIP-5 collection is by identifier, and
a sender's `Status` is by identifier. An exchange that restarted at
zero would hand a new item the identifier of an old one, and a sender
asking after their old message would be told about somebody else's.

**Why the SIP-68 record.** The former home deletes on `took`, so the
home is the only copy; but the home marks a hint collected only once
the whole answer is stored, and a restart between storing and marking
would pull again. Without the record the items would be stored twice.

## Compatibility

Additive on the wire, which does not change. An exchange upgraded from
an in-memory mailbox starts with an empty durable one -- what was in
memory was lost at the restart that deployed this, as at every restart
before it -- and the identifier sequence begins where the durable store
says, which is one.

## Security considerations

**Ciphertext at rest.** The exchange already held it in memory and
could not read it; on disk it still cannot. What changes is that a
copy of the disk is a copy of the queue: every waiting item for every
recipient, sealed to each, plus the metadata SIP-5 already warns about
(sender, recipient, size, time -- a social graph with timing). An
operator's disk was already the log, the registry and the prekeys, and
the same care applies; SIP-5's forward-secrecy note -- a recipient's key
compromised later opens what is still stored under it, bounded by the
TTL -- now bounds it on disk rather than in a process.

**Deletion is still not erasure.** SIP-5 said so of the collection
record; it holds of the disk. A collected item's payload is dropped, and
an exchange SHOULD overwrite rather than merely unlink where its store
lets it, and MUST NOT keep the payload past collection.

## Reference implementation

`sqex` 0.87.0. `sqexd`'s mailbox is SQLite beside the other stores
(`mailbox.db`, WAL, `synchronous = FULL`), with `mail` (id
autoincrementing, payload null once collected, collected time) and
`collected_from`; the API is unchanged, and the in-memory form is what
a memory-only deployment gets from the same code. `/status` reports
`mailbox_durable`. Tests: the store's own suite runs against a file and
an item, its record and the identifier sequence are there after the
store is opened again; a collected item's payload is gone from the file.
