## Abstract

A device keeps a copy of its store at the exchange, sealed under a key the
person holds and the exchange never sees, in the exchange's own blob store
(SIP-18) held against the account rather than a channel, and named by a
**manifest** the device signs and seals. The copy is written incrementally,
read by any device of the account -- or, after SIP-44, by the successor --
and opened only with the **backup key**, a 32-byte secret shown to the
person as words. A person with one device who loses it no longer loses
everything.

## Motivation

SIP-42 gave a person's devices each other's history, which is what a person
with two devices needs. A person with one has nobody to copy from: the
exchange keeps entries for its retention window and then does not, and the
epoch keys that open them (SIP-17) were never at the exchange at all. Lose
the device and the conversations are gone -- not sealed, gone -- and SIP-44,
which recovers the *account*, recovers a name with nothing behind it.

The exchange already stores sealed bytes it cannot read and serves them to
whoever may fetch them: that is SIP-18. What it lacks is a place to hold
bytes for an account rather than a channel, a way to say which bytes are
the current copy, and a rule for who may read a copy whose owner is gone.
This document adds those three things and nothing else. The bytes are the
client's own: a channel's history in the form SIP-42 already sends to a
sibling, so that restoring is receiving from oneself.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. Every request is bound to the
transport identity (SIP-3), resolved to a device and its account (SIP-22).

### The backup key

```
backup_key = 32 bytes, generated uniformly at random by the client, once
mk         = HKDF-SHA256(ikm = backup_key, salt = empty, info = "sqex-backup-v1 manifest", 32)
```

The person keeps `backup_key`. A client MUST show it as base58 and SHOULD
also show it as **24 words** from SIP-41's list: the 256 bits of the key
followed by the first 8 bits of `SHA-256(backup_key)`, read as 24 11-bit
indices. A client reading words back MUST check the 8 bits and refuse a
phrase that fails them. The key is not derived from the account key,
because a SIP-44 successor does not have the account key and is the reader
this document most exists for; and it is not derived from a passphrase,
because a passphrase weak enough to remember is weak enough to search when
the ciphertext is somewhere a search can be run.

### Segments

A backup is a set of **segments**, each a SIP-18 blob: chunked, sealed under
the blob's own random key, named by the hash of its ciphertext. A device
uploads a segment with SIP-18's `Begin`/`Put`/`Commit` **with `channel` set
to the account's own key**. An exchange MUST accept that from a device of
the account, reserve the upload against the account's backup quota rather
than a channel's, and store the result *held by the account*: served by
`Get` to any device of the account, and retained while the account's
manifest names it. `Attach` and `Detach` do not apply to it; the manifest is
its attachment. A `Begin` naming any other account is refused as SIP-18
refuses a non-member.

What a segment contains is by `kind`, recorded in the manifest:

| kind | contents |
|---|---|
| 0x01 | a channel's history: SIP-42 `Entries` and `Keys` messages, each `len`-prefixed as SIP-42 writes them to a stream, in `seq` order |
| 0x02 | contacts: `count: u32 | count × (account[32] | verified: u8 | label_len: u16 | label)` -- SIP-41's verified flag is the person's own finding and travels with them |
| 0x03 | client state, opaque to everything but the client that wrote it |
| 0x04 | a **held blob**: no upload; the manifest names an existing blob (an attachment SIP-18 would prune with its channel) and the exchange retains it for the account as it would a segment |

A restoring client feeds a kind-0x01 segment to the same code that receives
a sibling's SIP-42 session, with the same verification: entries by their
signatures and receipts, keys taken on the writer's word -- and the writer
was this account. Kinds it does not know it skips.

### The manifest

```
Manifest | type: u8 = 0x01 | generation: u64 | count: u32 | count × blob[32]
         | sealed_len: u32 | sealed | sig[64] |
Ask      | type: u8 = 0x02 | account[32] |
Drop     | type: u8 = 0x03 |

Held     | generation: u64 | device[32] | written: u64 | quota: u64 | used: u64
         | count: u32 | count × blob[32] | sealed_len: u32 | sealed | sig[64] |
Ack      | now: u64 |
```

`POST /backup/{write,read,drop}`.

`Manifest` is what a device writes. `generation` MUST exceed the generation
the exchange holds, or the write is refused with `stale` (SIP-21) carrying
the current generation, so two devices writing at once cannot silently drop
each other's segments: the loser reads, merges, and writes again. The
`blob` list, in the clear, is every segment and held blob this manifest
keeps; the exchange releases any blob held by the account that the new list
omits, and refuses a list naming a blob the account does not hold. `sealed`
is:

```
plain    | version: u8 = 1 | written: u64 | count: u32 | count × Segment |
Segment  | kind: u8 | blob[32] | key[32] | channel[32] | first: u64 | last: u64 |
nonce    = generation(8) || 0(4)
ad       = "sqex-backup-v1" || account[32] || generation(8)
sealed   = ChaCha20-Poly1305(mk, nonce, plain, ad)
```

`key` is the blob's SIP-18 key. `channel`, `first` and `last` say what a
kind-0x01 segment covers, so a restorer can pick the newest of overlapping
segments; they are zero for the other kinds. `sig` is the device's Ed25519
signature over `"sqex-backup-v1" || account || generation || blob list ||
sealed`, verified by the reader against the device's SIP-20 credential as
`/device/list` serves it (SIP-32) -- it says a device of this account wrote
this, which the exchange's word could not.

`Ask` names an account: the caller's own, or one the caller's account
succeeded (SIP-44), which the exchange checks against its succession
record. `Held` is the current manifest. `Drop` releases everything the
caller's account holds.

### Writing

A device writes a backup by uploading what changed since its last manifest
as new segments and writing a manifest naming both the new and the old.
It SHOULD compact -- upload one segment per channel covering everything and
drop the pieces -- when the segment count or the overlap grows; when is its
own affair. A device MAY hold blobs (kind 0x04) that messages it keeps
refer to, so that a message restored a year from now still has its picture;
it SHOULD NOT hold every blob it ever saw.

An account's backup is one, not one per device: siblings kept in step by
SIP-42 hold the same history, and a second copy would be the same bytes
under a second quota. The `generation` rule is what lets several devices
share it.

### Reading

A device that holds `backup_key` reads `Held`, verifies `sig`, opens
`sealed`, fetches each segment's chunks with SIP-18 `Get`, opens them with
the segment's key, and applies them by kind. A device that does not hold
the key can read `Held` and learn nothing from it but sizes; it SHOULD not
bother.

A **successor** (SIP-44) reads the predecessor's backup by naming the
predecessor in `Ask`. The predecessor's backup is not the successor's:
what the successor restores it writes to its own, under its own key, and
the predecessor's stays until dropped or until the exchange's retention
for succeeded accounts ends it.

### Limits

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| `sealed` in a manifest | 1 MiB |
| Segments and held blobs per manifest | 4096 |
| Backup quota per account | operator's; `Held` reports it; 512 MiB is the reference default |
| Words | 24, from SIP-41's list, with an 8-bit check |

## Rationale

**Why the exchange's blob store and not a new one.** It already chunks,
seals, names by hash, resumes, and enforces a quota; a backup needs every
one of those and nothing more. What it lacked was a holder that is not a
channel, which is one rule at `Begin`.

**Why the manifest carries the blob list in the clear.** So that the
exchange can keep what the backup needs and release what it does not,
without opening anything. The alternative -- the client detaching each
segment it stops needing -- leaves a client that crashed mid-compaction
paying for both copies until somebody notices. The exchange learns which of
the account's blobs the account keeps, and from a held channel blob, which
attachments it values; it already knew the account could fetch them.

**Why SIP-42's messages and not a new format.** Restoring is receiving
history from a device that happens to be a past self. The verification is
the same, the code is the same, and a segment written by one client is
readable by any client that speaks SIP-42.

**Why a random key shown as words, and not a passphrase.** Stated above.
The words are the person's one obligation, and they are the same
obligation SIP-44's will already gave them -- something to write down and
keep where the device is not.

**Why one backup per account.** Two devices in SIP-42 step hold one history;
the generation rule makes them one writer at a time; per-device copies
would double what the operator stores for no reader's benefit.

## Compatibility

Additive: three routes and one rule at SIP-18's `Begin`. An exchange from
before this document refuses `Begin` with the account as `channel` as a
non-member, and a client reads that as "no backup here". Nothing else moves.

## Security considerations

**The exchange holds the ciphertext and can lose it, withhold it, or serve
an old one.** It cannot read it or forge it: opening needs `backup_key`,
and `sig` ties a manifest to a device of the account. What it can do is
serve generation 40 when it holds 41. A device that wrote 41 knows, and
refuses; a device restoring from nothing does not, and cannot -- it has
the words and nothing else. Siblings that share the current generation
over SIP-42 narrow this to the case of losing every device at once.

**The words are the backup.** Anybody with them and access -- a device of
the account, the account, the successor -- opens everything. That is one
secret protecting everything the person has, and it is the design: the
alternative is a key the device holds, which is lost with the device. A
client MUST NOT store the words at the exchange, in a channel, or anywhere
a restore would need to have already succeeded to reach.

**A revoked device's manifest still verifies.** `sig` is checked against
the credential the exchange retained, which SIP-32 keeps after revocation
for exactly this. A revoked device that wrote a manifest before revocation
wrote a good one; one that writes after is refused at `write`, because it
is no longer a device of the account. A restorer SHOULD prefer a manifest
from a device that is still registered when it has a choice, and it never
has one: there is one manifest.

**Held blobs outlive their channel's retention** for as long as the account
holds them. A member removed from a channel keeps what they had, which
SIP-17 already grants; holding a blob adds nothing to that but its
survival past the exchange's pruning, at the account's own quota.

**The successor reads everything.** That is what a successor is for
(SIP-44), and the backup key is the second thing a will should be kept
with. A successor without the words has the account and an unopenable
copy, which is what they would have had without this document.
