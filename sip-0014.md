## Abstract

A framing for voice inside a SIP-12 relayed session that lets a speaker stop
transmitting while they are not talking. A four-byte media timestamp is carried
ahead of each codec packet, which separates *where audio belongs in time* from
*which packets were sent* — so a receiver can tell deliberate silence from a lost
packet, and conceal only the second. Nothing in SIP-12 changes: this lives
entirely inside the frame payload the exchange already cannot read.

## Motivation

A voice client with nothing to suppress transmission sends fifty frames a
second for as long as the call lasts, whether or not anyone is speaking. In a
two-party call that is merely wasteful. In a SIP-13 room it is quadratic: eight
people is fifty-six relayed streams, all running flat out, mostly carrying
silence — and the exchange pays for every one of them.

Opus already knows when nobody is talking. With discontinuous transmission
enabled it emits a one-byte packet for a silent frame instead of the sixty-five
a speech frame costs. Measured on the reference encoder at 24 kbit/s, 20 ms
frames:

| | bytes |
|---|---|
| speech frame | 65 |
| silent frame | 1 |

But **sending** those one-byte packets saves far less than it appears. Once the
SIP-12 datagram header, the AEAD tag, the QUIC framing and UDP/IP are counted, a
one-byte payload still costs on the order of a hundred bytes on the wire against
roughly a hundred and seventy for speech — under a factor of two. The saving
worth having comes from not sending at all, and that is what creates the problem
this SIP exists to solve.

**A gap is ambiguous.** SIP-12's sequence number is both the AEAD nonce and the
only ordering a receiver has. If a sender simply skips frames, the receiver sees
missing sequence numbers and cannot distinguish them from loss — and a jitter
buffer that treats them as loss will ask the codec to conceal, which invents
speech out of silence. The result is worse than sending the packets.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. This framing occupies the **plaintext of a
SIP-12 frame**, on either carriage; the exchange sees only ciphertext and is
unaffected.

### Frame

```
| timestamp: u32 | payload |
```

`timestamp` counts 20 ms media frames from an arbitrary origin chosen by the
sender, and increments for **every** frame the sender produces, including those
it does not transmit. `payload` is one codec packet.

Frame duration is fixed at 20 ms by this SIP; a sender using another duration
needs a different one. `timestamp` wraps, and a receiver MUST handle wrapping;
at 20 ms a `u32` lasts over two years.

Sample rate is deliberately **not** carried. Opus decodes any stream at whatever
rate the receiver chooses, so the two ends need not agree and there is nothing
to negotiate.

### What a sender transmits

A sender enables the codec's discontinuous transmission and encodes every frame.
A packet of **two bytes or fewer** is the codec's indication that it has nothing
worth sending.

A sender MUST transmit a frame when any of these hold, and MAY otherwise skip
it:

1. the packet is longer than two bytes — the speaker is talking;
2. it is the **first** short packet after a transmitted longer one — this begins
   a silent run, and carries the codec's comfort-noise description;
3. **a keepalive is due**: at least one frame per `KEEPALIVE` interval must be
   transmitted regardless. RECOMMENDED value one second.

SIP-12's sequence number increments **only on transmission**, since it counts
packets. The media timestamp increments regardless, since it counts time. That
separation is the whole mechanism.

### What a receiver does with a gap

A receiver holds packets by `timestamp` and detects loss by sequence number.
Given two successively received frames carrying `(seq_p, ts_p)` and
`(seq_n, ts_n)`:

```
missing slots = ts_n - ts_p - 1        (media frames not received)
lost packets  = seq_n - seq_p - 1      (packets that should have arrived)
```

At most `lost packets` of those missing slots may be treated as loss. The
remainder are deliberate silence.

- A slot attributed to **loss** SHOULD be concealed by the codec.
- A slot attributed to **silence** MUST NOT be concealed. Concealment
  extrapolates from what was last heard, so concealing silence invents speech
  that was never spoken.

A receiver MAY render silent slots as digital silence, but SHOULD instead let
the codec produce comfort noise from the short packet that opened the run.
Rule 2 above exists to make that possible.

### Liveness

The keepalive is what allows an application to keep treating prolonged silence
as a broken session — a live sender transmits at least once per interval even
when nobody has spoken for an hour. An implementation that concludes a session
is dead from silence MUST use a threshold comfortably above `KEEPALIVE`.

## Rationale

**Why a timestamp rather than making the sequence number mean time.** Making
SIP-12's sequence number advance across untransmitted frames would preserve
timing, but it is the AEAD nonce and the loss signal; advancing it for packets
that never existed makes every silent run look like loss. Two counters is the
answer RTP reached for the same reason, and for the same reason here.

**Why in the payload and not in SIP-12.** Timing is a property of media, not of
relaying. SIP-12 carries opaque frames for anything at all — a file transfer has
no timestamp and wants none. Putting this in the payload also keeps it
confidential: the exchange never sees the timestamp, only that a packet passed.

**Why the codec decides, and not an energy threshold.** Opus's own detector is
already tuned against speech, already handles onsets, and already produces the
comfort-noise description that rule 2 relies on. A hand-rolled gate would clip
word beginnings and have nothing to say about what silence sounds like.

**What the detector actually does, measured.** Two behaviours are worth knowing
before judging the saving, because neither is documented as a number and both
surprised the reference implementation:

- **It takes about ten frames to engage.** The first ~200 ms of a pause is still
  transmitted at full size. A short pause therefore saves much less than a long
  one, and an implementation should not expect a clean edge.
- **It refreshes periodically.** Deep inside a silence the encoder still emits a
  full-sized comfort-noise update every few hundred milliseconds, so a settled
  pause is not one packet per keepalive interval. Measured over a 1.4 s settled
  pause: 8 packets transmitted where continuous transmission would have sent 70.

Neither is a defect. Both mean the honest claim for this SIP is "a settled pause
costs well under a quarter of continuous transmission", not "a pause is free".

**Why "two bytes or fewer" rather than a flag.** It is what the codec's API
reports, needs no agreement, and a packet that short cannot be speech.

## Compatibility

This changes the plaintext both ends parse, so **both ends must implement it**.
A sender using this framing and a receiver that does not will have four bytes of
timestamp fed to its decoder as codec data, and hear nothing usable.

Nothing on the wire between a peer and the exchange changes: SIP-12 and SIP-13
are untouched, and an exchange needs no knowledge of this SIP and cannot tell
whether it is in use except by observing packet timing.

## Security considerations

**This trades privacy for bandwidth, and the trade is real.** With continuous
transmission a packet stream carries no information about speech activity: an
observer, the exchange included, sees a constant rate whatever is being said or
not said. Discontinuous transmission makes **packet presence a voice activity
signal**. An exchange relaying a call learns when each party speaks, how long
their turns are, who interrupts whom, and the rhythm of the conversation. In a
SIP-13 room it learns which members are talking and in what order, which is a
conversation graph over identities it already knows.

SIP-12 notes that frame sizes and cadence are visible to an exchange. This
sharpens that from a side channel into a clear one. The content stays sealed;
the *pattern* of speech does not.

An implementation MUST therefore keep continuous transmission available, and a
deployment where the pattern of who speaks when is sensitive SHOULD NOT enable
this. The keepalive does not meaningfully mask anything — one packet a second
against fifty is plainly distinguishable.

**Padding is not a fix here.** Constant-rate cover traffic would restore the
property, but it also restores the entire cost this SIP exists to avoid; an
implementation wanting both should simply not use discontinuous transmission.

**A malicious sender can lie about the timestamp**, claiming a large jump to
force a long silence, or a small one to compress time. It can do neither to
anyone else's stream — the frame is sealed under a key only the two peers hold —
so this is a peer misbehaving in its own conversation, which it could do more
simply by saying nothing. A receiver SHOULD bound how far a timestamp may jump
rather than allocate against it.

## Reference implementation

Shipped in [sqex](https://github.com/wave-cl/sqex)'s `sqex-voice`, on SIP-12
carriage:

- `sqex_voice::media` holds the frame layout, the sender's transmit decision and
  `classify`, which splits a gap into loss and silence. It is the whole of this
  SIP in about a hundred lines.
- `sqex_voice::jitter` keys its buffer on the **timestamp** rather than the
  sequence number, and gained a `Playout::Silence` beside `Frame` and `Conceal`.
  The distinction is enforced there: silence never reaches the codec's
  concealment path.
- Both the two-party call and the SIP-13 room use it, with a one-second
  keepalive. `--no-dtx` restores continuous transmission for the privacy reason
  in the security section.

Measured end to end through a live exchange: 200 media slots carrying a two
second pause cost **119 packets**, and the settled part of the pause cost 8
packets where 70 would otherwise have gone.

Tests cover the sender's decision (speech always sent, a run opened once,
keepalives, the timestamp advancing across frames nobody sent) and the
receiver's classification exhaustively: a pause of a hundred slots with no lost
packets concealing **nothing**; a hole in the sequence still concealing; a gap
containing both, with concealment bounded by the packets actually missing; and
timestamp wrapping not producing a two-year gap. End to end, a real pause
through a real exchange comes out quiet — RMS below 0.02 — with the speech
either side of it intact.
