## Abstract

One request that answers what a device that has been away needs: for every
channel the device names, the entries since a cursor it supplies and the
SIP-17 envelopes waiting for it there; the channels it did not name that
its account is in; and the prekey count it would otherwise ask for on its
own -- under a byte budget, in the order the device asked. Nothing here is a
new authority. Every part of the answer is exactly what the route that
owns it would have said to the same caller at the same moment, and the
route stays the authority. What changes is the number of round trips a
wake window costs: one, rather than one per channel.

## Motivation

SIP-47 gives a phone a budget it does not choose -- tens of seconds -- and a
fixed order of work inside it: connect, subscribe, register, top up, then
fetch what is new in every channel that moved, open it, write it, and only
then say anything. Count the requests. After the handshake: subscribe
(SIP-30), register the endpoint (SIP-45), count prekeys (SIP-23), list
memberships (SIP-16's `Mine`), and then for each channel that moved a
fetch (SIP-16) and, for any epoch the device does not yet hold, a key
collection (SIP-17). A person in eight conversations who was away for an
hour makes some twenty requests, one after another, each waiting for the
one before.

On a desktop that is nothing. On a phone the radio has just been promoted
from idle by the wake itself, and a round trip on a cellular network is a
hundred to three hundred milliseconds; twenty of them in sequence is
several seconds of a window whose length the platform decides and can end
early. The sqex-chat client measured 29 requests over a 60 s idle period
after SIP-30, and called it a success; a phone makes those 29 in the first
second, serially, on every wake, and the second half of them is the half
that opens the messages.

SIP-30 cannot carry the answer. Its central constraint is that a frame
names what changed and never carries it, so that the fetch routes remain
the sole authority on membership, retention and visibility -- and that
constraint is right. This document does not touch the stream. It adds one
**request**, made by a client that has just subscribed, whose answer is
composed from the existing routes' answers under their existing rules.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. The caller MUST have advertised an
identity (SIP-3); the exchange MUST answer `403` otherwise, as `Fetch` does.

### The request

```
Catchup | type: u8 = 0x01 | budget: u32 | count: u16
        | count × (channel[32] || since: u64 || since_epoch: u32) |
```

`POST /channel/catchup`. `budget` is how many bytes of entries and
envelopes the caller wants at most; an exchange MUST clamp it to
`MAX_CATCHUP_BYTES`. `count` is at most `MAX_NAMED`; a request naming more
MUST be refused as malformed. Each named channel carries the caller's
`since` (SIP-16's `Fetch` semantics: entries with `seq` greater than it) and
`since_epoch` (SIP-17's `Get` semantics: envelopes with `to_epoch` at or
above it).

**The order is the caller's.** An exchange MUST answer the channels in the
order they were named and MUST spend the budget in that order. The device
knows what matters -- the channel that rang, then the newest -- and the
exchange does not.

### The answer

```
CaughtUp | now: u64 | prekeys: u16 | count: u16 | count × Caught
         | unnamed: u16 | unnamed × (channel[32] || last: u64) |

Caught   | channel[32] | status: u8 | more: u8
         | fetched_len: u32 | fetched | got_len: u32 | got |
```

`now` is the exchange's clock. `prekeys` is SIP-23's `one_time` for the
calling device, exactly as `Count` would answer it.

`Caught` is answered once per named channel, in order. `status` is:

| status | meaning |
|---|---|
| `0x00` | read: `fetched` and `got` are the replies below |
| `0x01` | the caller may not read it, or it does not exist -- **one value for both**, as SIP-24's door rule requires; `fetched` and `got` are empty |
| `0x02` | not looked at: the budget ran out before it. Nothing is claimed about it either way; `fetched` and `got` are empty, `more` is `0x01` |

**`fetched` is a SIP-16 `Entries` reply and `got` is a SIP-17 `Got`
reply, as bytes.** Not fields copied out of them -- the replies themselves,
length-prefixed, so that "what the route would have said" is literal and a
client decodes them with the decoders it already has. For a channel with
`status = 0x00`:

- `fetched` MUST be byte-for-byte what `Fetch { channel, since,
  wait_secs: 0 }` without receipts would return to this caller at this
  moment, except that its entries MAY be cut short at the budget (oldest
  kept), in which case `more` is `0x01`;
- `got` MUST be byte-for-byte what `Get { channel, since_epoch }` would
  return to this caller, **in full** -- envelopes are small, and a device
  with an entry it cannot open has nothing. A channel with no keys to give
  (a public one) has an empty `Got`, which is an answer.

`more` is `0x01` when this channel has entries the answer does not carry:
because SIP-16's `MAX_BATCH` bound the batch, because the budget cut it, or
both. `first` and `last` are inside `fetched`, where SIP-16 put them, and
mean what they mean there: a `since` below `first` is a gap the device
cannot fill and MUST show.

**Composition, not authority.** An exchange MUST answer this route from the
same code that answers `Fetch`, `Get`, `Count` and `Mine`, and MUST NOT let
it disclose anything those would not: retention, the reset sequence space,
visibility and blocking all apply because the same functions apply them. An
implementation that answered it from anything else is one that will
disagree with them.

**The budget.** Spent in request order. A channel's `got` is counted first
and served whole; its entries are then added oldest first while the next
still fits. The channel that does not fit is answered with what fit and
`more`; every channel after it is answered `0x02`.

**Unnamed channels.** After the named channels, the exchange lists every
channel the caller's account is presently in that the request did not
name, with its `last`, up to `MAX_UNNAMED` -- the same set `Mine` would
answer, and no other. A device that has just been paired names nothing and
learns everything here; a device that was invited to something while it
slept learns that here rather than by listing.

**No waiting.** There is no `wait_secs`. A device catching up wants what is
there; what is coming, the stream it has already opened will say.

### What the device does with it

The rule from SIP-47 stands and is restated because this route makes it
easier to break: the device MUST write what it received to its store before
it composes any notification. This answer can carry an hour's messages and
the envelopes that open them in one body, and a device that opened the
envelopes, composed, and was suspended before writing has spent prekeys on
messages nothing can open again.

A device MUST still subscribe (SIP-30) before it catches up, for SIP-30's
reason: an event landing during the catch-up is queued behind the
subscription rather than lost between the two.

### Limits

| Limit | Value |
|---|---|
| `MAX_NAMED` | 256 channels per request |
| `MAX_UNNAMED` | 256 channels per answer |
| `MAX_CATCHUP_BYTES` | 1 MiB |
| Entries per channel | SIP-16's `MAX_BATCH` |

## Rationale

**Why not put payloads on the stream.** Because SIP-30 was right. A frame
that carries an entry is a second implementation of every authorization
rule, and the two drift. This is a request, and a request is answered by
the code that owns the answer.

**Why the replies ride inside as bytes.** The first draft copied `first`,
`last`, an epoch and the entries into fields of its own, and the moment it
was built the question was which of two encoders was the authority. Two
length-prefixed replies of the existing shapes make "what the route would
have said" a byte comparison rather than a paragraph, and give a client
nothing new to decode.

**Why the caller orders it.** The exchange could order by recency, and
would get it wrong for the one case that matters: a phone woken by a ring
wants the ringing channel first, whatever else moved. Only the device knows
why it woke.

**Why envelopes ride along, and in full.** An entry sealed under an epoch
the device has not collected is bytes. Without the envelopes a device
fetches, discovers it cannot open, asks for keys, and has spent two round
trips to learn what one would have carried. Envelopes are a few hundred
bytes each and there is at most one per epoch per device, so serving them
whole costs nothing the budget need protect against.

**Why a third status.** A channel the budget never reached is neither
readable nor absent as far as this answer knows, and saying either would
be a claim nobody checked. `0x02` says exactly that and nothing else.

**Why the prekey count is here.** Because it is the one thing SIP-47's
connect order asks for that is not about a channel, and the device decides
whether to publish from it. The publish itself remains its own request --
it is a write, and only when needed.

**Why unnamed channels are listed, not fetched.** A device names what it
holds; what it does not hold it has no `since` for, and fetching from zero
into a budget it did not plan would starve the channels it did name. It
learns they exist, with `last`, and names them next time.

**Why a byte budget rather than a count.** A phone on a metered network
cares about bytes; a count of entries says nothing about them, since a
body can be a word or 32 KiB. `MAX_BATCH` still applies per channel, so a
single busy channel cannot take the whole budget in one answer either.

**Why no waiting.** `Fetch` holds a request open because a desktop client
polling for the next message wants to be told the moment it lands. A
woken device is not waiting for the next message; it is collecting the
ones that already landed, and the stream it holds will say when more do.
A held catch-up would spend the window doing nothing.

**Rejected: a digest the exchange composes** -- "you have N messages in M
channels" as one small answer. It would be exactly the kind of summary the
exchange can compose only for public channels, since it cannot read a
sealed body; and a device then fetches anyway. The count is already in
SIP-30's `last_seq` and in `last` here.

## Compatibility

Additive: one route. An exchange from before this document answers it
with SIP-21's `not_found`, and a client falls back to the individual
routes, which is what it did. Nothing about those routes, the stream, or
any stored format changes. The answer carries the existing replies as
bytes, so a client that can decode `Entries` and `Got` can decode this.

## Security considerations

**Nothing new is disclosed.** Every byte of an answer is a byte the
caller could have obtained by asking the composed routes, and the exchange
is required to answer from their code rather than its own. A channel the
caller may not read is answered as one that does not exist, with the same
bytes. The list of unnamed channels is `Mine`, which the caller may always
ask.

**Work.** A request names at most `MAX_NAMED` channels and receives at most
`MAX_CATCHUP_BYTES`, so the cost of answering one is bounded by the same
numbers that bound the routes it composes, multiplied by a constant. An
exchange MAY rate-limit the route per connection as it does `Fetch`.

**Signals are consumed.** A `Fetch` delivers a channel's pending signals
to the caller at most once, and so does the `fetched` reply here; a client
that catches up and then fetches will not see them twice, which is what
`Fetch` alone promised.

**The budget is not a privacy control.** It bounds transfer, and a device
that wants less disclosed to the network chooses what to name. What the
network sees is what it saw for `Fetch`: sizes and timing.

## Reference implementation

`sqex-proto::catchup` holds the layouts; `sqexd::catchup::answer` is
composed from the same `fetch`, `get_keys`, `mine` and `count` functions
the four routes call, and is served at `POST /channel/catchup`;
`sqex_chat::Chat::catchup` decodes the two inner replies with the decoders
`Fetch` and `Get` already use and falls back to them on `not_found`. The
tests compare each channel's `fetched` and `got` against the routes byte
for byte. `sigil-chat`'s session makes its first reconcile one catch-up,
falling back to the sweep on `not_found`; `sigil-phone`'s wake window
inherits that. On sqex's main after 0.69.0; the tag will be cited here
when it is cut.
