## Abstract

When an account moves home (SIP-59), its former exchange refuses new
mail for it with `moved`, so a sender learns where to go -- and keeps
what was already queued, "until it expires by its own rule or the
account drops it". The account drops it by collecting at the old
exchange, which a client that followed its Move to the new home never
visits again; the mail waits seven days and is gone. This document has
the **new home collect it**: once, on the account's Move, over the
peering link, exactly as the account itself would -- list, take, delete
-- and serve it from its own mailbox with the sender and the time the
former home observed. Nothing is asserted twice: the former home's
observation travels as the former home's, the item stays sealed to the
account, and the sender's `Status` at the former home says `collected`
as SIP-5 promises.

Two more things an account keeps at an exchange follow it the same
way. Its **backup** (SIP-48): the manifest as the former home holds it
and every blob it names, collected once on the Move and stored at the
home exactly as the device wrote them, released at the former home once
held; a backup the account has already written at its new home stands,
and the old one is released without being copied. And its devices'
**wake registrations** (SIP-45), which are *copied*, not taken: the home
holds them so that what arrives there wakes a device that never
connected, and the former home keeps its own, since it goes on ordering
the channels the device is in and waking for them. All three are served
to one party -- the recorded home, by the account's own signed Move --
and nothing is asserted twice.

## Motivation

SIP-59 chose to refuse new mail at a former home rather than forward it,
on the ground that a mailbox item forwarded is one exchange's assertion
of who sent it made twice (§Rationale). That holds for a send that
arrives after the Move: the sender is told the home and sends there. It
does not reach the items that arrived before the Move -- sent honestly
to the account's exchange of the day, observed by it, queued by it, and
then stranded by an act of the recipient's the sender never saw. The
SIP-59 live run left one such item in the author's own mailbox at
trunk.exchange, where it is still waiting.

SIP-5's model already contains the answer. A mailbox belongs to whoever
can connect as its key, and collection is list, fetch, delete by id.
SIP-59 gave the account's home the standing to act for the account at
its former exchanges -- the acts-for gate, opened by the account's own
signature -- for the channels the account is in. A queue of mail for the
account is the same shape: what the account could collect, its home may
collect for it, and what the home then holds is the same sealed item
with the same observation attached, in the place the account now reads.

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-79.* The case SIP-48 exists for is the person with one device. The case
SIP-59 exists for is the exchange that is going away. Put them together:
a person whose exchange is closing moves their account, from the one
device, to a new home; the client writes its next backup there when it
next backs up -- which is on its own schedule, not the Move's; the
device is lost in between. The person holds the 24 words SIP-48 gave
them, sets up a new device, follows the account to its home (SIP-59
tells anyone who asks where that is), and asks for the backup. The home
answers generation 0: nothing held. The backup is at the former home,
which may by now be gone, and which nothing the person holds points at.

Nothing in that sequence is a fault of any one document. SIP-48 writes
where the device is; SIP-59 says what is not carried; the client backs
up when it backs up. The gap is between them, and the mail case above showed
its shape: what the account could collect at its former home,
its home may collect for it, over the standing SIP-59's Move confers.
A backup is the same shape as a queue of mail -- sealed bytes the
exchange cannot read, held for an account, that the account would
collect and re-place itself if it were online at both -- and the
reasons given above for the home pulling rather than the client
carrying hold unchanged.

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-84.* A registration is a device's standing request: *post here when there
is something for me*. It is made at one exchange because SIP-45 gave
it nowhere else to go, and it is honoured by the exchange that sees
the event -- the origin of the channel, the home that holds the mail.
After a Move there are two exchanges that see events for the account:
the former home, for the channels it orders, and the home, for its own
channels, for mail (collected there, above), for calls that ring
there. The former home holds the registration and wakes. The home
holds nothing and does not.

The device would fix this itself on its next run: sigil's phone client
registers on every connect, and the former home tells it `moved`. The
gap is the interval in between, and it is exactly the interval SIP-45
exists for -- the device is asleep, and the thing that would wake it
is at an exchange that does not know where to post.

## Specification

All integers are unsigned big-endian. **Home**, **former home**, **Move**
and **origin hint** are SIP-59's; **peering route** and its uniform
refusal are SIP-35's; **item**, **sender**, **received** and
**collected** are SIP-5's. **Manifest**, **segment**, **held
blob**, **generation** and `Held` are SIP-48's; **blob**, **chunk** and
`PulledBlob` are SIP-18's and SIP-43's; **endpoint**, **register**,
`MAX_TTL`, `MAX_ENDPOINT` and an **acceptable** endpoint are SIP-45's.

### Collecting mail

```
PullMail | type: u8 = 0x12 | account[32] |
Mail     | now: u64 | count: u8 | count × Item |
Item     | id: u64 | sender[32] | received: u64 | ephemeral[32] | len: u32 | ciphertext |
TookMail | type: u8 = 0x13 | account[32] | count: u8 | count × id: u64 |
```

`POST /peer/mailbox` is a peering route, served to a peer that is the
**recorded home of `account`** by the account's signed Move (SIP-59's
acts-for gate, the home case only -- an operator's `for` list does not
confer a mailbox), and refused uniformly to everyone else. It answers
every item waiting for `account`, oldest first, as SIP-5's `Listing`
orders them, each with what the former home observed: the sender's
identity as it connected, and when. `count` is at most SIP-5's messages
per recipient (64). Nothing is removed by asking.

`POST /peer/mailbox/took`, gated the same way, names the ids the home
has stored; the former home deletes each as SIP-5 deletes on collection
-- the payload dropped, the record kept, `collected` set to now -- and
answers SIP-16's `Ack`. An id not waiting for `account` is ignored.
Asking again after this answers the rest, so a home that stored some
and could not store others takes what it could and leaves the rest
where they were.

### What the home does with mail

When an account's Move names this exchange as home and carries origin
hints (SIP-59 `Moving.origins`; SIP-60 `/peer/invited` adds to them),
the home asks `/peer/mailbox` at **each hinted origin once** -- the
account's former home is among them, since a client presenting a Move
lists the exchange it is at -- after the Move is carried there
(`/peer/moved`), and stores each item in its own mailbox for the
account:

- as an item **received at the time the former home observed**, with
  the **sender the former home observed**, both taken as they came:
  the home's mailbox reports them on `List` and `Fetch` unchanged, and
  a client sees the item as it would have at the former home;
- subject to SIP-5's quotas at the home: an item that would exceed the
  account's count or bytes is not stored and not acknowledged;
- once per `(origin, id)`: an item the home already holds from that
  origin is not stored twice, and is acknowledged again.

It then sends `/peer/mailbox/took` with the ids it stored, and marks
the hint as collected. A pull that fails -- the origin unreachable, the
route refused, an origin from before §Collecting mail (0.85.0) -- is
tried again on
the home's next cycle for that origin (SIP-59's interval), and the mark
is set only on a pull that answered. An origin that is not a former
home answers no items, and is marked all the same.

`Status` (SIP-5) at the former home answers the sender `collected` with
the time the home took the item; the former home does not say by whom,
and SIP-5's `StatusReply` has no field for it.

### Collecting the backup

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-79.* ```
PullBackup     | type: u8 = 0x15 | account[32] |
               → Held (SIP-48), generation 0 for nothing held
PullBackupBlob | type: u8 = 0x16 | account[32] | blob[32] | chunk: u32 |
               → PulledBlob (SIP-43): the chunk's sealed bytes
TookBackup     | type: u8 = 0x17 | account[32] | generation: u64 |
               → Ack (SIP-16)
```

`POST /peer/backup`, `POST /peer/backup/blob` and `POST /peer/backup/took`
are peering routes, served to a peer that is the **recorded home of
`account`** by the account's signed Move -- the gate of §Collecting mail, the
home case only; an operator's `for` list confers no backup -- and refused
uniformly to everyone else.

`/peer/backup` answers `Held` exactly as `/backup/read` would answer a
device of the account: the manifest's generation, the device that wrote
it, when, the blob list in the clear, `sealed` and `sig`, all as the
device wrote them. `quota` and `used` are the former home's and carry
no meaning at the home. Nothing is removed by asking.

`/peer/backup/blob` answers one chunk of a blob the account holds
(SIP-48: attached to the account itself, whether as a segment or a held
blob), or refuses uniformly for a blob it does not. `chunk` is an index;
`BLOB_LIST` is not served here, since the manifest is the list.

`/peer/backup/took` names the generation the home stored. The former
home releases the manifest and every blob the account holds **if the
generation named is the one it holds** -- the manifest row, the
account's attachments, and each blob that nothing else attaches, as
SIP-48's `Drop` releases them -- and answers `Ack`. A generation that is
not the one held releases nothing and answers `Ack` all the same: it
means the home stored an answer this exchange no longer gives, which
cannot happen after a Move (§Rationale) and is not worth a refusal
where it does.

### What the home does with the backup

When an account's Move names this exchange as home and carries origin
hints, the home asks `/peer/backup` at **each hinted origin once**,
after the Move is carried there (`/peer/moved`) and beside the mail
pull, and does with the answer one of three things:

- **Generation 0**: nothing to collect. The hint is marked.
- **A backup the home already holds for the account, of any
  generation**: the account, or a device of it, wrote at its home after
  the Move, which is the newer state by the account's own act. The
  answer is not stored. The home sends `/peer/backup/took` with the
  generation the former home answered, so the former home releases what
  it holds, and marks the hint.
- **Otherwise**: the home fetches every blob the manifest names with
  `/peer/backup/blob`, chunk by chunk, and keeps a blob only if its
  chunks hash to its name (SIP-18; the check SIP-43 makes on every
  pulled blob). When **every** blob is held, it stores the manifest for
  the account -- generation, writing device, `written` as the former
  home reported it, blob list, `sealed`, `sig`, all unchanged -- as a
  device's `/backup/write` would have, sends `/peer/backup/took` with
  that generation, and marks the hint. A device of the account reading
  `/backup/read` at the home then sees what it would have seen at the
  former home, and its next write must exceed that generation, as
  SIP-48 already requires.

The blobs count against the account's backup quota at the home as a
device's upload would. A collection that would exceed it is **not
stored, not taken, and said once where the operator looks**; the hint
is marked, and the backup stays at the former home, readable there by
the account and its successor as before. A pull that fails -- the origin
unreachable, the route refused, an origin from before §Collecting the backup
(0.97.0), a
blob that could not be had whole -- leaves the hint unmarked and is
tried again on the home's next cycle for that origin (SIP-59's
interval); blobs already held are kept and not fetched twice. An origin
that is not a former home answers generation 0 and is marked all the
same.

A manifest the home stores this way is served by `/backup/read` to the
account and (SIP-44) to its successor, and released by `/backup/drop`
or by a later manifest that omits its blobs, exactly as one a device
wrote. The home verifies nothing of `sig` -- SIP-48 makes that the
reader's -- and nothing of `sealed`, which it cannot open.

### Collecting wakes

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-84.* ```
PullWakes | type: u8 = 0x18 | account[32] |
Wakes     | now: u64 | count: u8 | count × ( device[32] | until: u64 | ep_len: u16 | endpoint ) |
```

`POST /peer/wakes` is a peering route, served to a peer that is the
**recorded home of `account`** by the account's signed Move -- the gate
of §Collecting mail, the home case only -- and refused uniformly to everyone else. It
answers every live registration of the account's devices (SIP-22's
registry here) and of the account key itself: the device, when the
registration expires, and the endpoint, as the device gave them.
`count` is at most SIP-22's devices per account plus one. Nothing is
removed by asking, and nothing is removed afterwards: a former home
keeps its registrations, for the reason in §Rationale.

### What the home does with wakes

When an account's Move names this exchange as home and carries origin
hints, the home asks `/peer/wakes` at **each hinted origin once**,
after the Move is carried there and beside the mail pull, and
for each registration answered:

- skips one that has expired, and clamps `until` to `now + MAX_TTL`;
- skips one whose endpoint is not **acceptable** by this home's own
  policy (SIP-45: an `https://` address, or a loopback `http://` one
  where the operator allowed it) -- the former home's policy admitted
  it, and this home's is the one that governs what this home posts to;
- otherwise records it for the device, **held for the account**, as
  SIP-45's `register` would have, except that a registration the device
  has already made here since the Move stands and is not replaced: the
  device's own word at its home is the newer.

The home then marks the hint. A pull that fails -- the origin
unreachable, refusing, or from before §Collecting wakes (0.100.0) --
leaves the hint for the next cycle, as §What the home does with mail has
it.

### Waking

A registration held for an account is consulted exactly as one the
device made here (SIP-45 §Waking): when something for the account
arrives at this exchange, each of the account's devices with a live
registration is posted to, at most once per `WAKE_MIN_SECS` unless
urgent, and one the distributor answers `404` or `410` for is
forgotten. A device the home's registry does not list -- one that
registered at the former home and has not yet connected here -- is
woken by the registration held for the account, which is the case
§Collecting wakes exists for.

### Limits

**Mail.** `Mail` carries at most 64 items and, by SIP-5's byte quota, at most
1 MiB of ciphertext plus headers. `TookMail` names at most 64 ids.

**The backup.** `PullBackupBlob` is one chunk per request, SIP-18's chunk size. A
manifest names at most SIP-48's 4096 blobs, so a collection is bounded
by the account's backup quota at the former home, which is what the
home will hold. The home fetches one account's backup at a time.

**Wakes.** `Wakes` carries at most `MAX_DEVICES + 1` registrations of at most
`MAX_ENDPOINT` bytes each. A home keeps at most one registration per
device, as SIP-45 does.

## Rationale

**Why the home pulls and the former home does not push.** SIP-59's
reason for not forwarding was that a forwarded item is an assertion
made twice. A pull keeps every assertion where it was made: the former
home says "I observed this sender at this time", once, in answer to the
party the account authorised to collect; the home stores that
observation as the former home's and adds none of its own -- `received`
is not the time the home stored it. That is collection, which SIP-5
lets the mailbox's owner delegate by the plain fact of connecting as the
key, and SIP-59 lets the owner delegate to a home by signature.

**Why once per hint, and not on every cycle.** After the Move the
former home refuses new mail for the account, so its queue can only
shrink. One answered pull is the whole of it. Retrying an unanswered one
is the only reason to look twice.

**Why the record stays at the former home.** SIP-5 promises the sender
that collection is visible to them, for the item's TTL, at the exchange
they sent to. A sender who asks the former home is told `collected`,
truthfully: the account's home collected it. Telling them where it went
would disclose the account's home to anyone who ever sent it mail, which
SIP-59 discloses to identified callers anyway but which a status reply
need not repeat.

**Why an operator's `for` list does not confer a mailbox.** SIP-35's
acts-for peer copies what the accounts may read, on the operator's
word; SIP-59 said the stronger form is the account's own signature and
made that the home. A mailbox is the account's private queue, sealed to
its key, and moving it should rest on the account's signature and not
an operator's configuration. A configured peer that is also the signed
home qualifies as the home.

**Why not carry mail with the Move itself.** The client presenting the
Move could collect at the old exchange first and re-send to the new.
That makes every item the client's assertion instead of the former
home's, loses `received`, and needs the client online at both -- which
the account that moved because its old exchange was going away may not
manage. The home is already going to the origin for the channels.

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-79:*

**Why the home pulls and the former home does not push.** The mail
reason, unchanged: a pull keeps every fact where it was made. The
former home serves what the device wrote, once, to the party the
account's signature authorised; the home stores it as the device's and
adds nothing. `written` is the former home's report of when the device
wrote, not when the home collected.

**Why a backup the home already holds wins, at any generation.** A
device that wrote at the home after the Move started from generation 0
there and wrote 1; the former home holds, say, 37. Comparing
generations would have the home replace the account's newest backup
with an older one. The generation is SIP-48's rule for two devices
writing at *one* exchange; across two exchanges the only order that
holds is the Move itself: everything at the former home is from before
it, everything at the home is from after. The home's copy is newer by
construction, and the account's own write is the account saying so.

**Why the former home releases, and the successor case.** SIP-48 lets
a successor read the predecessor's backup where it is, and keeps it
"until dropped or until the exchange's retention for succeeded accounts
ends it". After the collection it is at the home, where the successor
-- who followed the account there to succeed it at all -- asks. A copy
left at the former home would be a second reader's target that
diverges from the first the moment the account writes again, and a
quota spent on bytes SIP-59 says the account no longer reads there.
The former home refuses writes for the account after the Move, so
what it holds can only be collected or dropped; one answered
collection is the whole of it.

**Why `took` names a generation.** So that a release is of the thing
the home stored and not of whatever is there. After a Move the former
home's copy cannot change, so the check never fails in practice; it
costs eight bytes and turns an impossible race into a no-op instead of
a loss.

**Why over quota gives up rather than retrying.** A quota does not
change on its own. Retrying each cycle is one refused fetch per cycle
forever, which SIP-78 removed for limits; the
operator who raises the quota can have the home ask again by the means
SIP-59 already gives (a fresh hint). The backup is not lost: it is
where it was, and the account can still read it there, which is what
it could do before the collection.

**Why the home does not verify `sig`.** It could: the writing device's
credential is in its registry once the device registers, or at the
former home's `/device/list`. But SIP-48 placed verification with the
reader on purpose -- the exchange's word about a manifest is worth
nothing to a restorer who has the device's -- and an exchange that
verified would have to decide what to do with a manifest that failed,
which is to refuse to carry the only copy of something. The reader
decides; the home carries.

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-84:*

**Why copy and not take.** Mail and a backup are the account's, held
in one place, read in one place; the sections above move them. A
registration is the device's request to *whoever sees an event for
it*, and after a Move two exchanges do: the former home still orders
the channels the device is in (SIP-59: "the exchange you left goes on
serving the conversations that live there"), and it is the party that
sees a post there first. Taking the registration from it would mean a
post at the former home wakes nobody until the home pulls the copy and
notices -- which the reference home does not do for pulled entries at
all, and which would be a regression from today. So both hold it, each
wakes for what it sees, and a device woken twice for one event is a
device woken.

**Why the home's endpoint policy.** The endpoint is where *this* home
will post. A former home configured to admit loopback `http://` for
its own tests would otherwise hand the home an address the home's
operator never agreed to post to.

**Why the device's own registration wins.** The device may have run at
the home between the Move and the collection -- the Move made from one
device, the phone connecting an hour later -- and what it registered
there is newer than what the former home held. The backup makes the same
choice above, for the same reason.

**Why not carry it with the Move.** The client making the Move may not
be the device: the case is a phone asleep while a laptop moves the
account. And the laptop does not hold the phone's endpoint; SIP-45 has
a registration "never served back".

## Compatibility

Additive. A former home from before §Collecting mail (0.85.0) refuses
`/peer/mailbox` as it refuses any unknown peering route; the home marks
nothing and tries again each cycle until the origin is upgraded or the
hint is gone, at the cost of one refused request per cycle. A home from
before it never asks, and the items expire at the former
home as they do today. No client change: the mailbox client lists at
its exchange, and the items appear there.

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-79.* Additive. A former home from before §Collecting the backup (0.97.0) refuses the
routes as
it refuses any unknown peering route; the home marks nothing and asks
again each cycle until the origin is upgraded or the hint is gone, one
refused request per cycle, as the mail pull does. A home from before
it never asks, and the backup stays at the former
home, where the account can read it as today. No client change: a
device reads `/backup/read` at its home and finds what it wrote at the
former home, and its next write exceeds that generation as it already
must. A client that wrote at the new home before the collection sees
its own backup, untouched.

A successor (SIP-44) asking at a former home after the collection is
told generation 0; it asks at the home, which SIP-59 names to anyone
who asks. The succession record is where SIP-44 put it and is not
moved by the collection.

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-84.* Additive. A former home from before §Collecting wakes (0.100.0) refuses
`/peer/wakes` as any unknown peering route; the home tries again each
cycle until the origin is upgraded or the hint is gone, one refused
request per cycle, as the mail pull does. A home from before it
never asks, and the device registers on its next run as today. No
client change.

## Security considerations

**Who can collect.** Only the exchange the account's own signature
names as its home, which is the exchange the account reads at and the
party SIP-59 already lets pull every channel the account is in. A
stranger, a listed peer, an operator-configured acts-for peer: refused
uniformly, as an existence oracle would otherwise be created for the
account's queue.

**What the home learns.** The senders and times of the account's
waiting mail at its former home -- the same facts the former home holds
and the account would see on listing. The items stay sealed to the
account (SIP-5 §Sealing); neither exchange reads them. The home holds
the queue it will serve anyway.

**What the former home learns.** That the home collected, and when --
which it knew it could, from the Move.

**Sender provenance is unchanged.** SIP-5 says the sender is an
observation and not a cryptographic fact; here it is the former home's
observation, served by the home, and a client that trusts it trusts
the former home exactly as it did before the Move. A home that altered
the sender would be lying about another exchange's observation, which
it could also do about its own; SIP-5's remedy -- sign inside the sealed
payload -- is unchanged.

**A stale Move.** SIP-59's `record_move` refuses an earlier `issued`
over a later one, so an account that moved on again is collected for by
its current home only; a former home that has since been named home
again holds the items itself.

*Added 2026-09-19, was SIP-79.* **Who can collect.** Only the exchange the account's own signature
names as its home -- the exchange the account reads at, and the party
SIP-59 already lets pull every channel the account is in and
§Collecting mail lets collect its mail. A stranger, a listed peer, an operator's
acts-for peer: refused uniformly, so that neither the existence of a
backup nor its size is disclosed to anyone the account did not name.

**What the home learns.** What the former home held for the account:
the manifest's clear fields (generation, device, when, the blob list)
and the sealed bytes, which it cannot open -- SIP-48's `mk` is derived
from a key the exchange never sees. These are the facts the home will
hold and serve anyway once the device next writes there.

**What the former home learns.** That the home collected, and which
generation -- which it knew it could, from the Move.

**Integrity.** A blob is kept only if its chunks hash to its name; a
manifest is stored only when every blob it names is held; `sig` travels
unchanged and the reader verifies it against the writing device's
credential, so a home that altered anything would be caught by the one
party that matters. A former home that served a manifest it did not
hold could equally have served it to the device; nothing here extends
what a former home could already do.

**A stale Move.** SIP-59's `record_move` refuses an earlier `issued`
over a later one, so an account that moved on again is collected for by
its current home only. A former home named home again holds the backup
itself and asks nobody.

**Denial.** A former home that answers a manifest naming blobs it then
refuses to serve leaves the home retrying one pull per cycle and
holding the blobs it did get, bounded by the account's quota at the
home; the hint is the account's own and expires with SIP-59's Move
handling as any hint does. It cannot make the home store a manifest it
cannot satisfy.

*Added 2026-09-20, was SIP-84.* **What is served, and to whom.** SIP-45 kept a registration "never
served back": an endpoint names a push distributor and, through it, a
device. This document serves it to one party -- the exchange the
account's own signature names as its home, which the account has
already told where it lives and which will hold the device's next
registration anyway -- and to nobody else, uniformly. A stranger, a
listed peer, an operator's acts-for peer learn nothing.

**What the home learns.** Where to post for the account's devices,
which it will learn from the devices themselves on their next run.

**What the home can do with it.** Post to the endpoint, which is what
the device asked for; SIP-45's distributor learns nothing from the
post but that there is something (SIP-45 §Security considerations), and the same
holds whichever exchange posts.

**Denial.** An account can hint any origin; SIP-78 §Backing off bounds what
a home tries. A former home can answer registrations for devices the account
never had; the home posts to their endpoints when something arrives
for the account, bounded by `MAX_DEVICES + 1` per account and
`WAKE_MIN_SECS`, and a distributor that does not know the device
answers `404`, on which the home forgets it.

## Reference implementation

`sqex` 0.85.0; 0.97.0 (the backup); 0.100.0, 0.100.1 (wakes).
`sqex-proto::peer::{PullMail, Mail, Item, TookMail}`;
`sqexd`: `/peer/mailbox` and `/peer/mailbox/took` gated by

**The backup** (§Collecting the backup, sqex 0.97.0). `sqex-proto::peer::{PullBackup, PullBackupBlob,
TookBackup}`; `sqexd`: `/peer/backup`, `/peer/backup/blob` and
`/peer/backup/took` gated by `Registry::home_of` as the mailbox routes
are, `Channels::{pull_backup_blob, holds_backup_blob, store_backup_blob,
import_backup, backup_generation, release_backup_if}`, `home_origin`
gains a `backup_collected` mark, and the SIP-59 home task's
`collect_backup` runs beside the mail pull, once per hinted origin.
Tests (`backup_home_flow`): a backup written at X before the Move is
read at H with the writing device, time, blob list, sealed bytes and
signature X held, its segment served at H and counted against the
account's quota there, X answering generation 0 with the segment gone,
a second cycle storing nothing twice and the account's next write having
to exceed the collected generation; a backup the account wrote at H
after the Move (generation 1) standing over X's (generation 37), which
X releases; a backup over H's quota left at X with nothing at H; a
stranger and a configured acts-for peer refused all three routes.
Negative control: with the collector disabled, the first two fail.

One thing the implementation does that the text leaves implicit: a
manifest written at the home *while* the blobs were coming (the
`import_backup` false case) leaves the collected blobs attached to the
account until its next manifest omits them, which SIP-48's write rule
then releases. They count against the quota until then.

**Wakes** (§Collecting wakes, §Waking, sqex 0.100.0). `sqex-proto::peer::{PullWakes, Wakes, WakeRow,
MAX_WAKE_ROWS}`; `sqexd`: `/peer/wakes` gated by `Registry::home_of` as
the mailbox routes are, `Registry::{wakes_of, import_wake,
wakes_pending, mark_wakes_collected}`, the `wake` table gains an
`account` column and `wakeable` consults registrations held for the
account beside the device table, `Server::wake_endpoint_ok` applies
the home's own policy, and the SIP-59 home task's `collect_wakes` runs
beside the mail's and the backup's. Tests (`wakes_home_flow`): a phone's
registration at X wakes it from H after the Move for a room posted at
H, and still from X for one posted at X, with X answering the
registration afterwards -- with the collector disabled the wake from H
never comes, which is the negative control; a registration the device
made at H before the Move was presented there stands over the copied
one; an `http://` loopback endpoint X admitted is not held by an H that
does not.

What the text calls a later document's: the reference home does not
run the waker for entries it pulls from an origin, so a post at the
former home wakes through the former home's own registration and not
through the copy. That is why the copy is a copy.

Run live on 2026-09-18: a throwaway account homed at trunk.exchange with
two items queued moved to a laptop exchange listed nowhere (SIP-35
§Open peering),
naming trunk.exchange as an origin; the laptop collected both within a
second of the Move (`collected an account's mail from its former home`),
listed them with the sender and the time trunk.exchange observed, and
opened them; trunk.exchange's queue was empty and told the sender
`collected`.

One finding beside the document, older than it. Mail is addressed to a
key and collected by whoever connects as it (SIP-5). An account that
handed over (SIP-62) is a key nobody connects as -- it is a seed sealed
in a device's store -- so mail sent to the account after a handover
waits for nobody, at the former home and at the new one alike; this
document moves such a queue faithfully to where nobody can read it
either. A device of the account should be able to collect its account's
mail, by SIP-22's registry; that is a document of its own.

Run live on 2026-09-20, both exchanges on 0.100.0. A throwaway account
homed at trunk.exchange registered a wake endpoint there (`sqex wake
register`, an `https://` address nobody serves, since trunk.exchange
does not admit loopback), then moved to a laptop exchange naming
trunk.exchange. Eight seconds later the laptop logged `collected an
account's wake registrations from its former home (SIP-84) stored=1` (the
log line still names the number the section was written under),
and its registry held the row -- device, account, the endpoint as
given, `expires` a day out, `woken` 0.

One finding beside the document, in the client. `move` registered the
device at the new home *before* presenting the Move there, and a home
the account is moving *back* to is its former home until the Move is
presented -- which, since SIP-81 (2026-09-20), refuses a device's
registration with `moved`. The Move now goes first. And the new home
carries the Move to the exchange being left the moment it lands, so
the client's own telling there can find the same Move already recorded
and refused as stale; the client reads that as the telling done. Both
in 0.100.1.
