## Abstract

A service publishes its endpoints to an exchange over its authenticated
connection, without signing anything. Consumers ask the exchange where a public
key can be reached. The exchange asserts the answer; the consumer verifies the
identity itself when it connects.

## Motivation

Resolving a public key to a network address is the oldest problem in this
ecosystem, and sqns solved it with signed records that verify without trusting
the server that supplied them. That property is real and this SIP gives it up
deliberately. What it buys is stated plainly below, as is what it costs.

The immediate motivation is consolidation. An exchange already authenticates
every caller and holds their verified transport key (SIP-2); resolution is a
few fields away from data it holds. Running one service instead of two is worth
something on its own.

The more interesting motivation is that **an exchange can answer a question sqns
structurally cannot**: not just where a service claims to be, but that it was
there recently. A signed record with an hour's TTL says nothing about whether
anyone is listening. An exchange that saw the service thirty seconds ago
(SIP-4) can say so.

## The trust boundary, and why it is narrower than it looks

Consumers must trust the exchange's answer. That is the cost. It is worth being
precise about what a dishonest exchange can and cannot do.

**It cannot impersonate.** A consumer resolving key `K` connects to the returned
address and sQUIC pins `K` in the handshake: the server must hold `K`'s private
half or the connection fails. An exchange that returns an attacker's address
achieves nothing, because the attacker cannot complete the handshake. The
end-to-end key pinning is the backstop, and it does not depend on the exchange
at all.

**It can deny.** Wrong or absent endpoints mean the consumer cannot connect.
This is a denial of service, not a compromise.

**It learns who asks about whom.** Every query, attributable to a
transport-authenticated caller.

So the exchange is trusted for *availability and privacy*, not for
*authenticity*. That is a much weaker requirement than "trust the server", and
it is why this design is defensible where an unauthenticated redirect would not
be.

## Specification

### Publishing

A caller whose verified transport key (SIP-2) the exchange can bind to a known identity may publish endpoints
for it. No signature is sent; the handshake has already established which key
is speaking, and a caller may only publish for that identity.

```
| type: u8 = 0x10 | ttl_secs: u32 | count: u8 | endpoint × count |
```

Each endpoint, reusing sqns's proven shape:

```
| kind: u8 | host | port: u16 | priority: u16 | weight: u16 |
```

`kind` is 4 for an IPv4 address (4 bytes), 6 for IPv6 (16 bytes), or 0 for a
DNS name (u8 length, then UTF-8). Lower `priority` is tried first; `weight`
distributes load among equal priorities.

Publishing replaces the identity's entire endpoint set. There is no partial
update: a set is what a service is offering right now, and merging two partial
views is how stale addresses survive.

### Expiry, and its coupling to liveness

Endpoints expire `ttl_secs` after publication and MUST be dropped when they do.

An exchange SHOULD treat a beat (SIP-4) as refreshing the publishing identity's
endpoints, so a service that is proving it is alive does not separately have to
prove its address is current. Implementations MAY reject a `ttl_secs` far larger
than the identity's declared beacon interval, since endpoints outliving the
evidence for them is the failure this design exists to avoid.

### Resolving

```
| type: u8 = 0x11 | ed25519_pubkey: [32] |
```

The reply carries the endpoints and, critically, their provenance:

```
| found: u8 | count: u8 | endpoint × count |
| published_at: u64 | expires_at: u64 | last_seen: u64 | now: u64 |
```

`last_seen` is the beacon observation from SIP-4, or zero if the identity does
not beat. `now` is the exchange's clock, for the reason given in SIP-4:
staleness must be computed against the observer's clock, not the consumer's.

A consumer MUST pin the key it asked for when connecting to a returned endpoint.
It MUST NOT accept a different identity at that address, and MUST NOT treat a
successful connection to one endpoint as validating the others.

### Rotation is not solved here

An identity may publish a pointer to a successor:

```
| type: u8 = 0x12 | successor: [32] | reason: utf8 |
```

**This is materially weaker than sqns's supersession and the difference must not
be glossed.** In sqns, retirement is signed by the *identity* key, held offline,
so a stolen service key cannot retire itself or forward callers anywhere. Here
the pointer is authenticated by the connection, which means whoever holds the
key can set it — and after a theft, that is the attacker.

An exchange therefore cannot express "this key was stolen, use this one
instead", which is the case rotation exists for. It can only express "I am
moving", and only while the mover is still in control.

Deployments that need authenticated retirement should keep an sqns record, or
wait for a future SIP that lets an exchange consult one.

## Rationale

**Why unsigned.** Signing would have been easy — a `Live` record is signed by
the service key, which is online — so this is not the beacon's argument. It is a
choice to keep one mechanism rather than two: the exchange already
authenticates, and a second scheme layered on top of it would need its own
format, its own verification path and its own bugs. The end-to-end pinning
described above means the loss is availability, not authenticity.

**Why the whole set is replaced.** Partial updates require reconciliation, and
reconciliation between an unsigned publisher and a trusting store is where stale
addresses live forever.

**Why provenance is in the reply.** A consumer that cannot see when an answer
was published, when it expires and when the service was last seen cannot judge
it. Returning a bare list of addresses would make the exchange's confidence
invisible and its errors indistinguishable from silence.

**What this gives up, gathered in one place.** Replication between exchanges,
because an unsigned record cannot be safely relayed. Upstream recursion, for the
same reason. Offline verification and long-lived caching. Authenticated
retirement. Each of those is a property sqns has and this does not.

## Compatibility

New protocol. Requires SIP-2. Does not interoperate with sqns and does
not read its records; nothing here retires sqns, which continues unaffected
until something replaces it.

Supersedes the recommendation in SIP-26, which proposed extending the sqns record
with capability data. With resolution in the exchange, capability belongs
alongside it.

## Security considerations

**Consumers trust the exchange for availability, not authenticity.** The
boundary above is the central claim of this SIP and implementations should
document it for users rather than leaving it here.

**A dishonest or compromised exchange is a denial-of-service position**, and a
silent one: a consumer cannot distinguish a withheld endpoint from a service
that never published. Consumers with strong availability requirements should
publish to and resolve through more than one exchange.

**Rotation after key compromise is not protected**, per the section above. This
is the most significant regression from sqns and the most likely to be
overlooked, because it fails only in the case it exists for.

**Query privacy.** The exchange observes every resolution, attributed to an
authenticated identity, and therefore learns the communication graph before any
communication happens. sqns leaked the same information but could be replicated
and self-hosted; an unsigned store cannot be replicated, so this is harder to
mitigate.

**Endpoint publication is self-asserted.** An identity may publish any address,
including one it does not control. Since a consumer pins the key on connecting,
pointing at a third party causes failed handshakes at that address rather than
misdirection — but an identity with many consumers could direct traffic at a
victim. Exchanges SHOULD bound the number of endpoints and MAY refuse addresses
that do not match the publisher's observed source address.
